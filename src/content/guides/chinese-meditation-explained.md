**Direct answer:** "Chinese meditation" is not one thing. The English word *meditation* maps onto at least five distinct Chinese practices with different goals, methods, and evidence bases: zuowang (sitting in oblivion), xinzhai (fasting of the mind), shouyi (guarding the One), jingzuo (quiet sitting), and Chan sitting (zuochan). They share a root vocabulary — emptiness, qi, forgetting, the One — but differ in aim: union with the Dao, receptivity, longevity, moral seriousness, or awakening. This guide is the entry point for navigating the distinction.

## The one-sentence map

| Practice | Period | Source | Stated aim |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zuowang 坐忘 | 4th c. BCE described; 8th c. systematized | Zhuangzi 6; Zuowang lun | merge with the Great Thoroughfare |
| Xinzhai 心斋 | 4th c. BCE described | Zhuangzi 4 | empty the mind so the Dao gathers |
| Shouyi 守一 | Han–Six Dynasties | Daodejing 10; Taiping Jing | keep jing–qi–shen united; longevity |
| Jingzuo 静坐 | Song–Ming | Zhuzi Yulei; Er Cheng waishu | collect the mind for study and conduct |
| Zuochan 坐禅 | 6th c. onward in China | Chan lineage texts | see one's nature; awakening |

Three caveats apply immediately:

1. The first two are *descriptions in philosophical parables*, not manuals; reading technique into them is an interpretive move.
2. The practices cross-pollinate constantly (Tang Daoism borrows Buddhist stages; Neo-Confucians borrow Chan sitting and redefine it). Clean boundaries are editorial convenience, not history.
3. "Meditation" as a modern category already biases the search — the Chinese terms do not mean "mental exercise" but are embedded in ways of life, cosmology, and, in several cases, religious goals.

## What the traditions share

- **The subtraction logic** (Daodejing 48): progress is removal, not addition.
- **Emptiness (xu) as the working state** (Zhuangzi 4): the Dao "gathers" where the mind stops imposing.
- **Qi as the middle register** between body and mind: subtle breath/vitality, the level at which listening becomes non-conceptual.
- **Forgetting (wang) as a technical term**: the deliberate dropping of acquired content — virtues, rituals, body-schema, knowledge.
- **A culture of caution**: the earliest method texts (Taiping Jing) instruct the practitioner to stop when restless and not to force.

## How to read claims about these practices

This knowledge base applies a fixed evidence discipline. Every claim on this site is classed:

- **PRIMARY SOURCE** — what the classical text says.
- **HISTORICAL EVIDENCE** — what the record shows happened (who taught whom, what institutions existed).
- **SCHOLARLY INTERPRETATION** — what modern scholars argue the texts mean.
- **TRADITIONAL / LINEAGE INTERPRETATION** — what practitioners' traditions claim (not history).
- **MODERN RESEARCH** — peer-reviewed empirical findings, with limitations stated.
- **PRACTITIONER EXPERIENCE** — anonymized reports, never treated as science.
- **EDITORIAL SYNTHESIS** — this site's own analysis, flagged as such.
- **UNCERTAIN** — what cannot be established.

Rule of thumb: **a claim about what a text says needs a citation to the text; a claim about what a practice does to a person needs a research citation or must be labeled traditional/uncertain.** No page on this site converts a tradition's self-description into a fact.

## Common questions, answered briefly

**Is Chinese meditation the same as mindfulness?**
No. Mindfulness meditation (as researched in the modern literature) is an attentional training with present-moment emphasis; zuowang and xinzhai describe emptying and merging; jingzuo serves study and conduct; shouyi guards inner unity. Overlaps exist; identity does not. See [comparisons](/comparisons/) as the section grows.

**Which is the oldest documented practice?**
The *descriptions* of xinzhai and zuowang (4th c. BCE) are oldest; the earliest *protocol* (room, schedule, exit conditions) is the shouyi method in the *Taiping Jing* (Han). Neither claim should be read as "therefore effective by modern standards."

**Are these practices safe?**
The traditions themselves caution against forcing and prescribe exit conditions. For modern practitioners, the honest answer: sustained meditation can surface difficult experiences, and anyone with a mental-health history should consult a clinician before beginning. This site documents traditions; it does not prescribe.

**What should I read first?**
The two root texts with commentary: *Zhuangzi* chs. 4 and 6, and *Daodejing* chs. 10, 16, 48 — then the [zuowang](/concepts/zuowang/) and [jingzuo](/concepts/jingzuo/) pages, which carry the historical development forward.

## Editorial synthesis

The productive way to approach Chinese contemplative traditions is as a *documented history of ideas about attention and self-loss*, not as a menu of techniques. The continuity is real — a vocabulary of emptiness and forgetting running from the 4th century BCE to the Ming — but so is the diversity: a Daoist sage, a Tang recluse, a Song official, and a Chan monk would not have said they were doing "the same meditation." This knowledge base exists to make both facts available: the shared vocabulary and the real differences.
