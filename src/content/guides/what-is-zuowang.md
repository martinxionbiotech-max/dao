**Direct answer:** Zuowang (坐忘) means "sitting and forgetting" — and the forgetting is the point. It is the classical Daoist practice of sitting in stillness while *forgetting*, in stages, everything that separates a person from the Dao: the body's claim, the senses' claim, the self's claim. What remains, the Zhuangzi says, is merger with "the Great Thoroughfare" — the Dao itself.

## Where the term comes from

The word appears in **Zhuangzi ch. 6**, in a dialogue where Confucius's student Yan Hui reports his progress: first he forgot benevolence and righteousness, then rites and music, and finally he sat and forgot — "I drop away the limbs and body, expel perception, leave form behind, dismiss knowing, and merge with the Great Thoroughfare." Confucius's verdict in the story: "I would like to become your follower."

Eight centuries later, the Tang Daoist **Sima Chengzhen** wrote a manual called the *Zuowang Lun* (Treatise on Sitting and Forgetting) that systematized the practice into stages — the text the tradition has used ever since.

## What it looks like in practice

- **Sit in stillness** — posture is ordinary upright sitting; the [jingzuo](/concepts/jingzuo/) page holds the posture details the tradition shares across schools.
- **Let go in stages** — the practice is progressive *forgetting*: what presents itself, one lets settle, until the boundary between self and world thins.
- **Do not force** — every manual's warning. Forgetting cannot be gripped; the grip is what forgetting forgets.

## The honest limits

- The Zhuangzi passage is a **parable**, not a manual — the stages it names are descriptions, not step-by-step instructions.
- The tradition's full system is on the **[zuowang concept page](/concepts/zuowang/)** — this introduction is the doorway, not the house.
