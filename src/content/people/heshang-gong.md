## The name, and the two figures behind it

河上公 is not a personal name but a description: "the Elder by the River." The tradition knows two river-elders, and the site keeps them separate:

- **The Huang-Lao master.** The *Shiji*'s Yue Yi chapter records a transmission line beginning from 河上丈人 ("the Old Man by the River"): Heshang Zhangren taught Anqi Sheng, and so on through six generations to Gai Gong, who taught Cao Can, the Han chancellor — the documented route by which Huang-Lao learning entered the early Han court.
- **The immortal of the Shenxian Zhuan.** Ge Hong's [Shenxian Zhuan](/people/ge-hong/) tells the story this page records: under Emperor Wen, an old man who lived in a grass hut by the river and read the Laozi; the emperor, unable to resolve difficulties in the text, sent envoys; the old man answered "the Way is honored and its virtue prized — it cannot be asked from afar"; the emperor came in person, quoting "within the four borders I am king," and the old man **rose slowly into the air a hundred feet up** and answered: "I reach neither to heaven above, nor touch the people between, nor rest on the earth below — whose subject am I?" The emperor bowed, received the commentary in two scrolls, and the old man vanished; the court built a terrace on the western hill to watch for him. The Shenxian Zhuan itself opens: 莫知其姓名 — "no one knows his name."

## What the commentary did — and why the site depends on it

- **The earliest surviving full Laozi commentary.** The *Heshang Gong Zhangju* is, per the standard scholarly account, the earliest extant commentary to circulate widely, and the source of the book's **eighty-one chapter titles** (体道, 养身 and so on) — the chapter-naming system every later edition inherits.
- **The book as body-manual.** Its two registers — governing the state and **governing the body** (治身) — made the Laozi a practice text: cherishing qi, nourishing the spirit, guarding the five viscera's spirits, removing desire. This is the reading that carried the Daodejing from philosophy into the [practice corpus](/practices/shouyi/) the site documents.
- **The site's textual anchor.** Two of this site's recorded variants come from the Heshang Gong recension: the [ch. 25 王亦大/人亦大](/translations/daodejing-25/) variant (the king reading documented from the Heshang Gong line) and the [ch. 5 多言/多闻](/translations/daodejing-05/) pair. The [people page for Laozi](/people/laozi/) and the [Daodejing text page](/texts/daodejing/) both route through this commentary — the site treats "the received text" as, in large part, *this* recension.
- **The historical layer, stated plainly.** The attribution to Emperor Wen's court is the legend; scholarship conventionally dates the commentary's composition to the Eastern Han (with some arguments for the Han–Wei transition), under a pseudonym — the same pattern the site records for the [Liezi](/texts/liezi/) and the [Huangting Jing](/texts/huangting-jing/): a text carrying an honored pseudonym, whose value is in the reading it institutionalized. The Tang dynasty made the commentary an examination text, and the Dunhuang Laozi manuscripts are mostly of this recension — the legend did its work.

## Why he belongs in this knowledge base

Because the site's whole [translation discipline](/blog/translation-policy/) stands on a recension that no one can date to a person — and the page makes that the point: the tradition's oldest *reading* of the Laozi is anonymous in exactly the way the Laozi's first line predicts. The [verify-your-sources tool](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/) uses this page as its case study: a legendary name, a real commentary, and the difference kept visible at every step.

**Uncertainty preserved:** no dates are given for the person (the legend places him under Emperor Wen; the commentary is dated by scholarship, not by the legend); the Shiji's Heshang Zhangren and the Shenxian Zhuan's Heshang Gong are recorded as possibly-related, possibly-distinct figures, per the sources; the ascension and the terrace are the Shenxian Zhuan's register.
