## Who she was

**The only woman among the Seven Perfected — and, by marriage, half of the archive's most famous pair of fellow disciples.** Sun Bu'er (孙不二, 1119–1182) was born Sun Fuchun (孙富春) into the wealthy Ninghai family whose other half appears on this site's [Ma Yu](/people/ma-yu/) page: she was Ma Yu's wife, mother of their three sons, and — when Wang Chongyang arrived in 1167 and was lodged in their house — she became his disciple together with her husband. The marriage that had produced the three sons became a pair of fellow students; the school's memory of the conversion is inseparable from hers. Her dharma name 不二 (Bu'er — "not two") and her title 清静散人 (Qingjing Sanren, "the Clear-Stillness Wanderer") gave their name to the lineage she founded: the 清静派, the Qingjing lineage, the quiet wing of Quanzhen — and her popular name 孙仙姑 (Immortal Lady Sun) marks the rare case of a woman entering the Seven Perfected as a founder, not a footnote.

## What the sources record — and disagree on

- **The dates: two versions, both registered.** The majority tradition and the Chinese encyclopedia entries give **1119–1182**; the Wikipedia entry gives precise dates of **February 16, 1119 – January 24, 1183**. Per this site's rule for disputed facts (record both, judge neither), both appear here. The difference is one year; the sources agree on the century, the place, and the life.
- **The birth legend: seven cranes dance in the courtyard.** The lineage tradition (registered here as traditional, not historical) says that at her birth seven cranes danced in the courtyard — the portent that later readings connected to her place among seven disciples. The site files this with the same flag as the other birth omens on the [Wang Chongyang](/people/wang-chongyang/) and [Ma Yu](/people/ma-yu/) pages: lineage interpretation, not evidence.
- **The conversion: the household was the hinge.** In 1167 Wang Chongyang came to Ninghai and was lodged in the Ma household — the house that became the 全真庵, the school's first permanent address (the building that gave Quanzhen its name; see the [Ma Yu](/people/ma-yu/) page). Sun Bu'er's conversion happened *inside the home*, and the sources preserve the specific difficulty: she was, as the Epoch Times account puts it, initially held back by family ties — the same entanglement the Ma Yu page records for her husband. The tradition credits Wang Chongyang's teaching of gradual release (the "ten separations" from the pear) with working on both spouses; the site notes the pattern without endorsing its mechanisms: the archive's founding generation is repeatedly described as *extracted* from family wealth rather than lifted out of it.
- **The retreat: the cave at Luoyang.** After ordination she withdrew to the Fengxian'gu (凤仙姑洞) area near Luoyang — the site where, by the received account, she died in sitting posture (趺坐而化). The Wikipedia phrasing (于洛阳风仙姑洞趺坐) is the soberest version; the tradition adds the detail that her death in sitting was the completion of the practice rather than an accident of biography. Registered as traditional.
- **The lineage.** The Qingjing lineage she founded is remembered as the school's women's line and its stillness line — the two descriptions are not competing but consecutive: the lineage was founded *by* its only woman founder, and its name is the practice. This site's [jingzuo](/glossary/jingzuo/) page holds the vocabulary that made the name possible: 清静, clear stillness, was already the common coin of the meditation tradition her school took as its badge.

## Why she is on this site

Because the Quanzhen founding generation is not complete without its woman founder — and because her story is the archive's clearest instance of the pattern this site's [teacher question](/experiences/questions/do-i-need-a-teacher/) tracks: **the couple who became fellow disciples.** The Ma Yu page ends with the image of the man who paid for the school refusing its money; the Sun Bu'er page supplies the other half of the same story — the wife who entered the same school from inside the same house, and whose lineage carried the practice's stillest name. The two pages are best read as one record: one marriage, one teacher, one school, two lineages — and the school's own memory registering that a woman's cave at Luoyang is one of the places where its founding happened.
