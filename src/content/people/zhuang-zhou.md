**Direct answer:** Zhuang Zhou (庄周, "Master Zhuang") is the traditional author of the *Zhuangzi*'s Inner Chapters and the namesake of the whole anthology. Unlike Laozi, his existence as a historical person is broadly accepted: the *Shiji* places him in the 4th century BCE as a minor official from Meng who refused high office. But nearly everything colorful about him comes from the *Zhuangzi*'s own parables — stories told *about* him by a school that used him as a character. The person is historical; the anecdotes are literature.

## What the Shiji says (c. 100 BCE)

- "Zhuangzi was a man of Meng, named Zhou. Zhou once served as an official of the lacquer garden (漆园吏) in Meng, and was contemporary with King Hui of Liang and King Xuan of Qi."
- "His learning was vast, but its essentials went back to the words of Laozi. He wrote more than one hundred thousand characters, most of them parables (大抵率寓言也)."
- The refusal of office: King Wei of Chu, hearing of Zhuang Zhou's worth, sent messengers with rich gifts to make him prime minister. Zhuang Zhou laughed and said: "A thousand in gold is great profit; a minister's rank is an honored position. But have you not seen the sacrificial ox? Fed for years, robed in embroidered cloth, led into the great temple — at that moment, even if it wanted to be a lone piglet, could it? Go away. Do not pollute me. I would rather play about in a muddy ditch for my own pleasure than be bridled by the ruler of a state. To the end of my life I will not take office, and so please my own will" (終身不仕，以快吾志焉).
- Sima Qian notes the *Zhuangzi*'s attacks on Confucian and Mohist scholars and its unmatchable style, "so that from kings and dukes down, none could put him to use."

## The famous parables (literature, not biography)

- **The butterfly dream** (ch. 2): "Once Zhuang Zhou dreamed he was a butterfly... between Zhou and the butterfly there must be some distinction — this is called the transformation of things" (此之謂物化). A philosophical dream-parable about the instability of identity, not a biographical event.
- **The death of his wife** (ch. 18): he sang and drummed on a pot, answering the mourner that life is a transformation of qi. Again a teaching story in which "Zhuangzi" is the exemplar-character.
- **The useless tree, the happy fish, the skull** — the same pattern: the named author is deployed as a character inside his own tradition.

## Person vs. book

The *Zhuangzi* is an anthology (see the [text page](/texts/zhuangzi/)): the first seven chapters are traditionally attributed to Zhuang Zhou, the rest to his school and successors. Modern scholarship accepts a historical Zhuang Zhou as the probable core author of the Inner Chapters — while emphasizing that even the Inner Chapters are composite. Claims in popular literature that "Zhuangzi said X" usually mean "the *Zhuangzi* says X."

## Why the distinction matters for contemplation

The two key practice passages — xinzhai (ch. 4) and zuowang (ch. 6) — are fictional dialogues placed in the mouths of Yan Hui and Confucius, not autobiographical reports by Zhuang Zhou. Nothing in the text claims he practiced or attained what the dialogues describe. The tradition's own framing is parabolic: the descriptions are authoritative *as descriptions*, not as memoirs. This is the opposite of, say, Chan encounter literature, which does claim to record real practitioners.

## Editorial synthesis

Zhuang Zhou is the rare case where the historical person matters less than the literary device: the *Zhuangzi* uses its own author as the model of the free, useless, unburdened person — and that self-portrait, precisely because it is artful, is what survived. For a knowledge base, the discipline is simple: cite the Shiji for the person, cite the book for the ideas, and never present the parables as biography.

**Uncertainty is preserved:** the exact extent of Zhuang Zhou's own composition within the Inner Chapters is debated; the Shiji's biographical details (lacquer-garden office, the Chu offer) are themselves unverifiable traditions recorded three centuries later.
