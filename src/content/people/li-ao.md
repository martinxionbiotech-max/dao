## The man, and the poem he should not have written

- **The anti-Buddhist who climbed the mountain.** The verified biography is blunt: Li Ao, of Didao in Longxi, studied classical prose under Han Yu, and was **lifelong Confucian, hostile to Buddhism and Daoism** (崇儒排斥佛老). And the same man, as prefect of Langzhou, repeatedly invited the Chan master Weiyan of Yaoshan to come to him; the monk declined every time; so the prefect went up the mountain himself — and came down having written the two most famous Chan poems by a Confucian hand. The site's [boundary pages](/experiences/exp-013-buddhist-daoist-boundary/) hold this irony as a standing category: the tradition's recorded boundary-crossers — [Fu Yi](/people/fu-yi/) submitted the petition to abolish the Buddha-dharma and gave the [Daodejing](/texts/daodejing/) its best ancient recension; Li Ao opposed Buddhism and wrote its poetry. Both pages carry the same instruction: the boundary is crossed in both directions, by the very people policing it.
- **The poem, in full.** The first of the two poems, with the variant recorded (青霄 一作青天 — "blue empyrean," one text has "blue sky"):

  练得身形似鹤形，千株松下两函经。我来问道无馀说，云在青霄水在瓶。

  "His form, through practice, is like a crane's form; under a thousand pines, two boxes of sutras. I came to ask the Way — no words left over: **the cloud is in the blue sky, the water is in the bottle.**"

  And the second: 选得幽居惬野情，终年无送亦无迎。有时直上孤峰顶，月下披云啸一声 — "He chose a hidden dwelling to suit a wild heart; all year, no send-offs and no welcomes. Sometimes he climbs straight to the lone summit and, under the moon, parts the clouds and gives one long cry." The [Jingde chuandeng lu](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/) background, recorded on the page the site verified: the invitations declined, the prefect's own visit, the two exchanges, the poems written on the spot.
- **云在青天水在瓶 — the sentence as a doctrine.** The line is the tradition's most compact statement of the [ziran](/concepts/ziran/) grammar in Chan dress: the cloud is not *trying* to be in the sky; the water is not *choosing* the bottle; each is exactly where its own so-of-itself puts it. The site cross-links the line to the [qingjing-jing](/texts/qingjing-jing/) and the [DDJ 45 translation](/translations/daodejing-45/) — the clear-and-still as the world's standard — because the poem is, in practice terms, the same claim as 清静为天下正: the asked question dissolves into the state of things, and the state of things was the answer before the question arrived.
- **The Fuxing Shu — the Confucian borrowing, documented.** The verified biography lists his main philosophical work, the *Fuxing Shu* ("On Returning to the Nature"), and scholarship holds what the biography's own hostility makes obvious: the text is the Tang Confucian attempt to answer the Buddhist-and-Daoist *practice* tradition — above all its sitting — within Confucian terms, and the later Confucian quiet-sitting tradition (the site's [Zhu Xi timeline page](/timeline/zhuzi-yulei-jingzuo/)) looks back to it as its ancestor. The site registers the claim at its scholarly confidence level, and keeps the [jingzuo concept](/concepts/jingzuo/) as the family home: 静坐 is the one practice word the three teachings shared, and Li Ao is the man who carried it across the fence while insisting the fence was high.

## Why he belongs in this knowledge base

Because he is the site's proof that the boundary it polices is real and porous at once: the texts cross it, the practices cross it, and the people who policed it crossed it last. The [people pages](/people/fu-yi/) collect the refusals; Li Ao is the refusal's mirror — the man who went up the mountain anyway.

**Uncertainty preserved:** dates (772–841) follow the standard biography; the Jingde chuandeng lu account is recorded as the tradition's own hagiography-adjacent record (a lamp-history, not a court chronicle); the Fuxing Shu's role as ancestor of Confucian quiet-sitting is registered as scholarly interpretation, not primary-source fact; the 青霄/青天 variant recorded, not adjudicated.
