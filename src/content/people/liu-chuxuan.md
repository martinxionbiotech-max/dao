## Who he was

**Liu Chuxuan (1147–1203)** was the Quanzhen disciple recruited by a wall. In the spring of 1169, in Wuguan village in Laizhou, a verse appeared in brushwork on a neighbor's wall, in a spot no one else could reach: *"Wuguan, the land for nurturing the nature, the true immortals' place — here there must be one who lives without dying"* (武官养性真仙地，须有长生不死人). Liu, struck by it, went to meet the master who had just arrived in the region. Wang Chongyang looked at him and smiled: "Do you know the ink-traces on that wall?" Liu understood at once and asked to become his disciple; the master took the wall's phrase and gave him his names — hao *Changsheng* (长生, "long-living") from the verse, hui *Chuxuan*, courtesy *Tongmiao*. The site registers the age discrepancy between the two records of the scene (21 or 23) without adjudicating — the scene itself both sources tell.

## The years that made him

- **The filial first stage.** Liu's father died when he was young; the sources' first fact about him is that he served his mother with exceptional care. Like [Tan Chuduan](/people/tan-chuduan/)'s crutches, the biography opens with what the tradition counts as the precondition of practice.
- **The Luoyang years.** After Wang Chongyang's death, Liu kept the three-year grave-watch with Ma, Tan, and Qiu, then — following the master's recorded instruction — went west with Tan Chuduan to Luoyang, living as a wandering beggar, refining the nature amid the flower lanes and wine quarters, the same program as Tan's red-streets discipline.
- **The prison calligraphy.** The tradition's most distinctive Liu story: he built a practice hall in Wuguan, neighbors accused him of a murder, and he did not defend himself. Held in prison for nearly a hundred days, the barely-literate Liu — so the record goes — used the confinement to read and practice writing, and came out with calligraphy "like dragons and serpents in motion." He was released because the real killer confessed. The site registers the story in its traditional layer, with the note that it is the mirror image of the usual hagiography: where others are vindicated by miracles, Liu's vindication is simply that the truth came out on its own — which is itself the story's point about the [empty boat](/stories/xuzhou-empty-boat/) virtue of not contending.
- **The patriarch.** In 1185 Tan Chuduan died and Liu succeeded as the fourth patriarch of Quanzhen. He returned to Wuguan, built the Lingxu Abbey, and taught; the archive says the crowds at his door never slackened.
- **The audience with the emperor.** In the winter of 1197 the Jin emperor Zhangzong summoned him to the capital and asked what the supreme Way is. Liu's recorded answer is the shortest political document in the Quanzhen canon: **"The essential of the Way: few desires and the body is at ease; light taxes and the state is at peace"** (道之要，寡嗜欲则身安，薄赋敛则国泰). The site notes the structural echo with the site's own [non-action](/concepts/wuwei/) pages: the contemplative claim translated, without loss, into a governance claim.
- **Death and titles.** Liu died in 1203 at his Lingxu Abbey. The titles came later, in the usual sequence: Yuan Shizu's 长生辅化明德真人 (1269), Yuan Wuzong's longer 长生辅化宗玄明德真君.

## The lineage he left

Liu founded the **Suishan (随山) lineage**, one of the seven Quanzhen branches, whose tradition places its ancestral seat at the Taiqing Palace on Mount Lao. His recorded disciples include the ascetic Yu Daoxian and Song Defang (Song Piyun), who went on to supervise the compilation of the Daoist canon. His works — the *Xianle ji*, the *Wuyi qingjing changsheng zhenren zhizhen yulu* (the *Zhen yu lu*), a *Daodejing* commentary, a *Yinfu* exposition, and a *Huangting* commentary — complete the Seven Perfected's record of the practice as text.

## Boundary notes, stated plainly

The wall-verse and the prison story are traditional records, registered in the tradition's layer, not independent historical confirmation. The succession dates differ between the site's two main sources (1185 per the Daoist Culture Centre Database's 大定二十五年 statement; 1189 per zh.wikipedia's table) — the site registers both, as it does for the age at the 1169 conversion. What no source disputes: Liu Chuxuan was the fourth patriarch, the founder of the Suishan lineage, and the man whose answer to an emperor was one sentence about few desires and light taxes.
