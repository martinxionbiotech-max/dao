## The man, and the irony the sources preserve

- **The astronomer against Buddhism.** Fu Yi, of Ye in Xiangzhou, served the Sui prince Yang Liang in the Kaihuang era, and at the Tang founder's accession was summoned as taishi cheng and promoted to taishi ling — the imperial astronomer. In 621 he submitted the [Petition to Abolish the Buddha-dharma](/sources/), the opening move of the Tang court's great debate — and the site registers the historical fact plainly: the man who gave the [Daodejing](/texts/daodejing/) its most respected ancient recension is the same man who tried, on the same court's floor, to have the rival religion abolished. The [Daoist-Buddhist boundary pages](/experiences/exp-013-buddhist-daoist-boundary/) hold the site's standing line on that boundary; Fu Yi is its earliest *documented* instance in the political record.
- **The fourth reader.** The site's [commentary pages](/people/heshang-gong/) now hold the tradition's four foundational readers of the book, and the quartet is complete: [Heshang Gong](/people/heshang-gong/) (the anonymous practice reading), [Yan Zun](/people/yan-zun/) (the market-counter philosophical reading), [Wang Bi](/people/wang-bi/) (the court philosophical reading, at twenty-three), and Fu Yi — the *textual* reading. His contribution is not interpretation but **collation**: the instinct to compare versions against an excavated manuscript and to prefer the older form. The site marks him as the quartet's philologist — the one who asked, before asking what the book means, what the book *says*.
- **The ancient-text recension.** The verified record: in the early Tang, Fu Yi collated the *Daodejing guben pian* from the "Xiang Yu's concubine" manuscript said to have been found in a Northern Qi tomb, combined with several transmitted editions; scholarship treats the result as a good text — the [Chinese Writers' essay](/sources/) says it preserves many ancient phrases, words, and characters — while also noting its source editions' errors and the alterations it suffered in transmission. The site registers both halves, per its [edition discipline](/translations/daodejing-22/): the Fu Yi recension is the tradition's first documented attempt at a critical edition, and it is *not* a pure ancient text — it is a scholar's construction from mixed materials, valued precisely as that.

## Why he belongs in this knowledge base

Because he is the site's [verification tool](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/) in biographical form: the tradition has always known that *which text* you read decides *what* you read — and the man who institutionalized that knowledge is also the man whose career shows the tradition's texts and its politics were never far apart. The [people pages](/people/chen-tuan/) teach the refusals (no alchemy for the emperor, no office for the hermit); Fu Yi teaches the opposite career — the hermit's counterpart who took the court post and used it, with mixed results, for the tradition's library.

**Uncertainty preserved:** dates (555–639) follow the standard sources; the "Xiang Yu's concubine" manuscript is recorded as the traditional attribution (a tomb-find said to date from the Northern Qi) without independent verification; the recension's textual quality follows the scholarship the site verified, including its caveats; the label "atheist" (wushenlun zhe) is the Baike's modern categorization, recorded as such.
