## The record and the legend, kept apart

- **What the official history actually says.** The Ming Shi's Fangji zhuan (the official biographies of technicians and adepts — itself the historians' own category for figures at the edge of the record) gives a portrait the site keeps in full: the unkempt giant (tortoise back, crane frame, halberd beard), the one robe in all seasons, the irregular eating, the once-read memory, the wandering without fixed dwelling, the prophecy that Wudang would flourish, the rebuilding of its ruined halls with his disciples, the death-and-revival at the Jintai guan, the meeting with a prince, the emperors' failed searches, and the posthumous titles (Tongwei Xianhua Zhenren under Yingzong, further titles under Xianzong and Shizong). All of this is *recorded* — and every line of it is the government's own documentation of a man it could not find. The site's framing: Zhang Sanfeng is the best-documented *absence* in the tradition — three Ming emperors searching, titles conferred on a man never produced.
- **What the record does not say.** No reliable birth or death dates (the site, per its [dating rule](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/), writes none); birthplace disputed (Yizhou per the Ming Shi; Shaowu, Fujian per local genealogies; Baoji per another local tradition); and — the largest absence — no early source connects him to any documented boxing system. The Taijiquan attribution rests on Wudang's own tradition (the dream-received art, the bird-and-snake fight), first written down centuries later in the Wang Zhengnan epitaph-era materials; modern scholarship dates the documented boxing lineages later and treats the Zhang Sanfeng attribution as the tradition's own construction. The site registers this as the *posthumous founder* pattern it has already documented in [Zhang Boduan](/people/zhang-boduan/) — the patriarch who did not found what he founds — here in its extreme form: the man is named, the lineage is real, and the documents connecting them are the tradition's, not the record's.
- **The inner-alchemy line, same treatment.** The tradition presents him as a synthesizer of [xing and ming cultivation](/glossary/jing-qi-shen/) (性命双修 — the charter word this site holds from [Zhang Boduan](/people/zhang-boduan/)), practicing quiet-mind-to-nurture-nature (清静炼心以养性), with the 1314 initiation-under-Fire-Dragon-Zhenren story and the years of cave practice carried by local and Daoist sources. The works under his name (Xuantan quanji, Xuanyao pian, the Wugenshu lyrics) are traditional attributions, some doubted by scholars — registered as such. The site's line: the doctrine is real and continuous (it flows through the [alchemical record](/timeline/daoshu-neiguan-zuowang/) this site documents); the biography is the tradition's own, told by people who needed a first ancestor.
- **Why he belongs here anyway.** Because his *function* in the tradition is itself a fact worth documenting: Zhang Sanfeng is the name under which the tradition joined two of this site's central threads — the [body-first practice](/people/zhang-boduan/) line and the mountain-recluse image this site holds from [Chen Tuan](/people/chen-tuan/) — into the single most famous Daoist figure outside China. The [experience archive](/experiences/) documents contemporary teachers who say body work comes first and display is forbidden; Zhang Sanfeng is the traditional figure in whom those two rules were welded together — the hidden man with the famous name, searched for by emperors who never found him, which is, on the tradition's own terms, exactly the point.
