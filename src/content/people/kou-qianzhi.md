## Who he was, and why he matters to this site

Kou Qianzhi is the figure who moved Daoism **from community religion to state religion** — and he did it in the generation between Ge Hong's southern [Baopuzi](/texts/baopuzi/) project and the southern Shangqing revelations ([Wei Huacun](/people/wei-huacun/)). Born into a northern aristocratic family with a hereditary Celestial Master affiliation, he withdrew to the mountains as a young man (the sources name both Huashan and Songshan for different phases of his mountain years, and the page keeps both), and emerged to become the architect of the **Northern Celestial Master** (新天师道) reform — the first Daoist movement shaped to a barbarian-dynasty court.

For a knowledge base about sitting practice, he matters on two specific points:

- **He is the reformer of the tradition's *institutions*, not its practice methods.** His reforms — abolishing hereditary libationer offices, forbidding the tax in grain and coin, purging the sexual "union of breaths" practices attributed to the three Zhangs, replacing them with fasts and a Confucian-flavored ethics — are about what the community *was*, not about how one sits. The site keeps him for the map: he is the reason the word "Celestial Master" means something different in the north after 415.
- **He is the cautionary counterweight to the site's practice focus.** His story runs on revelation claims (the 415 descent of the deified Laozi with the *Hymned Commandments*; the later commission recorded in the *Weishu*), on court alliance (with the minister Cui Hao), and on imperial registers (the Northern Wei emperor taking Daoist registers at the altar in 442, which became the rite for every later emperor of the dynasty). None of it is sitting practice — and all of it is the historical frame inside which the practice lineages later sat.

## The three layers, kept separate

- **Historical layer:** the *Weishu* and *Beishi* record his life (365–448), his family, his mountain years, his court career under Emperor Taiwu, his opposition to the 446 Buddhist suppression, and his death at eighty-three. This is the person.
- **Reform layer:** the content of the reform — documented in the tradition's own new scripture and in the official history: the end of hereditary offices, the end of the grain tax, the ban on the Zhangs' sexual practices, fasts and ethics in their place, and the "assist the Northern Wei, restore the interrupted lineage" program. This is the historical movement.
- **Revelation layer:** the 415 descent of the deified Laozi, the later commission, the "Great Peace True Lord" title conferred on the emperor, the imperial registers of 442. This is the tradition's own register, reported as such — and the [timeline page](/timeline/kou-qianzhi-reform/) anchors each date.

## Why he belongs in this knowledge base

Three structural reasons. First, he completes the site's fourth-century map: [Ge Hong](/people/ge-hong/) in the south consolidating texts, Wei Huacun's lineage generating the Shangqing corpus, and Kou Qianzhi in the north making Daoism an imperial institution — three different relations between practice and power in one generation. Second, he is the site's standing example of the **difference between a practice tradition and its institutionalization**: the reforms that bear his name concern who may teach and who may tax, not how anyone sits — the distinction the [tools pages](/tools/sitting-protocol/) keep sharp. Third, his rejection of the Zhangs' sexual practices is the single most important early precedent for the site's own boundary discipline: the tradition itself, at its first imperial moment, drew lines around what counted as its practice.

**Uncertainty preserved:** the two-revelation structure is the tradition's account; the exact mountain sequence (Huashan vs. Songshan) varies by source and is kept as given; the year of his emergence to court is recorded as 423 by one account and 424 by another — the page keeps the range.
