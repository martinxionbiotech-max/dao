## Who he was

Ge Hong (葛洪), courtesy name Zhichuan (稚川), self-styled *Baopuzi* — "the Master Who Embraces the Uncarved Block" — was born in Danyang Jurong (present-day Jiangsu) around 283 CE into a southern gentry family in decline. He studied with the esoteric master Zheng Yin, heir to the alchemical lineage of Zuo Ci and Ge Xuan; served as a military officer in the campaigns of the early fourth century; and in his fifties obtained the minor magistracy of Goulou (in present-day Guangxi) specifically for its cinnabar deposits — then stopped at Luofu Mountain in Guangdong, was detained there by hospitality and good conditions, and stayed: writing, alchemy, and teaching until his death, traditionally placed in 343 (some accounts extend it to 363, age 81; the sources disagree and this page keeps both dates visible).

## Why this site needs him

Because the [Baopuzi](/texts/baopuzi/) — his great compilation — is the **primary documentary source** for two practices this site covers: the classical definition of [taixi](/practices/taixi/) ("one who has attained taixi breathes without nose or mouth, as in the womb") and the economy-argument for [shouyi](/concepts/shouyi/) ("among thousands of visualization methods, knowing the One completes the ten thousand affairs"). Ge Hong did not invent these practices; he *preserved* them, at a moment when the tradition was consolidating, with a scholar's care and a practitioner's caution — he repeatedly marks what he has practiced, what he has only heard, and what the texts keep secret for oral transmission.

## The three Ge Hongs, kept apart

- **The historian** — author of the *outer* chapters' essays on government and society, and of a partial account of his own life (the Zixu chapter), which is the basis of every later biography.
- **The compiler-practitioner** — author of the *inner* chapters, where the sitting, breathing, and visualization methods of the third–fourth century survive.
- **The legend** — the later tradition's "Little Immortal Elder" (小仙翁), credited with feats of alchemy and immortality in Luofu. This site records the legend as legend and cites the texts.

**Uncertainty preserved:** the death date dispute (343 vs 363) and the hagiographic later material are flagged, not flattened; what is secure is the man's authorship of the inner chapters and their witness to the sitting practices.
