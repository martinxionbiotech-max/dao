## Why this record matters

The exchange begins with the oldest question in moral philosophy — why do the wicked prosper — and ends, thirty minutes later, with the claim that good and evil do not exist. That arc is the interesting thing. Nobody refuted anybody; the question was not answered but dissolved, and the room seemed satisfied.

It is worth keeping because the dissolution is so ordinary. Three positions appear, each with a long history, each doing a different job for the person holding it, and none of them meeting the others.

## The exchange

## 1. The question is a theodicy, and China has a text for it

"Why do the wicked prosper" is not a modern complaint. It is the question that breaks the doctrine of a just heaven, and it has a canonical Chinese statement — in 《史记·伯夷列传》, where 司马迁 asks it about two men who starved rather than do wrong:

> 若伯夷、叔齐，可谓善人者非邪？积仁洁行如此而饿死！…… 盗跖日杀不辜，肝人之肉，暴戾恣睢，聚党数千人横行天下，竟以寿终。是遵何德哉？

*Were Bo Yi and Shu Qi not good men? They accumulated benevolence and purity of conduct, and they starved to death… While Zhi the robber slaughtered the innocent day after day, roasted men's flesh, ran wild with thousands at his back — and died of old age. What virtue was he following?*

And then, having laid the evidence out, he declines to solve it: 余甚惑焉，傥所谓天道，是邪非邪？ — *I am deeply confused. If there is a way of heaven, is it this, or is it not?*

Against that doubt stands 《道德经》 ch. 79: 天道无亲，常与善人 — the way of heaven has no favourites, and yet it is always with the good. The room is arguing across that gap, two thousand years late and without either text in hand.

## 2. Karma's accounting period — and why it cannot lose

A's answer — 命可以担, ancestors' merit, past lives — is not folk improvisation. It has a doctrine, and the doctrine was built precisely to answer this objection.

慧远's 《三报论》 sets it out: 业有三报 — 现报, 生报, 后报. Some deeds ripen in this life, some in the next, some after two, three, a hundred or a thousand lives. That structure is the point: it explains why the wicked man is comfortable *now* — his good fortune is an earlier deposit, his punishment is booked forward. The accounting period is simply longer than a life.

That is also why the answer cannot lose. Any outcome fits: prosperity proves carried 命 or prior merit; ruin proves the debt came due; a life that shows neither proves the account is still open. A theory that accommodates every observation is not explaining anything — it is consoling, which is a different function and should be named as one.

A's ancestral version has a text as well, and it is the one that makes the ledger hereditary — 《周易·坤·文言》:

> 积善之家，必有余庆；积不善之家，必有余殃。

*A family that accumulates good has surplus blessings; a family that accumulates evil has surplus calamity.*

Note what this does to the moral bookkeeping. It moves the unit of account from the individual to the lineage, which is why A can say "the ancestors accumulated it" and why the poor can be told, in effect, that their grandparents are the reason. The consolation is real for the person who receives it. Its cost is also real: it turns an injustice into an invoice, and it sends the invoice to the person who can least afford it.

## 3. 德位: the aphorism is a warning, not a promise

The room spends several turns on 德位相配. It is worth being precise, because the saying everyone has in mind is not what the classical text says.

The passage is 《易·系辞下》:

> 德薄而位尊，智小而谋大，力小而任重，鲜不及矣。

*Virtue thin but position honoured, wisdom small but plans great, strength small but the burden heavy — rarely does one come through.*

Read it plainly: it predicts collapse. It does not promise that virtue will be rewarded with office, and it does not describe a just distribution. It says that a mismatch is dangerous — which is precisely B's point ("when has virtue ever matched position?"), not A's. The popular formula 德不配位，必有灾殃 is a modern condensation of this passage, and it has drifted: the original warns the person who holds the position; the modern saying is usually used to explain why someone else's fall was deserved.

So 山茶花's "德位相配 is an ideal" and B's "it has never matched" are both closer to the text than the assumption that it should. The mismatch is the normal condition the warning addresses.

B's next move is the sharpest thing said in the first half: 分寸 — the measure of doing bad things. That is an accurate description of a certain kind of successful operator. The point is not that they are lawless; it is that they are precise. They know exactly how far a contract can be pushed, where a disclosure obligation ends, which harm is actionable and which is merely cruel. But this is a **risk-management skill, not a virtue**, and the room slides from one to the other. C says "they know the measure, so perhaps their virtue matches their position" — the slide from *effective* to *good* is the oldest mistake in the book, and it is how comfortable people explain comfort.

## 4. C's three positions, and the slide between them

C does the most work in the exchange and ends furthest from where he began.

**The consensus rule.** If everyone says a man is bad, he is bad; if only you think so, you have the problem. As a claim about **reputation**, this is true and useful: a name is made by other people, and a man can be ruined without having done anything, or protected after doing a great deal. As a claim about **conduct**, it is false, and the difference matters. B objects at once — "isn't that just following the crowd?" — and B is right.

**The political criterion.** Anything not beneficial to the people is evil. It sounds decisive. It is circular until "the people", "beneficial" and "harm" are specified, and each of those is contested in exactly the way the previous sentence was. It also changes the subject: from what a person did to whether the outcome was useful to a collective.

**The dissolution.** Dig deeper and good and evil do not exist; it all depends on the definition. This is a non sequitur, and a common one. That definitions vary, and that some are political, does not entail that there is nothing to define. We do not conclude that "poison" is unreal because the label has been applied wrongly. The step from *contested* to *nonexistent* is the move that ends the conversation, which is presumably why it is popular.

**C's best line is the last one.** If I call you a bad person, you will label me in return, and I lose whatever benefit I might have had from you — so I will not say it. That is a genuinely sharp observation about **speech incentives**: moral accusation is expensive for the accuser, so public moral talk is systematically unreliable, and the people with the most to lose say the least. It is true. It is also completely irrelevant to whether the act was wrong — which is the tell that the conversation has stopped being about morality and become about strategy.

## 5. The question nobody answered

C asks, in passing, why defrauding someone of money is a crime while defrauding them of affection is not. Nobody picks it up. It is the most concrete question in the thread, and it has a real answer.

Law protects what it can define, prove and price. Money has a ledger, title, and a measurable loss; a court can order it returned. Affection has no ledger, no title, and no reliable way to prove that a promise caused a loss rather than a disappointment. The line is drawn by **enforceability**, not by harm — which is why the most damaging things a person can do to another are frequently legal, and why "it isn't illegal" is such a poor guide to whether it was wrong. A room trying to work out what evil is could have started there and had a real conversation.

## 6. The one operational answer, delivered as a joke

The last line in the exchange is A's: one kind of person does evil and benefits both himself and others; another does evil and benefits himself at others' expense.

It is offered lightly, with a laugh, and it is the only sentence in thirty minutes that can be applied to a case tomorrow. It does not ask what the man is, or what his 命 can carry, or how the group defines its terms. It asks **who pays**. That is a question with an answer, and the answer can be checked.

The first category is worth pausing on, because it is not a joke: there are acts that harm someone abstractly while benefiting a great many concretely — a factory that pollutes a river and employs a town, a development that displaces a dozen households and houses a thousand. Calling those "evil that benefits both" is a category error at the level of the individual and an accurate description at the level of the ledger. Which is exactly why "who pays" is a better question than "is he evil": it forces the second half of the sentence to be spoken.

## Editor's judgment

This is opinion, marked as opinion.

**Karma answers the question by making it unanswerable, and sends the bill to the poor.** That is the cost, and it is not small. A doctrine that reads prosperity as stored merit and poverty as inherited debt gives the comfortable a clean conscience and gives the struggling an explanation that requires them to accept the fault. It consoles — genuinely, sometimes — and consolation is not nothing. But it should be honest about being consolation rather than explanation.

**B's cynicism is the same consolation from the other side.** "Everyone's mouth is full of good people and their heart is full of bad people" is often true as a description of a room. As a conclusion it is a release from obligation: if nobody is good, there is nothing to be, and no one to be it for. It is karma without the arithmetic — still a way of making the world's injustice bearable, and still at the price of making action pointless.

**C's relativism is the most common educated position and the least useful.** It begins as a fair point about reputation, borrows the authority of philosophy, and ends by removing the ground it is standing on. Note that C also supplies the exchange's one real insight — that moral speech is shaped by the cost of speaking — and then uses it to change the subject rather than to explain why the subject is hard.

**The room never separates three questions.** Why does this man prosper? Is he evil? What should I do? Karma answers the first, C's consensus answers the second, and nobody answers the third. Most moral arguments that go in circles are three arguments wearing one coat.

**My own answer to the first question is boring and does not console.** People prosper because they hold capital, or access, or information, or a tolerance for risk that others do not; because they are inside a network that routes opportunities to them; and because a great deal of profitable harm is legal. None of those is a moral category. That is the uncomfortable part: the distribution of prosperity is not a verdict, and treating it as one — whether by karma, by "德位", or by "he must be secretly good" — is an attempt to make an amoral mechanism mean something.

**To the third question, the only answer in the room is A's last line, and it is enough to start.** Not "is he evil" but "who is paying for this, and can that cost be moved". That question survives contact with a real case, and it does not require either a cosmology or a definition of evil.

**And one line in the exchange deserves to be taken seriously rather than pitied.** A's "live your own days well, do no evil, cheat no one" is usually read as a retreat. It is a retreat — the question was too big, so it was made private. But it is also the only position in the room that produces an action: today, in front of you, with the people you actually deal with. It is a smaller answer than the question asked for. It is also the only one that does not require anyone else to be wrong.

## What this does not say

- It is not the site's position. It is an anonymized transcript with the editor's opinion clearly marked.
- It does not say karma is false. It says that as usually stated it cannot be tested, and that its social cost is real.
- It does not say that prosperity is proof of wrongdoing, or that rich people are bad. It says prosperity is not a moral verdict in either direction.
- It does not say law and morality are unrelated. It says legality is a poor proxy for rightness, and why.
- It does not settle what evil is. It says the question "who pays" can be answered while that one is still open.
