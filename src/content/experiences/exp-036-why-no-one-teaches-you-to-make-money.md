## A note on what this is

This entry is different from most in the archive. It is not the editor's reflection and not a clinical or research report. It is a **contributed note** — a chat exchange reproduced as it happened, in translation, from a correspondent writing under the handle 鼐熋.

The contributor closed with their own caveat, and it is kept in the text rather than buried: this is the reflector's view, not the site's position, offered for reference and discussion.

The editor's part is confined to two things: supplying the classical references the note argues inside but does not name, and stating plainly what the note claims versus what it shows. The note's own argument is left standing as it was made.

## 1. The utopia the note names is a real text

The note's opening move is to explain a national silence about money by pointing at a peasant ethic. Whether or not that explanation is right, the ethic it describes is not invented.

It has a text. The 道德经's eightieth chapter is usually titled by its first four characters, 小国寡民 — a small state, few people:

> 使有什伯之器而不用……邻国相望，鸡犬之声相闻，民至老死不相往来。

*Let them have weapons and machinery and not use them… neighbouring states within sight of one another, the crowing of cocks and barking of dogs audible across the border, and the people grow old and die without visiting one another.*

Read as economics rather than as nostalgia, the passage describes an economy with almost no exchange. And an economy with no exchange is one in which **no one can profit from anyone** — which is exactly the ethic the note attributes to the peasant: your plot yields its fixed amount, my plot yields mine, the boundary is absolute, and gain at another's expense is not merely imprudent but wrong.

This is worth pausing on, because it means the note is not describing a folk superstition. It is describing the economic logic of one of the tradition's own founding images of the good society.

Two cautions, though. First, this is a **reading** of 小国寡民, not the only one — the chapter has been read as primitivist nostalgia, as a description of a state so well ordered that its machinery goes unused, and as a thought experiment about scale rather than a programme. Second, the note takes that ethic as the *cause* of a present-day culture. That is a much larger claim than the text can carry, and the note offers no evidence for the causal step.

## 2. The premise the tradition does not support

The note's moral premise is that 我要赚你的钱 is 不义 — to profit from you is unrighteous.

That premise belongs to a long argument, the 义利之辨 — the debate over righteousness and profit — and it is worth knowing that the tradition did not settle the way the note assumes.

The famous line is 论语·里仁: 君子喻于义，小人喻于利 — the noble person understands righteousness, the small person understands profit. Read carelessly it sounds like a verdict against profit. But the contrast is between two things a person may understand, not between a thing permitted and a thing forbidden.

The most careful statement of the problem is in 荀子·大略, and it is worth quoting at length because it answers the note almost directly:

> 义与利者，人之所两有也。虽尧舜不能去民之欲利；然而能使其欲利不克其好义也……故义胜利者为治世，利克义者为乱世。

*Righteousness and profit are both things that people have. Even Yao and Shun could not remove the people's desire for profit; what they could do was prevent that desire from defeating the love of righteousness… So where righteousness prevails over profit, the age is well governed; where profit overcomes righteousness, the age is in disorder.*

On this account the problem is not profit. The problem is the **order** between profit and righteousness. Desire for gain is not a moral defect to be extirpated — it is a fact about people that even a sage cannot remove — and a doctrine that treats it as unrighteous does not purify anyone; it only makes the subject unteachable, which is precisely the silence the note is complaining about.

There is also a counter-tradition the note does not mention. In 史记·货殖列传, Sima Qian writes:

> 天下熙熙，皆为利来；天下攘攘，皆为利往。

*All under heaven chatter and bustle — they come for profit, they go for profit.*

He is not deploring this. The chapter is a defence of 货殖 — of trade and the accumulation of goods — and its author treats commerce as an ordinary and necessary part of a world that has to be fed. So "making money is unrighteous" is one position inside a long argument, not the tradition's verdict. Anyone who wants to argue it has to argue it, not assume it.

## 3. The sharp part: the self-learning paradox

The note's best observation is not about peasants or profit. It is this: **every cognitive framework any of us has was acquired by ourselves, and yet what people ask for is a procedure to copy.**

That is a real gap and it is not a small one. Nobody taught you to read a room, to judge a person, to know when a price is wrong. Those came from doing, failing, and adjusting. And yet the same person who learned all of that by trial will ask, about money, for the one thing that cannot be given: a model whose copying produces the result.

What the note gets right is that this expectation is not neutral. It is a **demand for transferable knowledge in a domain where the knowledge is largely tacit** — the kind of thing that can be shown but not stated, learned by apprenticeship but not by lecture. Polanyi's term for it is tacit knowledge; the Chinese tradition has its own word for the adjacent problem, 意会, understanding that arrives without being spelled out.

Where the note overstates is the word 没有人教. People do teach it — in family firms, in apprenticeships, in the ordinary transmission of a shop from a parent to a child, in the first job where someone senior bothers to correct you. The scarcity is not curriculum. It is **feedback**: someone who will tell you, at your own expense, that you were wrong. That is expensive, it is unflattering, and it is exactly what a copyable model promises to replace.

And the note's own best question goes unanswered: *where does the belief in a copyable model come from?* It asks, and then moves on. Three candidate answers, offered here as hypotheses rather than findings:

- **Schooling rewards reproduction.** For most of a person's life, the rewarded act is producing the procedure that was taught, not constructing one. The habit is learned early and does not announce itself as a habit.
- **A model locates failure outside the self.** If the method was correct and the result was bad, the fault is the world's. Self-teaching has no such shelter — every failure is yours, which is why the copyable model is not only convenient but comforting.
- **It is a residue of an inherited economy.** Where the path was in fact passed down — the land, the trade, the shop — the model really did exist and really did work. The expectation may be a memory of that world surviving into one where the transmission has stopped.

## 4. What the note claims but does not show

This has to be said plainly, because the alternative is to let a good observation launder a set of unsupported ones.

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Nobody teaches how to earn money | **Overstated.** Many do — family firms, apprenticeships, trade schools, employers. What is scarce is reliable feedback, not instruction. |
| The cause is a peasant-utopian ethic | **Unsupported.** A plausible cultural hypothesis with no evidence offered, and one that cannot be tested as stated. |
| People's notions of assets, cash and profit are blank | **Unsupported and unfalsifiable.** A claim about the inner life of an entire population, asserted without a single case. |
| The same is true of their concept of life | **Unsupported**, and doing a great deal of work in the argument on no evidence at all. |

The note also slides between two different things: *nobody teaches it* and *it cannot be taught*. The first may be partly true of a given milieu. The second is false, and the slide is what lets the first do the work of the second.

There is a further caution, and it is the one this site owes its readers. A thesis that explains a population's economic behaviour by its mentality has a long and not always honourable history. It can be true. It can also be a way of converting a structural problem — no capital, no credit, no market access, no margin — into a character flaw, which is both unkind and analytically lazy. The note does not make that move explicitly. But a reader should notice how easily the move is available.

## What this does not say

- It is not the site's position. The contributor's own caveat is retained above and is meant.
- It does not say that profit-taking is righteous, or that the 义利 problem is settled. It says the tradition argued about it, and that the argument is more interesting than the commonplace.
- It does not say that people who fail to earn are failing because of their mindset. Structural constraints are real, and the note's own evidence does not rule them in or out.
- It does not say that no one can be taught to earn. It says the teachable part is smaller than people hope and the unteachable part is larger, which is a different claim.
- It does not endorse 小国寡民 as a programme. The passage is cited because it is the text the note's ethic resembles, not because it is a recommendation.
