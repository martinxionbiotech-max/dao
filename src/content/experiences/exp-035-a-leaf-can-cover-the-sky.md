## Why this record matters

Most of the entries in this archive describe what happens on the cushion — pain, breath, drowsiness, unusual states. This one is about the ordinary mechanics of being pulled: the remark that ruins an evening, the purchase that promises more than it delivers, the plan that will not stop rehearsing itself at two in the morning.

It is also the first entry here that says something uncomfortable and then immediately qualifies it: that a great deal of what we call trouble is not done to us but *held up in front of us*. The qualification matters as much as the claim.

## The reflection

> Who in this world is not striving to cross?
>
> And in the dust of the world, a single leaf can cover the sky.
>
> To have your mood moved by something outside you — is that not already to have entered the game?
>
> What one can do is not to refuse the game, but to move with the current.
>
> **The noble person is not commanded by things.**
>
> The more you care about a thing, the more it will occupy your thinking. So govern your own desires, follow the current, and answer to what heaven has apportioned.
>
> What you can take, take with all your effort; what you cannot take, put down cleanly.
>
> Do not look back too much at the past; do not expect too much of the future.
>
> And then — where would so many troubles come from?

## The leaf does not cover the sky. The position does.

The second line is the one worth slowing down for, because it is easy to hear as poetry and miss as a description.

A leaf is small. The sky is not. If a leaf covers the sky, it is not because the leaf grew — it is because the leaf is *in front of the eye*, and near. The classical saying the line echoes, 一叶蔽目，不见太山, makes the same point with a mountain: not that the mountain is small, but that a leaf held close enough removes it from view entirely.

This is the whole mechanism of 入局 in one image. What blocks the sky is rarely the size of the thing; it is its proximity to the eye. A comment from a stranger does not have the power to ruin a week. But held close — re-read, rehearsed, argued with in the imagination — it occupies the whole visual field, and the week is gone.

The practical consequence is not "ignore it". It is that the lever is *distance*, not strength. You do not defeat a leaf by being stronger than it. You move it away from your eye — and the sky is simply there again, unimpressed by the whole drama.

## 入局 — being moved is already a move

The reflection's sharpest line is its question: if your mood is moved by something outside you, have you not already entered the game?

This is a stricter claim than it first sounds, and it has a precise classical companion. The 《大学》 lists the conditions under which the mind loses its correctness: 有所忿懥，则不得其正；有所恐惧，则不得其正；有所好乐，则不得其正；有所忧患，则不得其正 — when there is anger, the mind is not correct; when there is fear, it is not correct; when there is liking, it is not correct; when there is anxiety, it is not correct. The list is notable for what it does *not* say. It does not say these states are sinful. It says that in them, the mind is no longer level — and a mind that is not level cannot see straight.

Zhu Xi's commentary on the passage sharpens this considerably, and in a direction the reflection would endorse. The four states, he notes, are 皆心之用，而人所不能无者 — functions of the mind that no one can be without. The problem is not their presence but 一有之而不能察 — that once one is present and goes **unnoticed**, 欲动情胜, desire moves and feeling overpowers. On that reading, the failing is not feeling but failing to see the feeling while it is happening. Which is another way of saying what 入局 describes: the stake does not capture you at the moment you acquire it; it captures you at the moment you stop noticing it has a say.

So 入局 is not a moral failing. It is a description of a state: you have been given a stake, and the stake is now doing the steering. The board does not have to be a bad one. Wanting something good — a job, a person's approval, a sale — puts you in the game just as surely as wanting something base. That is what makes the reflection useful and what makes it hard: there is no clean exit by choosing better objects. The exit, if there is one, is in the relation to the object, not in the object.

## 君子使物，不为物使

The reflection's line is almost exactly a sentence from the 管子·内业: 君子使物，不为物使 — the noble person puts things to use, and is not put to use by them. It is the closest classical match to the wording, and it is worth noticing how little room the sentence leaves: 使 is an active verb in both halves, so the choice is not between using and not using, but between which direction the verb runs.

The better-known parallel comes from the 荀子·修身: 君子役物，小人役于物 — the noble person commands things; the small person is commanded by them. Two things about that version are worth keeping.

First, it is quoted there as 传曰 — "the tradition says" — which means Xunzi is not inventing it. The line was already old when he used it. It is one of the oldest formulations of a problem that has not aged: the difference between using a thing and being used by it.

Second, the verb in the Xunzi version is 役 — corvée, forced labour. Not "influenced by", not "fond of". *Conscripted*. The claim is that a person who is commanded by what they want is not merely distracted; they are working, unpaid, for something else. That is a much harder image than "attachment", and it is closer to what the reflection is describing.

The test it implies is not "do I want this?" — wanting is not the problem — but "who is giving the orders?" If the answer to "why am I doing this today?" is a thing outside you, then the thing is in charge, whatever the vocabulary you use for it.

## The tension the reflection does not hide

There is a seam running through this reflection, and it is worth naming rather than smoothing over.

**顺势而为** — move with the current — sounds like release. **能拿的尽力拿** — take what you can with all your effort — sounds like the opposite. One posture yields; the other pushes. A reader could reasonably ask which one is being recommended.

The reflection's answer, I think, is that they are two halves of one stance, and that either half alone is a distortion:

- Effort **with** grip is the ordinary striving the reflection opens by naming: everyone is trying to cross, and the trying becomes the whole of life.
- Release **without** effort is its counterfeit — the thing that borrows the vocabulary of 顺其自然 and uses it to describe giving up. The tradition has a word for this too, and it is not a compliment.
- Effort **without** grip is what is being pointed at: take the shot with everything you have, and do not let the outcome take up residence in front of your eye. 尽人事，听天命 — do the human part fully, leave the rest to what is apportioned — is the standard Chinese compression of it, and it is a sentence about *sequence*, not about proportion. The effort comes first and comes whole. The letting go comes after, and is about the result.

This is not a comfortable position. It requires doing two things that feel contradictory at the same time, and it has no technique that makes them stop feeling contradictory. What it has is a test: when the outcome arrives, is the leaf still in front of your eye?

## What this does not say

- It does not say that being moved is wrong, or that feeling is a failure of practice. Being moved is what a nervous system does. The claim is about what happens *after* the movement — whether the thing is handed the controls or not.
- It does not say to care about nothing. 不在意 is not 不在乎. The reflection asks for full effort, which is impossible without caring. What it asks is that caring not become conscription.
- It does not say that all trouble is self-made. Illness, loss, poverty and injustice are not leaves held in front of the eye, and telling someone in genuine hardship that their suffering is a matter of attention would be a misuse of this reflection, not an application of it.
- It does not say that letting go means giving up. The line 能拿的尽力拿 is explicit: the effort is not optional, and it comes first.
- It does not say that 天命 excuses passivity. "What heaven apportions" is not a licence to do nothing; it is a name for the part one does not control, and naming it is supposed to free the hands for the part one does.
- It does not claim the classical sources as Daoist. 荀子 and 《大学》 are Confucian texts, and 顺势 is used here in a modern, Daoist-adjacent sense. The reflection is a lay synthesis, and it is presented as one.
