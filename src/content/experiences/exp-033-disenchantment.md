## Why this record matters

Most of the material in this archive is about what happens on the cushion — pain, breath, drowsiness, strange states. This note is about what happens **off** it, in the ordinary transactions of a day: the authority one grants a teacher, a superior, a lecturer, a face in the street, and what becomes visible when that grant is withdrawn. It belongs to the site's practice layer rather than its research layer: it is a description of an attentional habit, not a claim about the world, and it is published as the editor's own reflection rather than as a community report.

## The reflection

> In the work of cultivating an ordinary mind, one baseline capacity matters more than most — and it is a capacity many people would rather you did without. It is the capacity for **disenchantment**: the ability to take the glow off.
>
> When you have it, the world looks different. A beautiful woman loses her ethereal aura. The leader is no longer towering and impeccable. The professor at the podium is no longer the seat of wisdom and learning. Even the master you once knelt to is no longer omnipotent.
>
> Everything shows itself as it is. They too are sentient beings turning in the six realms. They too have the seven emotions and six desires. They too are walking through the eight sufferings of life.
>
> **Exactly like you.**

The claim is not that the teacher is a fraud, the leader incompetent, or the professor ignorant. It is narrower and more useful: the **aura** is added by the observer, and it is the aura — not the person — that the capacity removes. What remains is a person with a profession, a history, a temper and a set of difficulties, who knows some things and not others.

## The framework it draws on

The reflection uses a standard Buddhist vocabulary without arguing for it, and the terms are worth setting out plainly:

- **六道轮回 — the six realms of rebirth.** The round of existence — hell-beings, hungry ghosts, animals, asuras, humans, devas — in which every being, including every teacher and every authority, is still turning. No position in the world removes one from the round.
- **有情众生 — sentient beings.** The category that includes the master and the student alike: beings with feeling, not disembodied authorities. The term is doing the work of the reflection's last line.
- **七情六欲 — the seven emotions and six desires.** The ordinary emotional and sensory life that no role abolishes: joy, anger, sorrow, fear, love, hate and desire, and the desires that arrive through the six senses.
- **人生八苦 — the eight sufferings of life.** Birth, ageing, sickness, death, parting from the loved, meeting the hated, not getting what one seeks, and the suffering of the five aggregates. The shared itinerary; the one thing every authority has in common with the person looking at them.
- **平常心 — the ordinary mind.** The term the reflection organises itself around, and the Chan tradition's own: 平常心是道, "the ordinary mind is the Way." Practice is located in plain, unglamorous functioning — not in exceptional states, and not in exceptional people.

## What this capacity is not

The reflection is easy to misread in two directions, and both are worth naming.

**It is not contempt.** The closing line — *exactly like you* — puts the observer on the same footing as the observed, not above them. A version of this capacity that ends in disdain for teachers, leaders or beautiful people has changed direction rather than deepened: it has replaced one projection with another, in which the observer is the one who sees clearly.

**It is not a tool for judging others.** The capacity is most useful when it is turned on one's own self-image at the same time — when the glow comes off the teacher and off the observer in the same movement. Used only outward, it becomes a way of feeling superior to people one has never met.

## A note on register

This is a first-person reflection, not a report: there is no anonymised community source behind it, no episode to cite, and no claim that the capacity is easy, stable or universally available. It is published because it names something the archive's other records keep running into from the side — the question of how much of what a practitioner sees in a teacher is the teacher. The site's standing rule applies as usual: it is recorded as one practitioner's account, not as a finding, and the doctrinal terms are glossed rather than endorsed.
