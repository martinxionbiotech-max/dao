## Why this record matters

This is the kind of text that arrives in a family group chat at seven in the morning, gets forwarded twice, and is never read closely. It is also, paragraph by paragraph, a compact anthology of three separate traditions that are usually kept apart: Buddhist doctrine (the eight sufferings, karma), Chinese popular religion (physiognomy, feng shui), and modern self-help (do not live in other people's opinions).

None of those is a problem in itself. Devotional writing is allowed to blend registers. The problem is that the blend makes the text feel more authoritative than any of its parts, and that the most confident sentence in it — the one beginning 佛曰 — is the one with the weakest textual basis.

It is worth keeping because it shows, in four short paragraphs, how a doctrine becomes a proverb, and what is gained and lost in the process.

## The text

*Translated from the Chinese; the original lines are retained where the wording matters. Emoji removed, paragraph order unchanged.*

> 心有多好，命就有多好，心有多静，福就有多深。你的心决定了你的相貌，你的心就是你的风水。人心慈则貌美，人心善则面善……所谓福相，就是人心向善。

*How good the heart is, so good the fate; how still the heart is, so deep the blessing. The heart decides the face; the heart is your feng shui. A kind heart makes a beautiful face; a good heart makes a kind face… the so-called blessed face is simply a heart inclined to good.*

> 从表面看来，善良的人可能诸多磨难，而作恶之人多享快乐。其实真正的因果恰恰相反，自己现在受用的，是以前所造善恶业的果报。而自己现在造作的，是未来的因。

*On the surface the good may meet many hardships while the wicked enjoy themselves. In truth the causality is the reverse: what you enjoy now is the fruit of good and evil done before; what you do now is the cause of what comes next.*

> 人生八苦：生，老，病，死，爱别离，怨长久，求不得，放不下。佛曰：命由己造，相由心生……坐亦禅，行亦禅，一花一世界，一叶一如来，春来花自青，秋至叶飘零，无穷般若心自在，语默动静体自然。

*The eight sufferings of life: birth, age, illness, death, parting from the loved, long resentment, not getting what one seeks, being unable to let go. The Buddha said: fate is self-made, appearance arises from the mind… sitting is Chan, walking is Chan; one flower, one world; one leaf, one Tathagata; in spring the flower is green of itself, in autumn the leaf drifts down; infinite prajna, the mind at ease.*

> 生活在人世间，虽然我们受到很多条条框框所限，但没有限制你的心灵。所以不要活在别人的观念里……人生短暂，不要为别人而活，过自己的日子，活自己的人生。鞋子合不合适脚知道，生活快不快乐心清楚！

*Living in the world, we are constrained by many rules, but your mind is not constrained. So do not live inside other people's ideas… life is short; do not live for others. Live your own days. Whether the shoe fits, the foot knows; whether life is happy, the heart knows.*

## 1. Two promises in one text

Paragraph one is a promise: a good heart produces a good fate, a still heart produces deep blessing, and the mechanism is visible in the face. Paragraph two is a correction: the wicked prosper, the good suffer, and the ledger settles later.

These are not the same claim. The first is a prosperity formula with a short delivery time — be good and your life improves. The second postpones delivery past the end of a life. Once the accounting period is extended, the first paragraph can no longer be checked by anything in this world, and it stops being advice about how to live and becomes a statement about a system no one can observe.

The text does not notice the swap, and most readers will not either, because the two paragraphs are separated by an emoji and a line break. But the sequence matters: the promise is made first, and the correction arrives as reassurance rather than as a retraction. That is how devotional writing usually works — the consolation is stated plainly, the caveat is stated warmly.

Which of the two is the better teaching? The second, clearly. It admits the world as it is. It does not tell the suffering that their suffering is evidence of virtue rewarded, and it does not tell the comfortable that their comfort is proof of it either. It says only: do not be fooled by the present arrangement.

That last instruction — 不要被当下福报迷惑 — is the part of paragraph two that survives without any cosmology. Comfort is anesthetic. It is worth saying on its own.

## 2. 相由心生: which 相?

The phrase is doing two jobs in this text, and they are not compatible.

In canonical Buddhist usage, 相 (Sanskrit lakṣaṇa) means a mark, sign or characteristic — the way a thing appears to be grasped. 诸法空相 does not mean that all things have empty faces; it means that all dharmas are without a fixed mark. The phrase 相由心生, read in that vocabulary, is an epistemological claim: the marks we perceive are shaped by the mind that perceives them.

In the text, and in most popular usage, 相 means the face. 人心慈则貌美 is a claim about appearance. That is a different claim, from a different tradition — Chinese physiognomy (相法), where the face is read as a map of character and fate.

The first reading is philosophically serious and hard to test. The second is easy to test and, as stated, false: kind people are not reliably beautiful, and beautiful people are not reliably kind. What makes the second reading survive is not evidence but a halo effect — we read a pleasant face as a good person, then count the impression as proof of the rule.

There is a defensible middle. Repeated states do leave traces: the set of the mouth, the line between the brows, the speed of a smile, the way attention lands on other people. A person who has spent thirty years being irritable looks different from one who has spent thirty years being curious, and the difference is not magic — it is muscle, habit and expectation. That reading keeps the causal chain from mind to face and drops the inference from face to virtue. It also drops the promise that the face is a verdict.

The text wants both. 人心慈则貌美 is causal and cosmetic; 福相就是人心向善 is definitional and moral. The second is an attempt to rescue the first by redefining its terms: a blessed face is not a beautiful one, it is a good heart. That is a sensible move, and it quietly concedes the argument. Once 福相 means character rather than appearance, the physiognomy has been abandoned and the sentence is simply ethics.

One more register is worth naming: 你的心就是你的风水. Feng shui is geomancy — the reading of place. Using it as a metaphor for the mind is a modern syncretism, not a classical Buddhist idea, and it tells you what kind of text this is. It is devotional self-help, drawing on whatever vocabulary is currently persuasive.

## 3. Karma on a longer ledger

The karma answer in paragraph two is not folk improvisation. It has a doctrine, and the doctrine was built to answer precisely this objection.

慧远's 《三报论》 sets it out: 业有三报 — 现报, 生报, 后报. Some deeds ripen in this life, some in the next, some after two, three, a hundred or a thousand lives. The structure is the point: the wicked man is comfortable now because his good fortune is an earlier deposit, and his punishment is booked forward. The accounting period is simply longer than a life.

That answers the theodicy. It also makes the theory unfalsifiable: prosperity proves carried 命 or prior merit, ruin proves the debt came due, and a life that shows neither proves the account is still open. A theory that accommodates every observation is not explaining anything. It is consoling, which is a different function and should be named as one.

The social cost is already visible in this archive (see EXP-040). Once the unit of account is the individual or the lineage, suffering can be read as a debt, and the person who suffers can be told — gently, or not — that the fault is theirs or their grandparents'. This text is milder than most: it does not say the poor deserve their poverty. But the structure is the same, and the gentleness of the tone does not change the accounting.

What survives without the cosmology is the practical instruction: do not let comfort anesthetize you. That is true whether karma is a law of nature or a story we tell to make injustice bearable. The instruction does not require the metaphysics, and the metaphysics does not need the instruction. They travel together because the text is devotional, not because they depend on each other.

## 4. The eight sufferings, and the one that was swapped out

The canonical list is stable across the Chinese canon. 《增一阿含经》 gives 生、老、病、死、愛別離、怨憎會、求不得、五陰盛苦; the same eight are standard in 《大般涅槃經》 and in the commentarial tradition.

The forwarded text gives: 生，老，病，死，爱别离，怨长久，求不得，放不下.

Two items have changed. 怨长久 is almost certainly a mishearing or miswriting of 怨憎会 — meeting what one hates, the mirror of parting from what one loves. The substitution is small and the meaning survives: resentment that lasts is close enough to resentment that keeps returning.

The second substitution is not small. 五阴炽盛 — the burning of the five aggregates — has been replaced by 放不下, being unable to let go.

五阴炽盛 is the most analytical item in the list. It says that suffering is not only a set of unfortunate events but a condition of the way a person is put together: form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness are burning with clinging, and even pleasant experience is unstable, conditioned and therefore unsatisfactory. Remove it and the eight sufferings become a list of misfortunes — birth, age, illness, death, loss, dislike, frustration. Keep it and the list becomes a diagnosis: the problem is not that bad things happen, but that the aggregates grasp.

放不下 is a symptom, not a category. It is therapeutically useful — it names the experience of being stuck — and analytically empty: it tells you what the person is doing, not why the structure of experience makes it inevitable. Swapping the two turns a doctrine into a slogan, and the slogan is easier to remember. That is probably why the swap spread.

It also changes the ending. The canonical list ends with the summary item; the folk list ends with 放不下, which makes the whole list feel like a message about attachment. Attachment is a real part of the teaching, but it is not the whole of it, and a list that ends there quietly replaces the analysis of the aggregates with a piece of advice about attitude.

None of this makes the text wrong as devotional writing. It makes it a different teaching from the one it claims to quote.

## 5. "佛曰" as a citation practice

Two quatrains in the text are presented as the Buddha's words.

The first — 命由己造，相由心生，世间万物皆是化相…… — is widely attributed to 《无常经》. The second — 一花一世界，一叶一如来，春来花自青，秋至叶飘零…… — is widely attributed to 《华严经》.

I could not trace either to a specific passage, fascicle or citation in the Chinese canon. Both read like Chinese devotional compositions built from Buddhist vocabulary — the second especially, with its 华严 imagery of the one and the many, its seasonal parallel and its 般若 vocabulary. The attributions are popular rather than textual: the verses circulate in collections of 佛语 and 禅语, and the label 佛曰 is carried along with them.

Why does this matter? Because 佛曰 is an authority claim. It converts a devotional poem into doctrine, and it makes disagreement feel like impiety. The ethical content of the verses does not need it. 命由己造 is a claim about responsibility that can be argued on its own; 一花一世界 is an image that can be appreciated without a canon behind it. Labeling the verses as devotional poetry would make the text more honest and, to a skeptical reader, more persuasive, not less.

This site's own rule is that traditional interpretation is not primary source. The same discipline applies here: a text that says 佛曰 should be able to say which sutra, and if it cannot, the honest label is 偈语 or 禅语, not 佛说.

## 6. The fourth paragraph is the most modern and the least Buddhist

The last paragraph is the one most people will remember, and it is the one that belongs least to the tradition the text invokes.

不要活在别人的观念里, do not let other people's opinions decide your life, is modern expressive individualism. Its ancestors are Romanticism and Emerson, not Chan. It treats the self as something to be defended against the crowd, and freedom as the removal of external constraint.

Buddhist freedom runs the other way. 解脱 is release from clinging — including clinging to the self-view. 无我 does not strengthen the self against other people; it loosens the self that needs defending. The two can coexist as 方便 (skillful means): use the modern language of autonomy to get someone to stop outsourcing their life, and if they go further, the self that was being defended turns out to be a construction. But the directions are opposite, and the text does not notice.

It is still the most immediately useful paragraph. Many people are governed by other people's opinions, and the permission to stop is real. The mistake would be to think that permission is the whole of the teaching.

The best sentence in the text is the last one: 鞋子合不合适脚知道，生活快不快乐心清楚. It is not a Buddhist formula; it is an epistemological claim, and a good one. It says the test is first-person, and it quietly undercuts the 佛曰 above it: if the foot knows, you do not need a quotation to authorize your shoe. The text ends by trusting experience over authority, which is a more radical move than it looks.

There is also a tension between paragraph three and paragraph four that the text does not resolve. 心不动，万物皆不动 is quietist: if the mind does not move, nothing moves. Paragraph four is activist: go and seek your freedom. One says the world is settled by inner stillness; the other says the world must be left. They can be reconciled if 不动 means non-attachment rather than passivity — but that reconciliation is not in the text, and the reader is left to supply it.

## Editor's judgment

This is opinion, marked as opinion.

**What holds.** The observation that present comfort can mislead is true and useful, and it does not need karma to be true. The permission not to live inside other people's opinions is useful, even if its ancestry is not Buddhist. The first-person test at the end is the best epistemology in the text.

**What does not.** The causal promise that a good heart produces a good fate is not supported by the world, and the text itself admits it two paragraphs later. The physiognomic inference — that the face reveals the moral state — is an unfalsifiable social judgment, and it does real harm: it flatters the well-favoured, indicts the plain, and gives people a license to read character off a jawline. The 佛曰 attributions are unverified, and presenting devotional verse as the Buddha's words is a small dishonesty that makes the rest of the text easier to dismiss.

**What I would change.** Restore 五阴炽盛, or at least mark the substitution; a reader who knows the list will notice, and a reader who does not is being taught a different doctrine under the old name. Label the quatrains as 偈语 rather than 佛说. And separate the consolation from the ethics: the first is a way of bearing injustice, the second is a way of acting, and they should be allowed to stand or fall separately.

**My own position.** The durable claim in this text is about attention. What you attend to repeatedly becomes your expression, your habits, and eventually the shape of your days. That is naturalistic, it does not require karma or physiognomy, and it can be tested in the only laboratory that matters — your own. The metaphysics consoles; the attention claim instructs. I would keep the second and hold the first lightly.

I do not think the text is wrong to console. I think it is wrong to present consolation as causation. The difference matters, because a consolation can be accepted as a consolation and still do its work, while a false causation, once found out, takes the consolation down with it.

## What this does not say

- It is not the site's position. It is a forwarded devotional text with the editor's opinion clearly marked.
- It does not say karma is false. It says that as usually stated it cannot be tested, and that its social cost is real.
- It does not say that beautiful people are good or that plain people are bad. It says the inference from appearance to virtue is unreliable and harmful.
- It does not say the forwarded text is heretical. It is devotional literature, and devotional literature is allowed to blend registers.
- It does not settle whether 相由心生 is true. It distinguishes two readings and rejects only the physiognomic one.
- It does not claim the verses are forgeries. It says their attribution to the Buddha is unverified and should be labeled as popular.
