**Anonymized note (paraphrased from a public community report).** A practitioner of roughly one to two years' standing reports that in guided mindfulness sessions they fall asleep in the majority of cases, and only occasionally manage to remain awake through the exercise. Their question to the community was whether this is common, and implicitly whether it signals that meditation is failing for them.

Community replies converged on two factors: that guided relaxation material is itself sleep-inducing by design, and that a tired body, once given stillness, takes the opportunity to rest. The replies treated sleep-onset as information about fatigue rather than as a defect of the practitioner.

**Why this note matters.** The report is a representative instance of a recurring community theme — and it maps onto a distinction the classical Chinese sources already drew. The tradition's manuals warn against mistaking drowsiness (昏沉) for entering stillness (入静): real stillness is described as lucid, light, and clear, whereas drowsiness is dull and heavy. The tradition's own remedy list is practical — adjust diet, fix posture, rest before sitting — and does not blame the person. In other words, a problem that strikes modern beginners as personal failure has a two-millennium-old, non-moralizing answer: go sleep, then sit.
