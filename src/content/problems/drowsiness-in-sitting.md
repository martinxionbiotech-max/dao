**Direct answer:** Drowsiness during sitting is common and well-diagnosed in the Chinese sources — and it is **not** stillness. The manuals draw the line in experiential terms: true stillness (入静) is lucid, light, and clear; drowsiness (昏沉) is dull and heavy. Mistaking one for the other is a named beginner's error. The causes are listed, and they are practical rather than moral: heavy or greasy food before sitting, posture collapse (drooping head, bent back), and sitting while exhausted. The remedies follow the causes.

## What the sources say

- **The classical manuals:** three named causes — diet, posture, fatigue — with direct remedies: eat lightly, keep the spine and head upright, rest *before* sitting. The instruction is to correct conditions, never to blame the practitioner.
- **The Tiantai Xiao zhiguan** (see [tiaoxi](/glossary/tiaoxi/)): sleep is one of the five adjustments (diet, sleep, body, breath, mind) made *before* sitting — a formal recognition that the body's rest-debt shows up on the cushion.
- **Modern community answers:** sleep-onset under relaxation is read as accumulated fatigue surfacing — the body taking stillness as permission to rest. This is consistent with, but distinct from, the traditional account: the tradition treats drowsiness as something to *correct*; the modern community often treats it as information to *accept*.

## When to treat it as a medical question

The traditional and community answers assume adequate sleep. Persistent daytime sleep-onset despite adequate rest is a medical matter, not a meditation matter — and this page does not attempt to distinguish them.

**Not medical advice:** if you sleep enough and still cannot stay awake when sitting, seek professional evaluation.
