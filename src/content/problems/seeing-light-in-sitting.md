**Direct answer:** Light and imagery in sitting are a *named* phenomenon in the earliest sources, and the tradition's counsel is uniform: observe without grasping. They are neither an achievement to collect nor, by themselves, a warning. What matters is what you do with them — and whether they persist outside practice.

## What the sources say

- **Zhuangzi ch. 4:** in the same passage that defines emptiness, the text turns to the settled mind and says: "Look into the closed room: **the empty room gives rise to white light**, and good fortune rests there." Traditional commentators read this as the mind's natural clarity appearing when emptied; some modern readers take it more literally as a description of visual phenomena arising in deep quiet. Both readings are traditional; the text supports the image without settling its interpretation — which is itself the point: the image is *invited to rest*, not chased.
- **The Neiguan Jing:** the instruction is 内观己身，澄其心也 — "contemplate your own body within, and clarify the mind." The manual's frame for inner vision is *clarification*, not acquisition: the point of whatever appears is the settled clarity around it.
- **The manuals' uniform counsel (as transmitted):** do not grasp at scenes (勿贪景象). Phenomena that arise in stillness are passed through, not collected; pursuing them is the named error, because grasping turns a settled mind back into a pursuing one.

## The modern reading

Contemporary communities describe the same reports in ordinary terms: hypnagogic-like imagery, phosphene-like light from relaxed closed eyes, visual memory surfacing when outer input drops away. These are descriptions of the same events in a different vocabulary — no dedicated research exists on visual phenomena in Daoist sitting specifically, and this page does not cite any as if it did.

## The boundary that matters

The tradition's non-grasping counsel has a hard edge it cannot cross: **persistent or distressing visual symptoms — light or images that continue off the cushion, disturb sleep, or interfere with daily life — are a medical matter**, not a meditation matter. The tradition's texts say nothing about neurological or ophthalmological conditions, and neither does this page.

**Not medical advice:** new-onset or worsening visual symptoms warrant professional evaluation regardless of practice context.
