**Direct answer:** "Forgetting the body" in Daoist texts is a technical expression, not a metaphor for carelessness and not a code for dissociation. When Yan Hui says he "drops away the limbs and body" (堕肢体), he is describing the felt body receding from attention as [sitting-and-forgetting](/concepts/zuowang/) completes itself. The word 忘 (forget) is chosen carefully: the Zhuangzi's model of forgetting is the swimmer who forgets the water — something perfectly *there* that has stopped demanding notice.

## The two texts

- **Zhuangzi ch. 6:** "I drop away the limbs and body, expel perception, leave form behind, dismiss knowing, and merge with the Great Thoroughfare — this I call sitting and forgetting." The four phrases are one movement: the body's claim drops, the senses' claim drops, the self's claim drops, and what remains is merger with the Dao.
- **Zhuangzi ch. 19:** the swimmer asked how he survives the gorge answers that he forgot the water — born in it, raised in it, "I begin with what is given and follow it; the water and I become one." Forgetting, in this vocabulary, is *complete fit*, not absence.

## What the tradition does NOT mean

- It does **not** mean the body is evil or to be discarded. Nothing in the passage denigrates the body; the claim that is dropped is the body's claim on *attention*.
- It does **not** mean losing the ability to feel pain or respond to danger. The forgetting is on the cushion, in stillness; the texts presume the practitioner still stands up.
