**Direct answer:** A wandering mind is not a defect in these traditions — it is the documented starting point. The Han *Taiping Jing* assumes the beginner's restlessness and instructs leaving the chamber rather than forcing; Zhu Xi describes the mind as normally "scattered" and prescribes sitting precisely to gather it back. The classical model in *Zhuangzi* ch. 4 is also instructive: the mind's normal operation is "matching" — naming, categorizing, pursuing — and the practice is to drop below that level, not to fight it.

## What the sources say

- **Zhuangzi ch. 4 (xinzhai):** the mind's default is *fu* (符, matching names to things); the instruction "listen with qi" moves attention to the empty level beneath conceptual activity. Wandering thought is what the mind-level produces by default; the practice is a change of register, not a battle.
- **Taiping Jing:** the beginner should practice daily in a private room; "if restless or unwell, come out — do not force it." Restlessness is expected and explicitly de-pathologized.
- **Zhu Xi:** "when the mind has no idle scattered thoughts, it becomes orderly and fluent" — i.e., the scattered mind is the premise of the prescription. His advice to the scattered student was the famous "half day sitting, half day reading."

## The common modern misunderstanding

Popular English-language meditation instruction often frames wandering attention as failure ("the monkey mind you must defeat"). The Chinese sources frame it as the ordinary condition of an uncollected mind — the thing practice works *with* by settling and by lowering the level of attention, not by suppression. Zhu Xi's preferred term for the work is *convergence* (收斂, collecting back), not combat.

## What remains uncertain

- Whether "listening with qi" names a distinct experiential state or is a metaphorical instruction cannot be established from the texts (UNCERTAIN).
- The sources do not quantify how common or how long-lasting wandering is across practitioners; they are prescriptive documents, not surveys.
- No modern empirical study of wandering-thought rates during specifically Daoist or Neo-Confucian sitting practice exists in the literature this site has reviewed.

## Editorial synthesis

If you sit and the mind wanders, the Chinese sources would call that Tuesday. The distinctive Chinese contribution is the *register* model: don't try to stop thought at the mind's own level; settle the body, lower the attention to the empty/subtle register, and let the mind's noise be left behind rather than defeated. That is a structurally different instruction from modern attentional-control models — and one of the reasons these traditions deserve their own knowledge infrastructure.
