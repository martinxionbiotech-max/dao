**Direct answer:** The earliest documented Chinese sitting protocol answers this directly: **stop, do not force.** The Han *Taiping Jing* — the first text to give concrete meditation instructions — says the practitioner should enter the chamber daily, and "if restless or unwell, come out and do not force it." Comfortable settledness is described as something that *accumulates* with practice; it is not something produced by endurance in a single session.

## What the sources say

- **Taiping Jing (Han):** daily practice in a private room; exit on restlessness or discomfort; no forcing. The image-practice it describes is only to be sustained once sitting has become naturally settled.
- **Zhuangzi ch. 4:** the working state is *xu* (emptiness) — the Dao "gathers" where the mind stops imposing. A strained, effortful body-mind is the opposite of the described condition; the text's own vocabulary (empty, awaiting, released) rules out strain as method.
- **Zhu Xi:** lists quiet sitting among practices that *restore* the body and mind from fatigue — a restorative register, not an ascetic one. (His "half day sitting" formula was remedial: collect a scattered mind, then return to study.)

## Why modern practitioners often get this backwards

English-language meditation culture frequently valorizes "sitting through" discomfort as discipline. That norm does not come from these Chinese sources. The classical texts treat restlessness as (a) normal for beginners, (b) an exit signal, and (c) a sign that conditions (life circumstances, health, setting) may need adjusting — which is exactly why Tang zuowang's ladder puts "severing entanglements" and "simplifying affairs" *before* the deep sitting stages.

## Important modern boundary

This page describes what the sources say, and it is not medical advice. Acute physical pain, or sitting practice interacting with mental-health conditions, is a matter for a clinician — the Han text's "leave the room" rule is a tradition's guidance, not a substitute for medical judgment. ## What remains uncertain

- The *Taiping Jing*'s exit rule concerns restlessness and unease, not every conceivable discomfort; applying it to modern practice situations (e.g., mild stiffness vs. sharp pain) is an interpretive extension, labeled as such.
- Whether the Han chamber method reflects one community's practice or a widely shared norm of the period is not established (the text is the only early source of this granularity).

## Editorial synthesis

The Chinese sources are, if anything, *more* conservative than modern popular instruction: the earliest protocol has built-in exit conditions and explicitly forbids forcing. Restlessness is information — usually about unreadiness or unsettled circumstances — not an obstacle to overcome by will. If there is a single transferable principle from these texts, it is: **the practice should settle you; if it is agitating you, the practice is not yet right — stop, adjust conditions, and return.**
