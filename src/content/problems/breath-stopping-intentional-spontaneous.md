**Direct answer:** Spontaneous. The tradition's breath ideal — [taixi](/practices/taixi/) — is described as an *arrival*: breath become so fine it "seems as if present, as if absent," and at its far end, breath without nose or mouth. Nothing in the manuals commands the breath to stop; everything in them warns against forcing. The community archive shows both halves of the rule in action: the stoppage that arrived by itself was described as restful, the stoppage that was chased produced exactly the anxiety treated on the [breath-stopping](/problems/breath-stopping-anxiety/) page.

## The tradition's answer

- **Arrival, not technique:** the Baopuzi says the practitioner who has *attained* taixi breathes as in the womb — the verb is attainment, not performance.
- **The hierarchy of breath:** coarse breath is the fault, fine breath the ideal, and the direction of practice is always *letting* the breath settle, never *making* it stop.

## The archive case

One practitioner's account (kept in the site's [breath-stopping archive](/experiences/notes/exp-008-breath-stopping/)) records the moment the breath "stopped by itself" during deep sitting — and the fear that followed when attention seized on it. The pattern in the account matches the tradition exactly: the event was fine while it was *allowed*; it became frightening the moment it was *grasped*. The archive note is one person's report, not evidence for the tradition's claims.

## The practical rule

1. **Never force the breath.** Not to lengthen it, not to stop it.
2. **If the breath slows on its own, let it.** The tradition calls this the direction of travel.
3. **The sensation of needing air is a signal to breathe.** Full stop. No attainment is worth overriding it.

## The boundary that matters

**Breath discomfort, chest pain, dizziness, or any cardiac or respiratory symptom is a medical matter**, not a meditation matter. The tradition's texts say nothing about respiratory or cardiac conditions, and neither does this page.
