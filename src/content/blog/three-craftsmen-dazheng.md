The nineteenth chapter of the Zhuangzi, *Dasheng* — "Mastering Life" — is the book's workshop. Three of its parables star craftsmen and animals of no pedigree, and between them they hold the most practical account of practice the tradition ever produced. Each is documented separately on this site — the [wooden rooster](/stories/wooden-rooster/), [Artisan Qing](/stories/artisan-qing/), the [cicada-catcher](/stories/cicada-catcher/) — but read together they form a single argument. This page states that argument.

## The three parables, as a system

1. **The wooden rooster** — training by *subtraction*: bluster, reactivity, effort, gone in stages, until nothing external moves the cock and its "virtue is complete" (德全).
2. **Artisan Qing** — preparation by *graded fasting*: three days to drop reward, five to drop evaluation, seven to drop the body; then, and only then, the work — which turns out to be looking, not forcing: the bell-stand is seen in the wood, then released from it.
3. **The cicada-catcher** — training by *progressive accumulation*: five months of stacking balls on a stick, two, three, five, until catching is as easy as picking up; the body a stump, the arm a dead branch, the mind knowing only the wings of a cicada.

## What they jointly claim

**Training is real, and it is slow.** Forty days for a cock, five months of balls for a catcher, seven days of fasting before a single piece of work. Nothing in the chapter is instant; everything is accumulated or removed in stages. The chapter's word for skill is the same word as for ripening.

**There are two engines, not one.** The rooster and Artisan Qing train by *emptying* — removing what stirs. The cicada-catcher trains by *filling* — adding load until attention becomes single. The chapter holds both without reconciling them, because it does not need to: emptying works on what disturbs *within*, filling works on what supports *without*. The later traditions inherited exactly this split — the emptying path became [zuowang](/concepts/zuowang/), the one-pointed path became [shouyi](/concepts/shouyi/) — and the Dazheng chapter shows they were never rivals.

**The endpoint looks dead and is the most alive thing present.** A cock like wood, an artisan without a court, a body like a stump. The chapter's recurring joke — and its claim — is that complete training produces *unremarkable stillness*, and the world can't handle it: other cocks run away, spectators suspect spirits, Confucius stops his walk to point at the hunchback.

**Non-forcing is a consequence of preparation, not a technique.** All three figures *do* something — train, fast, stack — precisely so that at the moment of action they need not force anything. Wuwei, in this chapter, is not passivity; it is the condition reached at the far end of deliberate practice. That is the Dazheng chapter's answer to the modern misreading of "effortless action" as doing nothing: effortless is *expensive*.

## The boundary this site keeps

These are parables — literature, not protocol. The chapter nowhere says its sequences are literal schedules; the seven-day fast is mnemonic, not medical. Whatever modern practitioners take from these texts, they take as *tradition*, with the classical sources cited and the empirical claims unclaimed. The Dazheng chapter is the best description of practice the early canon has; it is not evidence about practice. The difference is this site's whole discipline.
