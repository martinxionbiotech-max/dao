Every page on this site rests on translation, and every translation rests on choices. This editorial states the choices, because a knowledge base that hides its translation policy is hiding its evidence.

## The policy, stated

**1. Technical terms stay romanized when English lacks the word.** *Qi*, *wuwei*, *zuowang*, *dantian*, *ziran* — the site's default is pinyin plus gloss, not a forced English equivalent. The reason is evidentiary, not aesthetic: "vital energy," "non-action," "sitting in oblivion" are *interpretations* wearing the clothes of *translations*. The pinyin term keeps the reader aware that a choice is being made, and lets the same term carry the same reference across 100 pages. Where an established scholarly rendering exists and helps — Kohn's "sitting in oblivion" for zuowang, Watson's prose for the Zhuangzi — it is used *and attributed*.

**2. Recommended renderings are recommendations, stated as such.** *Wuwei* → "non-action" (better than "inaction," which imports passivity); *zuowang* → "sitting in oblivion" (Kohn); 黜聪明 → "dismiss hearing and sight" (not "expel intelligence," which smuggles in a different claim); 心斋 → "fasting of the mind." Each is a *recommendation with reasons*, recorded on the relevant [glossary](/glossary/zuowang/) and [concept](/concepts/wuwei/) pages — not a decree.

**3. Untranslatable sentences are marked untranslatable.** The 吾/我 distinction in [今者吾丧我](/translations/qiwulun-wu-sang-wo/) — two different words for the self in one breath — has no English equivalent; the page says so and shows the tradition's two-word reading instead of pretending the English can carry it. 复命's 命, the 鐻 in [Artisan Qing](/translations/artisan-qing-passage/), the contested 骨/滑 variant — each is flagged with its alternatives rather than silently resolved.

**4. Translation problems are content, not noise.** The site's convention is that a translation page states its difficulties in a closing "translation problems" section. A reader who knows where the text is contested knows more than one who has been given a smooth sentence — because the smooth sentence hides the one place the tradition itself disagrees.

## What translation can and cannot do

What it **can** do: carry the sentence, the image, the argument; preserve the *structure* of a claim (this is a warning, that is a method, that is a question); keep the classical text's own vocabulary stable across the site so that [ch. 6's 堕肢体](/translations/zuowang-passage/) and [ch. 19's 忘吾有四枝形体](/translations/artisan-qing-passage/) visibly rhyme.

What it **cannot** do: settle interpretive disputes; replace the original as evidence (every page links its source text); or transport the *feel* of classical Chinese — the unpunctuated density that lets 致虚极守静笃 stand as a single four-beat unit. For that, the site gives the original characters on every translation page, because the reader who can read them should always be able to check the translator.

## The underlying discipline

This is the same discipline as the rest of the site: **the original text is the evidence; the translation is testimony about the evidence.** Every claim traces to a source, every rendering is a recommendation with reasons, and wherever the tradition disagrees with itself, the disagreement is displayed rather than adjudicated. The words are not the thing. The thing — [zuowang](/concepts/zuowang/), [ziran](/concepts/ziran/), the ten thousand things returning to the root — is what the words point at. A translator's first duty is to keep the pointing honest.
