**Direct answer:** A modern person can take four things from the zuowang tradition, and should decline several more. What transfers: the subtraction logic, the exit conditions, the life-conditions clause, and the register model of attention. What doesn't: the attainment claims, the stage guarantees, and the idea that the classical texts contain a hidden technique. This is an editorial assessment, labeled as such — the sources say what they say; the recommendations below are this site's synthesis.

## What transfers (and why)

### 1. The subtraction logic
The *Daodejing*'s formula — "pursuing the Dao, one subtracts daily" — and the *Qingjing Jing*'s "dispel desire and the mind stills itself" describe a practice of *removing* rather than acquiring. For a modern sitter this is immediately usable: less is added to the mind in sitting; conditions are simplified; the practice is not another thing to achieve. This is the opposite of goal-obsessed self-optimization culture, and the texts' consistent anti-forcing stance (Taiping Jing: leave the room if restless) is safer than much modern instruction.

### 2. The life-conditions clause
Sima Chengzhen put "severing entanglements" and "simplifying affairs" *before* the deep sitting stages. The tradition's position: a scattered life produces a scattered sitting; fix the conditions first. Modern research on stress supports the direction of this claim (chronic demands undermine settled attention), though it does not test the stages as such. The transferable principle is ecological: change the life, not just the technique.

### 3. The register model of attention
Xinzhai's three levels — ear (stimulus), mind (naming and matching), qi (empty receptivity) — offers a genuinely different way to think about what one is doing while sitting: not fighting thoughts at the mind's own level, but settling into a lower, emptier register and letting the mind's noise subside. Whether "qi" has a real referent is beside the point for this use; as a *model*, it changes the instruction from "control attention harder" to "stop feeding the naming level."

### 4. The documents themselves
The primary texts are short, beautiful, and freely available. Reading the actual *Zhuangzi* chs. 4 and 6 beats any secondary paraphrase — including this one.

## What does not transfer

- **The attainment claim.** "Merging with the Great Thoroughfare" is a religious-philosophical claim. No empirical study supports it, and a modern secular practice has no equivalent success criterion. Sitting to "attain the Dao" without the Daoist framework is category confusion.
- **The seven stages as a guarantee.** The *Zuowang Lun*'s ladder is a traditional program, not a validated protocol. Progress through it is claimed by the tradition, not demonstrated by research.
- **"The hidden technique of Zhuangzi."** The classical passages are descriptions and parables. Any modern teacher selling "Zhuangzi's zuowang technique" is adding something the text does not contain — the addition may be fine, but it is theirs, not the book's.

## The safe modern reading

Taken together, the sources actually recommend a conservative practice: sit simply, do not force, stop when agitated, simplify the surrounding life, let settling happen by removal rather than effort, and hold no doctrine about what "should" happen. That is defensible advice with or without the metaphysics — and it is *the texts' own* advice, not a secularization imposed on them.

## Editorial synthesis

The paradox of zuowang for the present: the most useful parts of the tradition are its least mystical parts. What the modern sitter needs from ancient China — subtraction, exit conditions, life conditions, the register model — has been sitting in these texts for two millennia, mostly ignored by a meditation market that prefers to sell attainment. The tradition's deepest gift is its modesty about forcing; the modern world's deepest failure is impatience with exactly that.
