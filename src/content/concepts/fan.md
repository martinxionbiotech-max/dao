**Direct answer:** Fan (反, fǎn) names the Dao's characteristic motion: what reaches an extreme turns back. "Reversal is the movement of the Dao; weakness is the working of the Dao" (反者道之動，弱者道之用, ch. 40). The same character also carries the sense "return" (返) — and the tradition keeps both readings, because in the text they are one movement.

## The term

反 means "to turn back, to reverse, to return." In early texts it is written both 反 and 返, and the two senses — *reversal* (a thing turning into its opposite) and *return* (a thing coming back to its root) — are distinguished by later commentators but deliberately fused in the *Daodejing*. Qian Zhongshu's reading, which holds both senses together, is registered on the [Daodejing 40](/translations/daodejing-40/) page.

## The four passages

1. **The law statement (ch. 40).** "Reversal is the movement of the Dao; weakness is the working of the Dao" (反者道之動，弱者道之用) — the most compressed statement in the book, two clauses, each pairing a motion with its application. See [Daodejing 40](/translations/daodejing-40/).
2. **The geometry of the motion (ch. 25).** "Great means flowing on; flowing on means far-reaching; far-reaching means turning back" (大曰逝，逝曰遠，遠曰反) — the motion of the Dao itself, which reaches its farthest and turns. See [Daodejing 25](/translations/daodejing-25/).
3. **Watching the return (ch. 16).** "The ten thousand things rise together, and I watch their return… Each returns to its root. Returning to the root is called stillness" (萬物並作，吾以觀復…歸根曰靜) — the contemplative application: the practitioner *watches* the return. See [Daodejing 16](/translations/daodejing-16/) and [jing](/concepts/jing/).
4. **Straight words seem reversed (ch. 78).** "Straight words seem reversed" (正言若反) — the book's self-description: its sayings invert common sense, because the movement they describe inverts expectation. See [Daodejing 78](/translations/daodejing-78/).

## The reversal family

The law shows up everywhere in the book, always in pairs:

- Being and non-being give rise to each other (ch. 2, see [wu-you](/concepts/wu-you/))
- "Bent becomes straight; hollow becomes full" (ch. 22)
- "What is to be pulled in is first let out; what is to be weakened is first strengthened" (ch. 36)
- "Misfortune is what fortune leans on" (ch. 58)

## Scholarly interpretation

- **Law or prudence?** Whether 反 is a metaphysical law of nature or a prudential observation about politics and strategy is a live reading question. The book uses it as both: ch. 25 states it cosmologically, ch. 36 applies it tactically.
- **The 反/返 fusion** is itself meaningful: in the *Daodejing* a thing turns back *by* turning into its opposite — the return to the root and the reversal of the extreme are the same arc.

## Practice dimension

- **Why emptying works:** the practitioner's logic runs through 反 — the empty fills, the still moves, the soft wins. See [xu](/concepts/xu/) and [wuwei](/concepts/wuwei/).
- **Watching the return (观复):** ch. 16's verb 观 (to watch) makes the law a contemplative object, not just a fact — the ancestor of the whole inner-observation tradition. See [neiguan](/practices/neiguan/).

## Uncertainty

- Whether ch. 36 is a strategy manual or a warning about how things fall apart is genuinely contested; both readings have long histories and are recorded without adjudication.
- The exact line between the cosmological and prudential senses of 反 cannot be drawn from the text alone.

## Editorial synthesis

Fan is the Dao's signature: the one-directional flows we assume (growth, gain, strength) are each half of an arc that completes itself by turning. It links the cosmology (ch. 25), the method (ch. 16), the ethics of weakness (ch. 40), and the book's own rhetoric (ch. 78) into a single pattern.
