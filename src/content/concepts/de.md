**Direct answer:** De (德, dé) is the way the Dao works *in* a thing — its native potency and integrity. The *Daodejing*'s title pairs the two: the Way (Dao) and its working (De). It is not moral virtue in the narrow modern sense; the book's own ladder makes that explicit.

## The term

德 covers "virtue, power, inner potency, what a thing gets from the Dao." Translation is a known trap: "virtue" moralizes, Waley's "power" suggests coercion, "integrity" is closer but partial. Practice on this site follows the [glossary entry](/glossary/de/): keep romanized in technical contexts, gloss as "virtue/power" on first use.

## What the texts claim

1. **Two kinds of virtue (ch. 38).** "The highest De does not display itself as De, and therefore has De; the lower De never lets go of De, and therefore lacks De. The highest De does not act, and has no ulterior motive" (上德不德，是以有德；下德不失德，是以無德。上德無為而無以為). Then the ladder of decline: after De comes benevolence (仁), then righteousness (義), then ritual (禮) — "ritual is the thinning of loyalty and trust, and the beginning of disorder" (夫禮者，忠信之薄，而亂之首).
2. **The four-agent pipeline (ch. 51).** "The Dao gives birth, De nurtures, matter shapes, circumstances complete" (道生之，德畜之，物形之，勢成之) — each stage its own agent, none of them coercive. The chapter ends with the recurring formula: "gives birth but does not possess, acts but does not rely, leads but does not lord over — this is called the mysterious De" (生而不有，為而不恃，長而不宰，是謂玄德). See [Daodejing 51](/translations/daodejing-51/), which also registers the two received variants of the second line (Wang Bi's 亭之毒之 vs. Heshang Gong's 成之熟之).

## Historical development

- **Pre-Daoist 德:** in Zhou texts, the charisma or moral force of a ruler (the "virtue" that heaven rewards).
- **Wang Bi (226–249)** glosses the term through its homophone: 德者，得也 — "De is what is obtained": what a thing gets from the Dao. His note on ch. 38 reads the whole chapter as a ranking of ways of obtaining. See [Wang Bi](/people/wang-bi/).
- **玄德 (mysterious De):** the formula of producing-without-possessing recurs across chs. 2, 10, 51, 65; it becomes the standard Daoist description of the sage's and the practitioner's relationship to outcomes.

## Scholarly interpretation

- The ch. 38 ladder is the earliest sustained Chinese account of moral decline by stages — and the clearest evidence that Daoist De is not "being good" but something prior to goodness: the lower De that *insists* on being virtuous is already a step down the ladder.
- The pairing 道德 (dao-de) became the name of the book and of the whole tradition — "the teaching of the Way and its working."

## Practice dimension

- **Non-possessive agency:** 玄德 describes how practice should relate to its results — cultivating without clinging to the cultivation. See [wuwei](/concepts/wuwei/).
- **抱朴 (holding to the uncarved block)** is the practical twin of De: the unworked original as the state in which the Dao's working is unobstructed. See [pu](/concepts/pu/).

## Uncertainty

- Whether ch. 38 is anti-Confucian polemic (a direct attack on ren/yi/li) or simply a redefinition of 德 within its own system remains contested; the chapter reads coherently either way.
- How much of the "mysterious De" formula is genuine Laozi and how much is later editorial stitching across chapters is an open textual question.

## Editorial synthesis

De is the answer to "what does the Dao look like once it has entered a particular thing?" — the same pattern in each vessel, without coercion, without display, and without clinging to itself.
