**Direct answer:** Jingzuo (静坐, "quiet sitting") is the seated contemplative practice of the Song–Ming Neo-Confucian tradition, made famous by Zhu Xi's (1130–1200) advice recorded in the *Zhuzi Yulei*: "spend half the day in quiet sitting and half the day in reading" (半日靜坐，半日讀書). It descends from a longer Chinese history of seated absorption (Zuowang, Chan zuochan) but is distinguished by its Confucian rationale: sitting to collect and stabilize the mind so that one can investigate principle and cultivate moral seriousness.

## Documented sources

- **Zhu Xi, Zhuzi Yulei, juan 116** (recorded conversation with the departing student Guo Deyuan): "If a person... uses half the day for quiet sitting and half the day for reading, after a year or two like this, what worry is there about lack of progress?" The context is explicitly remedial — Guo was scattered in his studies, and the prescription is for collecting the mind.
- **Er Cheng waishu, juan 12**: "Yichuan [Cheng Yi] would sigh that anyone he saw doing quiet sitting was good at learning" (伊川每見人靜坐便歎其善學) — quiet sitting valued as a learning practice.
- **Song shi, "Biography of Yang Shi"**: the "Cheng's gate, standing in snow" story records Cheng Yi sitting with eyes closed while the students Yang Shi and You Zuo waited outside — evidence that seated contemplative practice was part of the Northern Song Confucian teacher's routine.
- **Zhu Xi's qualification:** the same tradition records him distinguishing Confucian quiet sitting from Buddhist meditation: he preferred Cheng Yi's *jing* (敬, reverence/seriousness) to Zhou Dunyi's *jing* (靜, stillness) precisely to mark the difference from Chan. In his usage, quiet sitting serves study and moral cultivation, not release from the world.

## What the practice was for (Neo-Confucian frame)

| Purpose | Source emphasis |
|---|---|
| Collecting a scattered mind | Zhuzi Yulei: the mind must be settled before principle can be grasped |
| Experiencing the "un-aroused" (未發) state | Li Tong taught Zhu Xi to sit and seek the mind before feelings arise |
| Recovering from fatigue, restoring memory | Zhu Xi: sitting restores the body and mind |
| Preparing for study and moral action | balance with reading; sitting alone was never the whole program |

## Antecedents and neighbors

- **Zhuangzi's zuowang** supplied the classical vocabulary of sitting and forgetting (ch. 6), and xinzhai the emptying of the mind (ch. 4).
- **Chan Buddhism's zuochan** (坐禅) was the contemporary seated practice the Neo-Confucians both borrowed from and defined themselves against; Zhu Xi's youth included Chan study, and his mature position was formulated in explicit contrast.
- **Daoist shouyi** (守一) from the Han Taiping Jing onward was the other major lineage of seated inner cultivation.

## Misunderstandings to avoid

1. **"Half day sitting, half day reading" was a universal curriculum.** It was a prescription for one student's specific weakness, recorded in a conversation — not a general rule for all learners. Ming critic Yan Yuan attacked it as "half a day being a monk," but the target of his attack was a caricature.
2. **Quiet sitting = Buddhist meditation under another name.** The Neo-Confucians themselves drew this distinction insistently: sitting cultivates seriousness and prepares for study and action; it does not aim at detachment or altered states as its goal.
3. **The tradition had a single standard technique.** Zhu Xi gave almost no posture or method instructions; later figures (Gao Panlong in the Ming) systematized the practice, but early sources emphasize simplicity: sit, be still, don't force thoughts.

## Modern research

Empirical research specific to Neo-Confucian jingzuo is extremely scarce. Studies of sitting meditation generally (often Buddhist-derived practices) show attention-related and stress-related effects (see the [Goyal 2014 meta-analysis](/research/mindfulness-meta-analysis-2014/)), but generalizing those findings to the historical jingzuo tradition is an interpretive leap this site does not make; the meditation-harms literature (see the [Farias 2020 review](/research/farias-adverse-events-2020/)) likewise concerns other traditions and says nothing about jingzuo itself. The historical claim stands on its own register: jingzuo is one of the main vehicles through which seated contemplative practice entered the Confucian mainstream of East Asia.

## Editorial synthesis

Jingzuo is the term that connects classical Daoist absorption (zuowang) to the mainstream scholar-official culture of later China — and through Zhu Xi's curriculum, to East Asian education generally. For the modern knowledge map it is the key bridge node: Chinese contemplative practice survived in the "respectable" Confucian world precisely by being reframed as preparation for study and conduct, while its Daoist and Buddhist relatives kept the absorptive and release-oriented aims.

**Uncertainty is preserved:** the degree to which Song jingzuo practice was directly continuous with earlier Daoist methods, versus a parallel development borrowing Buddhist forms, is debated in scholarship and cannot be settled from the surviving sources.

The mid-Tang precursor usually credited for the quiet-sitting revival is [Li Ao (李翱)](/people/li-ao/).
