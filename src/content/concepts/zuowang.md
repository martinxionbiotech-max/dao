**Direct answer:** Zuowang (坐忘, "sitting in oblivion" / "sitting in forgetfulness") is the state described in the *Zhuangzi* in which a person "drops away the limbs and body, expels hearing and sight, leaves the form and departs from knowledge," becoming "identical with the Great Thoroughfare." It is the best-known classical Chinese description of an absorptive contemplative state, and it became the name of an organized Daoist meditation system in the Tang dynasty.

## What the source says

The only passage in the *Zhuangzi* that uses the term is a dialogue placed in the mouth of Yan Hui and Confucius (*Zhuangzi*, ch. 6, "Da Zongshi"):

> 顏回曰：「回益矣。」仲尼曰：「何謂也？」曰：「回忘仁義矣。」曰：「可矣，猶未也。」
> 他日復見，曰：「回益矣。」曰：「何謂也？」曰：「回忘禮樂矣。」曰：「可矣，猶未也。」
> 他日復見，曰：「回益矣。」曰：「何謂也？」曰：「回坐忘矣。」仲尼蹵然曰：「何謂坐忘？」
> 顏回曰：「墮肢體，黜聰明，離形去知，同於大通，此謂坐忘。」
> 仲尼曰：「同則無好也，化則無常也。而果其賢乎！丘也請從而後也。」

Literal rendering of the key lines:

> Yan Hui said: "I drop away my limbs and body, dismiss my hearing and sight, leave my form, depart from knowledge, and merge with the Great Thoroughfare (*datong*). This is what I call sitting in oblivion." Confucius said: "Merged with it, one has no preferences; transformed with it, one has no constancy. You are indeed worthy! May I ask to follow behind you?"

## Why the framing matters

The passage is a deliberate inversion: the model Confucian disciple Yan Hui progresses by *forgetting* ren and li (benevolence and ritual), the core Confucian virtues, and the Confucius figure approves. Commentators widely read this as the *Zhuangzi* using fictional dialogues to make its own point (the historical accuracy of the conversation is not claimed by anyone; the chapter is an anthology of parables). The progression is also explicit about stages: forgetting social virtues first, then embodied ritual practice, then finally the body and cognition themselves.

## Historical development

- **Warring States (4th–3rd c. BCE):** the term appears once, in the *Zhuangzi* inner chapter quoted above. Nothing in the text gives seated posture instructions; the "sitting" is conventional for absorption practice of the period, and the passage is a description of a state, not a technique manual.
- **Han–Six Dynasties:** early Daoist communities developed adjacent practices — *shouyi* (guarding the One) in the *Taiping Jing*, visualization (*cun*) methods in Shangqing texts — but the word *zuowang* itself did not become a technical system in this period.
- **Tang (7th–8th c.):** Sima Chengzhen (司马承祯, 647–735), the Shangqing patriarch who was honored at the Tang court, wrote the *Zuowang lun* (坐忘论, Treatise on Sitting in Oblivion), which organizes the practice into seven successive stages: 敬信 (reverent faith), 断缘 (severing conditions), 收心 (collecting the mind), 简事 (simplifying affairs), 真观 (true observation), 泰定 (profound stability), 得道 (attaining the Dao). This turned a classical phrase into a structured religious program, explicitly borrowing Buddhist contemplative vocabulary (e.g., *guan*/observation) while framing the goal in Daoist terms.
- **Song and later:** the *Daoshu* (道枢, Pivot of the Dao) compiled by Zeng Zao in the Song period contains a "Zuowang" chapter treating the practice as a basis for longevity cultivation; later texts continue to cite it as a classical method.

## Interpretive disagreements

1. **What exactly is forgotten?** The literal reading is progressive loss: social virtues → ritual form → body awareness → cognitive activity. Some scholars stress the cognitive dimension (letting go of conceptual knowledge); others stress the somatic dimension (dissolution of body-schema awareness). The text includes both.
2. **Is this an attainment or a method?** The *Zhuangzi* passage describes an attained state; Sima Chengzhen's treatise converts it into a graduated method. Most English scholarship (Kohn 2010) treats both as legitimate and historically continuous.
3. **Is "sitting" literal?** The Chinese *zuo* may function as "in a seated posture" or simply "while still." Traditional sources generally assume seated practice, but the *Zhuangzi* itself does not specify posture.

## What modern research does and does not show

Empirical studies specifically on zuowang as such are essentially absent from the scientific literature. The adjacent field of research on meditation more broadly reports physiological and attentional changes associated with sustained sitting practice, but those studies overwhelmingly concern other traditions (Buddhist mindfulness, Transcendental Meditation, etc.). **This page makes no claim that modern research validates the traditional account of zuowang**; the traditional claims belong to the religious and philosophical register and are labeled as such.

## Editorial synthesis

Zuowang is best understood as a *classical description of absorption* that a later religious tradition converted into a *method*. Its value for a modern knowledge system is threefold: it is the earliest Chinese literary description of a non-dual contemplative state; it shows a documented historical pattern of Buddhist–Daoist technique borrowing in the Tang; and it anchors a family of Chinese terms (xinzhai, shouyi, jingzuo) that modern English literature often collapses into "meditation" without distinction.

**Uncertainty is preserved:** whether the Warring States description refers to the same experience as the Tang seven-stage system cannot be established from surviving texts; the connection is a traditional and scholarly reconstruction, not a documented chain of transmission.
