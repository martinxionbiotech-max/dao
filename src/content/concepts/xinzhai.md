**Direct answer:** Xinzhai (心斋, "fasting of the mind" or "fasting of the heart-mind") is a Zhuangzian contemplative instruction: do not listen with the ear, or with the mind, but with *qi*; keep the mind empty, and the Dao will gather in it. It is the other major classical description of inward practice alongside zuowang.

## Original passage

From *Zhuangzi*, ch. 4 ("Ren Jian Shi"), again in a fictional dialogue between Yan Hui and Confucius. Yan Hui, preparing to remonstrate with a dangerous ruler, has tried every rhetorical strategy and failed; Confucius then teaches him "the fasting of the mind":

> 仲尼曰：「若一志，無聽之以耳而聽之以心，無聽之以心而聽之以氣。聽止於耳，心止於符。氣也者，虛而待物者也。唯道集虛。虛者，心齋也。」

Literal rendering:

> Confucius said: "Unify your intention. Do not listen with the ear but listen with the mind; do not listen with the mind but listen with qi. Listening stops at the ear; the mind stops at matching [names to things]. As for qi, it is empty and awaits things. Only the Dao gathers in emptiness. Emptiness — that is the fasting of the mind."

The passage continues with Yan Hui's report that, entering this state, "there was no longer any Hui" (未始有回也) — a self-loss formulation parallel to zuowang.

## Context

The framing is practical, not merely mystical: the dialogue concerns how to speak to power without being destroyed. The proposed answer is that one must first empty one's own agenda, so that one's words arise from the situation rather than from self-assertion. This sets xinzhai apart from Buddhist-influenced later practice: in its original context it is as much about action in the world as about withdrawal.

## Key distinctions in the text

The instruction builds a three-tier hierarchy:

| Level | Instrument | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Ear | physical hearing | stops at sound |
| Mind (*xin*) | conceptual matching | stops at names and categories (*fu*, 符) |
| Qi | empty receptivity | awaits things; the Dao gathers in it |

The contrast drawn between ritual fasting (abstaining from wine and meat — what Confucius calls "the fasting of sacrifice") and the fasting of the mind is explicit in the text: the former purifies the body's intake; the latter empties the mind's fixation.

## Interpretation disputes

1. **What is "listening with qi"?** Traditional Chinese commentators and modern scholars differ: some take qi as a vital substrate of perception below conscious thought; others take the line as metaphor for non-conceptual receptivity. No consensus exists; the text does not define qi here.
2. **Xinzhai vs. zuowang:** both are *Zhuangzi* parables using Yan Hui, both describe loss of the ordinary self. Scholarship treats them as two angles — emptying (xinzhai) vs. forgetting (zuowang) — of the same ideal of *xu* (emptiness/vacuity). The family resemblance is noted in both classical and modern commentary.
3. **Political reading vs. contemplative reading:** some interpreters stress the parable's political frame (how to survive and improve a bad ruler's court); others read it purely as meditation instruction. Both readings are defensible from the text.

## What it is not

- It is not a breathing technique: the text says nothing about breath manipulation.
- It is not "mindfulness" in the modern sense of sustained attention to present experience: the instruction aims at emptying conceptual matching, not strengthening it.
- It is not a description of a trance: Yan Hui is presented as returning to ordinary circumstances, changed in his stance toward them.

## Editorial synthesis

Xinzhai matters because it supplies the earliest Chinese account of a *methodology of emptying*: a graded instruction (ear → mind → qi) with an explicit failure mode for each stage. For a modern reader it explains why later Chinese traditions use qi-language in contemplative contexts, and why the *Zhuangzi* — unlike later meditation manuals — ties the practice to speaking and acting well in the world rather than to withdrawal from it.

**Uncertainty is preserved:** the exact experiential referent of "listening with qi" cannot be recovered from the text, and modern reconstructions differ.
