**Direct answer:** Pu (朴, pǔ) is the *Daodejing*'s image for the unworked original: uncarved wood, the block before it is split into tools. It names the state the practitioner returns to — and the book itself warns that the block, once split, becomes a thousand utensils (ch. 28).

## The term

朴 is unworked timber — the whole block, nothing yet carved out of it. Its pair in ch. 19 is 素 (undyed silk): "Show the plain silk, hold to the uncarved block" (見素抱樸). Both images say the same thing: the state before shaping, when no possibility has been chosen and none has been lost.

## The four passages

1. **The pairing with 素 (ch. 19).** "Show the plain silk, hold to the uncarved block, lessen selfishness, reduce desire" (見素抱樸，少私寡欲) — the block as what the sage holds *to*, against the claims of learning and profit. The received text is quoted from the [Daodejing](/texts/daodejing/) editions collated in the sources.
2. **The return and the warning (ch. 28).** "Return to the uncarved block. The block scattered becomes utensils; the sage uses it and becomes chief of the officials — the great cutting does not cut" (復歸於樸。樸散則為器，聖人用之則為官長。故大制不割) — the block is the state *before* the world of roles and tools, and the sage's art is governing from that state instead of adding more cuts.
3. **The nameless block (ch. 32).** "The Dao is ever nameless — the uncarved block" (道常無名，樸) — the block is what the Dao looks like: unshaped, unclaimed. See [Daodejing 32](/translations/daodejing-32/), which registers the punctuation dispute over whether 樸 belongs to ch. 32's close or opens ch. 37.
4. **The nameless block again (ch. 37).** "The Dao is ever without action… When desires arise, I press them down with the nameless block" (吾將鎮之以無名之樸) — the block as the *instrument* of restraint: not a rule but the unshaped state itself, applied against desire. See [Daodejing 37](/translations/daodejing-37/).
5. **The portrait of the adept (ch. 15).** "Uncarved like a block of wood" (敦兮其若樸) — the chapter's sevenfold description of the ancient master ends on the block as the image of the whole demeanor.

## Historical development

- **The name's career:** the image becomes a personal name — Ge Hong's *Baopuzi* (抱朴子, "Master Who Embraces the Uncarved Block," 4th c.) takes the term as a title, and "return to the block" (返樸歸真) becomes the standard phrase for the goal of self-cultivation across later Daoism.
- **Political reading:** ch. 28's "great cutting" (大制) ties the block to rulership — the text explicitly applies its own image to governing, not only to private practice.

## Scholarly interpretation

- **Simplicity vs. primitivism:** whether 朴 means "go back to a primitive society" or "keep the unshaped core within civilization" is a live debate; ch. 28's pairing of the block with *being chief of officials* points toward the second reading — the block is what the sage governs *from*.
- **The block and ziran:** 朴 is often read as the human version of [ziran](/concepts/ziran/): what is so-of-itself looks, in a person, like uncarved wood.

## Practice dimension

- **Holding to the block (抱朴):** the contemplative counterpart of 抱一 — keeping the unshaped state against the pull of roles, names, and outcomes. See [shouyi](/practices/shouyi/).
- **The block as restraint:** ch. 37's 鎮之以無名之樸 gives practice its non-violent instrument — desires are not fought but settled by returning to the unshaped state.

## Uncertainty

- Whether 樸 in chs. 32/37 is a *name* for the Dao or a *state* one returns to is itself the punctuation question the tradition never settled; both readings are registered on the [Daodejing 32](/translations/daodejing-32/) page.
- How much of ch. 28's political application is original and how much is editorial layering remains textually uncertain.

## Editorial synthesis

Pu is the site's image for the *original*: the state before the cuts, available as both goal (return to it) and instrument (settle desires with it). It links the cosmology (the Dao is nameless like the block) to the ethics (show plain silk, hold the block) and the method (the block as restraint).
