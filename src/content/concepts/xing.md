**Direct answer:** Xing (性) is the nature a thing is born with — what it is *by itself*, before cultivation adds or society imposes. In the Zhuangzi it anchors the argument that the inborn is not a defect to be corrected: the duck's short legs and the crane's long ones are both whole as they are. In later internal alchemy, xing becomes one half of the central pair **xingming** (性命, "nature and life") — the inside of the person (mind, nature) paired with the outside (body, vitality), both to be cultivated together.

## What the sources say

- **The Zhuangzi's defense of the inborn:** chapter 8's webbed-toes argument is a defense of 性 — the inborn constitution is not a flaw to be surgically corrected; what must be corrected is the *imposition* on it.
- **The pair xingming:** in Quanzhen and later alchemical usage, 性 (nature) is the mind's inborn character and 命 (life/vitality) is the body's allotted force — and the tradition's characteristic claim is that the two are cultivated *together* (xingming shuangxiu, "joint cultivation of nature and life"), not one at the expense of the other.

## The distinction that matters

- **Xing vs. de:** [de](/concepts/de/) is the nature's *power and character in action* — what the inborn nature becomes as it expresses itself. Xing is the seed; de is what the seed does. The two are close enough that commentaries often gloss each by the other.
- **Xing vs. ziran:** [ziran](/concepts/ziran/) ("so-of-itself") is the *spontaneity* of the inborn nature; xing is the nature itself. A thing's xing acts ziran — the terms are the noun and the adverb of the same claim.

## The practice connection

In sitting practice, xing enters through the tradition's claim that stillness lets the inborn nature *surface* — the imposed layers settle, and what remains is what was always there. The [zuowang](/concepts/zuowang/) page's "forgetting" is, in this vocabulary, the removal of what was imposed on xing.

The companion half of the pair is [ming](/concepts/ming/).

## What is uncertain

The xingming pair is solid in the alchemical tradition from roughly the Song onward, but the earlier history is looser: pre-Song texts use xing for "inborn nature" without a fixed counterpart, and how much the alchemical pair owes to Neo-Confucian usage (Zhang Zai, the Cheng brothers) rather than to Daoist sources alone is a matter of scholarly discussion. The Mencian claim that human nature tends toward the good is a Confucian claim, not a Daoist one, and this site does not import it.
