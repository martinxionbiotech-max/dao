**Direct answer:** Yi (意) is the mind's *directedness* — intention in the ordinary English sense, the aiming of attention. The term matters for practice because the manuals locate the work of sitting in exactly this faculty: not in stopping thought (which belongs to the mind at large), but in *intending* — where attention is aimed and how long it stays. The breath work's instruction "guard the intention at the breath" is a yi instruction.

## The term's place

- **Yi vs. xin:** [xin](/glossary/xin/) (心, the heart-mind) is the whole mental field — thoughts, feelings, the lot. Yi is its *aiming*: the directed edge of the mind. The manuals' recurrent counsel is to let the field settle while keeping the aim.
- **Yi vs. shen:** [shen](/glossary/shen/) (神, spirit) is the mind's *clarity*; yi is its *motion*. The practice economy runs on all three: the heart-mind settles, intention steadies, and spirit clarifies.

## What the practice manuals do with it

- **The breath work:** the [tiaoxi](/glossary/tiaoxi/) analysis is a yi discipline — the instruction to *follow* the breath is an instruction about where intention rests, and the faults (wind, panting, coarse) are named failures of the intention's steadiness.
- **Neiguan:** the [Neiguan jing](/practices/neiguan/)'s "contemplate within, clarify the mind" is the same faculty turned inward — intention aimed at the interior, steadily, without grasping.

## The boundary that matters

- **Yi is not a force.** The manuals aim intention, then let go — the tradition's word for aiming-with-grip is exactly the error the [wuwei](/concepts/wuwei/) page treats. Intention in sitting is the quiet holding of a direction, not the gripping of a target.
- **The term is shared across schools** — Confucian and Buddhist manuals use 意 in their own ways, and this page's treatment follows the Daoist-facing usage of the practice manuals cited.

The term layer lives at [yi in the glossary](/glossary/yi/) alongside its family: [xin](/glossary/xin/), [shen](/glossary/shen/).
