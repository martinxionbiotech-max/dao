**Direct answer:** The *Zhuangzi* is a 33-chapter anthology of Warring States philosophical writing, traditionally attributed to Master Zhuang (Zhuang Zhou), containing the classical descriptions of zuowang and xinzhai that anchor Chinese contemplative language. The received text is not a single-author book: the first seven chapters (Inner Chapters) are traditionally attributed to Zhuang Zhou himself, while the Outer and Miscellaneous chapters are from related but not identical hands and periods.

## Structure

| Section | Chapters | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Inner Chapters (内篇) | 1–7 | Traditional core attributed to Zhuang Zhou |
| Outer Chapters (外篇) | 8–22 | Mixed authorship; includes the "school" material |
| Miscellaneous Chapters (杂篇) | 23–33 | Latest strata; includes the final chapter surveying schools |

The chapter division and the 33-chapter form descend from Guo Xiang (郭象, d. 312), whose commentary fixed the received text and is the earliest surviving full interpretation. The modern understanding of the book as an *anthology of several strands* of early Daoist thought is standard in scholarship (see IEP).

## Zhuang Zhou

Traditionally a minor official and near-contemporary of Mencius (4th c. BCE), known through anecdotes in the text itself and the Shiji biography — both of which scholarship treats with caution. The tradition's portrait: a figure who declined office, valued the useless tree (survival by non-prominence), and answered grief with a song and a pot (ch. 18, the death of his wife). Whether any given anecdote is biographical or parabolic is unverifiable.

## Why this book matters for contemplation

The *Zhuangzi* supplies the classical descriptions that every later Chinese sitting tradition quotes or adapts:

- **Zuowang** (ch. 6): dropping body, senses, and knowledge to merge with the Great Thoroughfare — the root text of Tang zuowang practice.
- **Xinzhai** (ch. 4): listening with qi, the emptiness where the Dao gathers — the root text of "empty-mind" practice language.
- **"The human's life is a gathering of qi"** (ch. 22): the cosmological basis of qi-language in later meditation theory.
- **The fasting/cultivation theme:** self-emptying as the general condition of skill, freedom, and survival (the knife of Cook Ding in ch. 3, the useless tree).

## Reception

- **Wei–Jin:** Guo Xiang's commentary made the book central to the "Dark Learning" (xuanxue) of the aristocracy.
- **Buddhism:** the book supplied the native vocabulary Chinese Buddhists used to translate and explain absorption (the *geyi* period).
- **Tang Daoism:** Sima Chengzhen's *Zuowang lun* built a religious method on the ch. 6 passage.
- **Song Confucianism:** the vocabulary of stillness and emptiness enters the jingzuo tradition partly through the *Zhuangzi*.
- **Modern translations:** Watson (1968) is the standard complete English translation; Ziporyn and Lynn (through Guo Xiang) offer competing interpretive lenses.

## Editorial synthesis

The *Zhuangzi* is the root text of this knowledge base not because it is a meditation manual — it is not — but because it is the earliest Chinese literature describing *what it is like* for the self to fall away in sitting, and because every later tradition's technical vocabulary (emptiness, qi, forgetting, merging) borrows from it. Reading its practice passages as literal technique is anachronistic; reading them as the description-and-ideal later technicians systematized is exactly right.
