**Direct answer:** The *Daodejing* (道德经, "Classic of the Way and Virtue") is an 81-chapter collection of aphoristic verse, traditionally attributed to Laozi (Old Master) of the 6th century BCE, and historically the founding text of Daoism. It is the source of wuwei, of "embracing the One," and of the subtraction program ("pursuing the Dao, one subtracts daily") that the contemplative tradition built on. Its earliest physical witnesses — the Guodian bamboo slips (pre-300 BCE) and the Mawangdui silk manuscripts (buried 168 BCE) — confirm the text's antiquity while showing it circulated in different arrangements.

## The book

- **Form:** ~5,000 characters of verse and aphorism in 81 chapters (traditional division); the division into chapters is a later editorial feature, and the two halves are the Dao jing (道经, chs. 1–37) and De jing (德经, chs. 38–81).
- **Traditional attribution:** Laozi, a figure of whom nothing historically reliable is known; the traditional biography (Shiji ch. 63) is itself legendary. Authorship is therefore classed UNCERTAIN.
- **Content:** the Dao (Way) as the unnameable source and pattern of things; De (virtue/power) as its working in the world; and the practical ideals of wuwei, simplicity, yielding, and self-emptying.

## Manuscript history (why editions differ)

| Witness | Date | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Guodian slips | buried before 300 BCE | oldest; ~2,000 characters corresponding to parts of the received text; lacks chs. 67–81; arranged differently |
| Mawangdui silk texts | buried 168 BCE | two complete-ish copies; the De jing precedes the Dao jing — i.e., in these copies the received "second half" comes first |
| Received text | fixed by the Han | Wang Bi (226–249) commentary became the standard edition used today |

Implication for citations: chapter numbers refer to the received arrangement; the same passages exist with variants in the older witnesses. Scholarship on wuwei (e.g., Slingerland 2003) and on the Guodian texts (Cook 2012) documents the variants.

## The contemplative core

Three passages ground the later meditation tradition:

1. **Ch. 48 — the subtraction program:** "Pursuing learning, one adds daily; pursuing the Dao, one subtracts daily; subtracting again and again until one reaches non-action" (為學日益，為道日損。損之又損，以至於無為). This is the classical warrant for emptying-practice.
2. **Ch. 10 — embracing the One:** "Carrying the soul and embracing the One, can you let them not separate? Concentrating the qi to suppleness, can you become like an infant?" (載營魄抱一，能無離乎？專氣致柔，能如嬰兒乎？) — the root of shouyi and of breath-subtlety practice.
3. **Ch. 16 — emptiness and stillness:** "Attain utmost emptiness, guard profound stillness... returning to the root is called stillness" (致虛極，守靜篤...歸根曰靜) — the canonical formulation of the empty-still ideal that Daoist sitting practice claims as its charter.

## What the book is not

- Not a meditation manual: no posture, schedule, or technique instructions.
- Not a single-author treatise: the earliest manuscripts already suggest a compiled, evolving text.
- Not "mystical" in a sense that dissolves into vagueness: much of the book is hard-headed practical-political advice on ruling, war, and survival, using the same vocabulary as the contemplative passages.

## Reception

- **Han Huang-Lao:** read as statecraft; "non-action" becomes the ruler's art of delegating.
- **Religious Daoism:** Laozi deified; the text becomes scripture (from the Han onward).
- **Buddhist translation:** its vocabulary (Dao, emptiness, non-action) supplied the early *geyi* bridge vocabulary.
- **Modernity:** the most translated Chinese classic; the "mystical" reading popular in the West since the 19th century is one reading among many, not the original frame.

## Editorial synthesis

The *Daodejing* and the *Zhuangzi* divide the labor of this knowledge base: the *Daodejing* supplies the *program* (subtract, empty, still, return) and the cosmology (the One, qi, the Dao); the *Zhuangzi* supplies the *phenomenology* (what the emptied state is described as). Every later Chinese sitting tradition — Daoist shouyi and zuowang, Chan, Neo-Confucian jingzuo — quotes or adapts these two books. That is why the site treats them as the root texts and cites chapter-and-verse rather than paraphrase.
