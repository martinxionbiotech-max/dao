**Direct answer:** The *Zuowang Lun* (坐忘论, "Treatise on Sitting in Oblivion"), by the Tang Shangqing patriarch Sima Chengzhen (647–735), is the foundational systematic treatise of Daoist seated meditation. It takes the classical *Zhuangzi* state description of zuowang and organizes it into seven successive stages — 敬信 (reverent faith), 断缘 (severing conditions), 收心 (collecting the mind), 简事 (simplifying affairs), 真观 (true observation), 泰定 (profound stability), 得道 (attaining the Dao) — a complete contemplative program from disposition through method to goal.

## The seven stages

| Stage | Chinese | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 敬信 | Confidence and respect for the practice as the precondition of progress |
| 2 | 断缘 | Cutting back entanglements; reducing the social busyness that scatters the mind |
| 3 | 收心 | Collecting the mind — the core practice: withdrawing the mind from outer pursuit |
| 4 | 简事 | Simplifying affairs; keeping only necessary commitments |
| 5 | 真观 | True observation — seeing things without projective attachment; the contemplative insight stage |
| 6 | 泰定 | Profound stability — settled absorption beyond disturbance |
| 7 | 得道 | Attaining the Dao — described as the natural issue of the preceding work |

Two structural facts stand out. First, the ladder opens with *disposition and life-conditions* (faith, simplification) before any technique — the treatise treats meditation as embedded in a way of life. Second, the ladder ends not in an experience but in a *way of being* (attaining the Dao), which the text immediately qualifies: "Though it is called attaining the Dao, in reality nothing is attained" (雖名得道，實無所得) — the attainment is described in emptying terms, not acquisition terms.

## Sources and influences

Sima Chengzhen drew on the *Zhuangzi* for the name and the end state, on the *Neiguan Jing* and *Qingjing Jing* for the vocabulary of inward observation and emptying (see the [neiguan-jing](/texts/neiguan-jing/) page), and on Buddhist contemplative vocabulary (*guan*, observation) for the middle stages — while keeping the goal Daoist. The *Zuowang Lun* is thus a documented synthesis point in the history this knowledge base maps.

## Textual history

- The treatise circulated in Tang Daoist circles and entered the canon; the Song *Daoshu* includes a "Zuowang" chapter continuing the theme.
- Modern critical study: Kohn's *Sitting in Oblivion* (2010) is the standard English treatment, with translation and historical analysis.
- The *Daoshu* records the tradition's own equation: inner observation (neiguan) *is* zuowang — confirming the practice family's continuity.

## What the treatise does not contain

- No detailed posture instructions (the tradition's sitting was apparently taken as conventional).
- No breathing-counting protocol — unlike some later inner-alchemy manuals.
- No claim that the stages can be skipped or that results come quickly; the ladder is explicitly progressive.

## Editorial synthesis

The *Zuowang Lun* is the pivot document of Chinese contemplative history: before it, zuowang was a famous passage; after it, zuowang is a named, teachable system. Every later Daoist sitting tradition, and much of the Neo-Confucian jingzuo vocabulary, stands downstream of this treatise. Its most modern feature is its honesty about preconditions: the text insists that mind-collection cannot succeed while one's life remains over-entangled — a claim that survives as practical counsel even for secular readers.

**Uncertainty is preserved:** the extent of direct dependence on specific Buddhist texts (versus general shared vocabulary) remains a scholarly question; the treatise itself cites no Buddhist sources by name.
