## What the book is

The *Liezi* is the great fable treasury of the Daoist canon: eight chapters of stories — the Foolish Old Man Who Moved the Mountain, the Man of Qi Who Feared the Sky Would Fall, the two children arguing over the distance of the sun, the story of the musician's teacher, and many more — gathered under the name of Lie Yukou, the "Master Lie" whom the *Zhuangzi* itself already describes as a legendary figure ("Liezi rode the wind, so lightly and skillfully, and returned after fifteen days"). Tang Daoism canonized it as the *Chongxu zhenjing* ("True Scripture of the Empty Void"), one of the four classics alongside the [Daodejing](/texts/daodejing/), the [Zhuangzi](/texts/zhuangzi/), and the Wenzi.

For this site's purposes, one chapter matters most: the received text opens with **Tianrui ("Heaven's Gifts")** — the cosmology of 太易/太初/太始/太素, the four pre-world stages, "before form and before matter" — which became standard vocabulary for the *xu* family of meditation concepts, and the second chapter, Huangdi, preserves the oldest version of some famous sitting and transformation material.

## The authorship controversy, stated with both sides

No book in the tradition has a sharper gap between its traditional attribution and modern scholarship, and this page keeps both positions in full:

- **The traditional position:** the *Hanshu* bibliography recorded a *Liezi* in eight chapters (now lost); Liu Xiang's preface (quoted in Zhang Zhan's Jin-era commentary) describes collating it from twenty palace and private copies, reducing duplicates to eight. On this account, the received book is the transmitted work of Lie Yukou, a Warring States master "of the school of Huangdi and Laozi, resting in emptiness and non-action" (贵虚).
- **The skeptical position** (Ma Xulun's *Liezi Weishu Kao*, and the mainstream of modern scholarship): the received text is **late** — a Jin-era compilation (3rd–4th c. CE) assembled from earlier sources. The evidence: (1) Liu Xiang's preface is dated against internal inconsistencies (Master Lie placed with Duke Mu of Zheng, while the book names figures centuries later); (2) the book demonstrably draws on the *Zhuangzi*, *Huainanzi*, and other received texts; (3) **Buddhist material**: Qian Zhongshu showed the book absorbs Buddhist ideas "without leaving a trace," and Ji Xianlin demonstrated that the automaton story in the Tangwen chapter is taken from the *Sheng Jing*, Dharmarakṣa's translation completed in 285 CE — which dates that passage, at minimum, to the late 3rd century or after.
- **What both positions can share:** even the skeptics agree the received book *preserves early material* — fables with no known Han-era source, possibly fragments of the lost Han-dynasty *Liezi*. The disagreement is about the *frame*, not about every stone in the wall. Zhang Zhan's own preface says the book's meaning "often corresponds with the Buddhist scriptures" (往往与佛经相参) — the tradition itself noticed the overlap.

## How this site uses the book

The *Liezi* is treated here with the same layer discipline as the [Zhuangzi compilation timeline](/timeline/zhuangzi-compilation/): quotations are flagged by chapter, the received text is dated to the Jin era as the working attribution, and traditional attributions to Master Lie are reported as tradition, not as established authorship. The book's *xu* cosmology is used as it was historically used — as the vocabulary of emptiness — without this site adopting its full metaphysics (the "three mountains," the immortal islands, the automaton wonders).

**Uncertainty preserved:** authorship and date are genuinely disputed; both positions are live in scholarship; this page's own citations of the book carry the Jin-era dating as the conservative default.

Its compilation history is summarized at [Liezi Compiled (Jin Era, c. 285–400 CE)](/timeline/liezi-compilation/).
