**Direct answer:** The *Xiao zhiguan* (小止观, "Smaller Calming-and-Insight"), also known as *Tongmeng zhiguan* (童蒙止观, "Calming-and-Insight for the Ignorant Child") or by its full title *Xiuxi zhiguan zuochan fayao* (修习止观坐禅法要, "Essentials for Cultivating Calming-and-Insight and Sitting Meditation"), is the beginner's meditation manual of the Tiantai master **Zhiyi** (538–597). It is the single most influential how-to document of Chinese sitting practice: its vocabulary of the **five adjustments** (diet, sleep, body, breath, mind) and the **four faulty breaths** was adopted across Buddhist, Daoist, and Confucian instruction, and it remains the operating manual behind much of this site's practical guidance.

## The book

- **Author and context:** Zhiyi, founder of the Tiantai (天台) school, dictated his mature system in the *Mohe Zhiguan* (摩诃止观, "Great Calming-and-Insight," 594 CE, recorded by Guanding 灌顶). The *Xiao zhiguan* is the short, popular condensation — written for a lay audience, including Zhiyi's own lay brother — teaching the same practice in plain steps.
- **Frame:** 止 (zhǐ) = calming, stilling (samatha); 观 (guān) = insight, contemplation (vipasyana). The title announces the pairing: quiet first, then clear seeing — a two-stroke method the later Chinese traditions absorbed even when they dropped its Buddhist metaphysics.
- **Structure:** preliminaries (conditions, desire, clothing, diet, sleep) → the practice of sitting (posture, breath) → the five adjustments → obstacles and cures → the path. The received text is compact — an evening's read — which is why it traveled so far.

## The five adjustments (调五事)

The manual's fourth chapter is its most borrowed section. Before and during sitting, the practitioner harmonizes **five things**:

1. **Diet (调食)** — neither too full nor too hungry; heaviness breeds drowsiness, emptiness breeds agitation.
2. **Sleep (调眠)** — rest enough, not too much; the manual explicitly names the sleep-debt that shows up on the cushion (see [drowsiness](/problems/drowsiness-in-sitting/)).
3. **Body (调身)** — the graded posture: seat, legs, torso, hands, neck, eyes — settle in stages, not all at once (see the [sitting protocol](/tools/sitting-protocol/)).
4. **Breath (调息)** — the centerpiece: expel stale breath slowly, three times; then breathe so that it becomes "fine and continuous, as if present, as if absent" (气息绵绵密密，若有若无); and correct the **four faulty breaths** — wind (audible), panting (labored), gasping (unfree), resting (uneven). Noisy, forced, blocked, or irregular breathing is each a fault to *adjust*, never to fight (see [tiaoxi](/glossary/tiaoxi/)).
5. **Mind (调心)** — the mind neither sinking (昏, sinking into dullness) nor floating (浮, scattering into thought), then re-checking body, breath, and mind *before, during, and after* each sitting — the three-phase audit modern instruction still follows.

## What it contributes

- **The cross-tradition operating language.** The tiao-shen/xi/xin triad, the four faulty breaths, the sinking/floating pair — this vocabulary is what Daoist and Neo-Confucian manuals picked up and reused. When the Zhu Xi school teaches quiet sitting ([jingzuo](/concepts/jingzuo/)) and warns against 昏沉, it is speaking, knowingly or not, in the Xiao Zhiguan's register.
- **Regulation as the core verb.** The manual's recurring word is 调 — *harmonize, adjust* — never 强 (force). Its whole stance is the anti-forcing position this site documents (see the [comparison](/comparisons/jingzuo-vs-zuowang/) and the [protocol](/tools/sitting-protocol/)).
- **A different lineage from the Daoist emptying texts.** Zhiyi's method is Buddhist calm-and-insight, with Buddhist metaphysics; the Daoist texts (zuowang, xinzhai) are emptying-and-forgetting. The two systems share technique and diverge in aim. Both claims are stated, neither is flattened (see the [three-craftsmen editorial](/blog/three-craftsmen-dazheng/) for the same boundary).

**Boundary note:** this is a Buddhist text documented on a Daoist-centered site *because the later Chinese sitting traditions absorbed its vocabulary* — provenance is stated on every page that uses it, and no Buddhist metaphysical claim is adopted here.
