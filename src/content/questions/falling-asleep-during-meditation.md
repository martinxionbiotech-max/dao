**The short answer:** falling asleep during sitting is common, has two well-documented explanations, and does not mean meditation "doesn't suit" you — but it does mean something should be adjusted.

## The tradition's answer: drowsiness is not stillness

The classical manuals treat this question explicitly. Drowsiness (昏沉) is an *error state*, and mistaking it for entering stillness (入静) is a named beginner's mistake. The distinction is experiential: true stillness is lucid and light (心神明晰，身心轻爽); drowsiness is dull and heavy. Three causes are standard in the manuals:

- **Diet** — heavy, greasy food, or sitting too full;
- **Posture** — the head drooping and back bending as the body sags;
- **Fatigue** — sitting while already exhausted.

The remedies follow directly: eat lightly, hold the posture, rest *before* sitting. The tradition's counsel is non-moralizing: drowsiness is a condition to correct, not a personal failing.

## The modern community's answer: the body takes its due

Contemporary Chinese psychology platforms answer the same question in physiological terms: guided relaxation is sleep-inducing by design, and a tired body, once given stillness, takes the opportunity to rest. The community consensus: sleep-onset during meditation is information about accumulated fatigue — often a sign of sleep debt rather than a failure of practice.

## What neither answer covers

Neither the traditional nor the community answer addresses pathological sleepiness (e.g., persistent daytime sleep-onset with adequate rest), which is a medical question, not a meditation question. If you sleep enough and still cannot stay awake when sitting, that deserves professional evaluation.

## The synthesis this site offers

The two answers converge: treat sleep during sitting as a signal, fix the conditions (light meal, upright posture, rest before practice), and never force wakefulness by fighting the body.
