**The short answer:** the term 走火入魔 is a traditional and community concept, not a clinical one — and the modern research that does exist (see the [Farias review](/research/farias-adverse-events-2020/)) shows meditation in general is not risk-free, while saying nothing about Daoist sitting specifically. Fear of the phrase is more common than the events the phrase describes.

## What the term means, and does not mean

走火入魔 combines two images: "fire deviation" (走火), from internal-alchemy discourse where the "fire" of practice is mistimed or forced, and "demon entrance" (入魔), the older language of disturbed mental states met in practice. In community usage the phrase is stretched to cover anything from mild anxiety during sitting to serious psychological crisis — which is precisely why it is unhelpful as a diagnosis. **It is not a clinical category, and no medical system recognizes it.** It is a cultural term describing the felt sense that practice has turned harmful.

## What the tradition itself says

The classical sitting manuals do address disturbance — but their emphasis is on prevention through *non-forcing*: gentle posture, no grasping at sensations, no chasing experiences, and stopping when distressed (see the [sitting protocol](/tools/sitting-protocol/)). The tradition's implicit claim is that harmful states arise from *wrong practice* — forced technique, obsessive goal-seeking — rather than from stillness itself. That claim is traditional, not tested.

## What modern research shows

The [Farias 2020 review](/research/farias-adverse-events-2020/) of meditation in general found:

- adverse events in 8.3% overall (up to 33.2% in observational designs);
- anxiety and depression the most common events;
- events occurring in people with **no prior mental health history**.

That evidence is about mindfulness and similar modern programs — **not** about Daoist sitting, for which no safety literature exists. This page therefore records the honest state: the fear has a name but no measured object for this practice.

## The answer, stated within its limits

- Terminology level: **answered** — the term is cultural, not clinical; the tradition's own answer is non-forcing prevention.
- Empirical level: **open** — whether quiet sitting specifically carries risks comparable to other meditation forms is untested, and this page does not close the question by assertion.

**Not medical advice.** Anyone experiencing persistent distress, anxiety, or altered states that interfere with daily life should seek professional support — this is the one part of the answer no tradition disputes.
