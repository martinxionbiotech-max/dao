**The short answer:** the classical tradition has one test and it is not in a mirror — **a posture is right when the breath stays even and unforced in it.** Zhiyi's *five adjustments* states the rule as a pair of negations: not slack, not strained (不宽不急). Everything else — the straight back, the level head, the hands in the lap — is the *means* to that one end, and the archive's own instruction says it outright: do not stretch upward like pulling a scallion; the spine is straight enough when breathing is easiest in it.

## The classical anchor

The [sitting-protocol](/tools/sitting-protocol/) page holds the full classical sequence; for this question, two facts matter:

- **The test is functional, not visual.** The *Xiao zhiguan* adjusts the body before the breath — but only so the breath can then be adjusted; the body-check is never free-standing. A posture that looks canonical in the mirror and produces strained breathing is wrong, by the tradition's own standard.
- **The founding image is not a heroic posture.** The Zhuangzi's opening sitting scene is a master *leaning on his armrest* (隐机而坐) — the book that supplies half the tradition's vocabulary begins with a lean, not a lotus. The site's [cross-legged question](/questions/must-i-sit-cross-legged/) draws the practical consequence: posture is a tool, not an entry requirement.

## The archive's concrete checklist

The archive chapter on lotus posture gives the most detailed symptom-based checklist the site has recorded, and it is useful precisely because every item is anchored to a *symptom* rather than a shape:

- **The hips come first.** Pad the rear third of the cushion so the hips sit above the knees — the pelvis tilts slightly forward in single lotus and long sitting becomes impossible; the pad is the fix.
- **The spine: straight but not pulled.** 腰板要挺直，但不要挺得自己不舒服 — straight, but not to the point of discomfort. The explicit negative: do not stretch upward like pulling a scallion. The test: breathe easily.
- **The head: slightly forward.** Both looking up and tucking too far down compress the windpipe and make breathing harder — the head stops where breathing is most open.
- **The eyes: half-open, unfocused, at about 1.5 meters** — closed eyes feed wandering thoughts in beginners; the gaze is a resting one, not a stare.
- **The mouth: closed; the teeth: lightly touching** — clenched teeth bring gum inflammation; a hanging jaw slackens the muscles and stimulates the salivary glands.
- **The tongue: lightly on the palate** — touching, not curled back.
- **The hands: the mudra resting in front of the lower belly** — right fingers on left, thumbs joined, sitting there without tension.
- **The single-lotus tilt** is the one posture the archive singles out as a known trap: it is fine as preparation, but it will not hold a long session, and forcing it longer than it holds is how injuries start.

## The error signal that overrides everything

The archive's most important rule for this question is the discrimination between *mental* and *physical* trouble, recorded on the [EXP-031](/experiences/exp-031-lotus-posture-chapter/) page: mental causes (restlessness, lust, irritation) are the targets of sitting — keep sitting through them; **physical pain that keeps intensifying into labored breathing means the posture has a problem, and the problem must be found and removed** — otherwise, the archive says plainly, the body breaks. The site's [leg-numbness-pain](/patterns/leg-numbness-pain/) pattern page states the modern form of the same rule: pain that worsens across sessions is a body signal, not a milestone.

**The asymmetry, stated once:** the tradition gives you a symptom checklist and a breath test, not a ruler — and that is not a deficiency. The [progress question](/questions/what-counts-as-progress/) explains why: what can be self-checked is the process (breath rate, session length, discomfort trends), not the stage. Posture is process, not stage — so it *can* be checked, and the check is the breath.
