**The short answer:** timers are allowed but optional, and they have exactly two legitimate jobs — *ending the session* and *protecting the schedule* — and one illegitimate job: **measuring progress.** Use a timer if it removes clock-watching; drop it if it becomes a finish line to stare at. The classical tradition's answer is simpler: sit until regulation breaks, not until a bell rings.

## What the tradition actually says

- **The manuals have no clocks.** The [Xiao Zhiguan](/texts/xiao-zhiguan/) regulates body, breath, and mind — and re-checks them before, during, and after — but prescribes no duration. The unit of a classical session is the breathing cycle: "expel stale breath three times," then regulated breathing; the session ends when the regulation does, not when time is up. The site's [duration question](/experiences/questions/how-long-should-i-sit/) states the same principle from the other side: the tradition's measure is the quality of regulation, not the minute.
- **The archive's version, verbatim in paraphrase.** The Taishi collection's lotus-posture chapter answers a reader who split a one-hour sit into two pain-relieved halves: *useful — at the beginning everyone endures it that way, and in the end it all opens* (有用！一开始都是那么熬过来的，最后都能通). And to another reader: *sit every day until you cannot hold it — the time doesn't matter; if each session is short, sit twice, morning and evening* (打坐每天坚持到坐不下去就行了，时间不重要；每次时间很短，那就早晚练两次). The archive's rule is a body-signal, not a number — and the site files it as the practitioner-world counterpart of the manuals' regulation: **the session's end is a quality, not a quantity.**
- **The one classical warning applies to the timer itself.** The [tiaoxi](/glossary/tiaoxi/) frame — harmonize, never force — covers the clock too: a timer that makes you *hold* a collapsing posture until the bell is the opposite of the method. A timer that frees you from looking at the clock is fine; a timer you fight is a forcing device wearing a neutral face.

## The modern cases, both stated

- **For a timer:** it ends the session so the rest of life can proceed; it removes clock-anxiety ("how long have I been here?") from the sitting; it makes the *daily* habit concrete, and the site's [habit question](/experiences/questions/why-do-i-keep-quitting/) notes that regularity — not length — is what keeps practice alive. A soft alarm (a bell, not a phone buzz) set for the *minimum* is the standard beginner's scaffold: sit the minimum, then stop, or continue if regulation holds.
- **Against a timer:** it installs a finish line the mind can watch for — the community discussions this site draws on (anonymized per the privacy rules) report that clock-watching, including timer-watching, is one of the commonest early distractions, filed on the [restlessness page](/problems/restlessness-in-sitting/); and an alarm that interrupts a *good* sitting teaches the wrong lesson, that the session's length is its value. The tradition's alternative is the archive's: sit until the body or mind genuinely breaks regulation — then stop, without self-judgment.

## The one rule that matters

**Do not let the timer measure progress.** The tradition's measure is stability ([progress page](/experiences/questions/what-counts-as-progress/): months of the same practice, not states); the archive's measure is the body's own signals. A rising timer — "last month 20 minutes, this month 25" — converts the sitting into a race against the clock, and the site's records are consistent that the race is what breaks the practice. The timer ends sessions; it does not grade them.
