**The short answer:** at the level of terminology, **zuowang is one specific thing; jingzuo is a broad family term.** Zuowang names a particular classical description (Zhuangzi ch. 6) and, from the Tang, a particular seven-stage system (the *Zuowang Lun*). Jingzuo (静坐, "quiet sitting") is a general term for seated stillness shared across Daoist, Confucian, and Buddhist usage from the Song onward.

## Zuowang (specific)

- **Origin:** a named state in the *Zhuangzi* (ch. 6): drop the body, dismiss the senses, leave form and knowledge, merge with the Great Thoroughfare.
- **Systematization:** Sima Chengzhen's seven stages (Tang).
- **Frame:** Daoist; goal = the Dao; method = progressive forgetting/emptying.

## Jingzuo (general)

- **Origin of the term's prominence:** Song-dynasty Neo-Confucianism — the Cheng brothers and Zhu Xi made 静坐 part of moral self-cultivation; the "standing in snow" episode and Zhu Xi's "half a day sitting, half a day reading" belong to this history.
- **Range:** the same word covers Daoist quiet sitting, Confucian sitting for moral clarity, and (in common usage) Buddhist-influenced sitting.
- **Frame:** cross-tradition; goal varies with the user — moral self-examination (Confucian), health and calm (popular), or Daoist aims.

## The practical relation

- A Daoist practitioner's jingzuo *can be* zuowang — the general term covers the specific practice.
- A Confucian scholar's jingzuo was explicitly **not** zuowang: Zhu Xi warned against sitting that "sinks into emptiness" and insisted sitting be paired with study and moral inquiry. The traditions shared the posture and the vocabulary, and disagreed about the goal and the emptiness.

## Misconceptions

- "They are synonyms" — only at the coarsest level; the specific/general distinction is load-bearing for understanding either tradition.
- "Jingzuo is secular, zuowang is religious" — jingzuo in its Confucian home is just as committed (to moral cultivation) as zuowang is to the Dao; neither is a neutral "technique."
