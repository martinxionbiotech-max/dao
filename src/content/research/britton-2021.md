## The question

How common are meditation's harms when someone actually *measures* them? The [Farias 2020 review](/research/farias-adverse-events-2020/) showed the literature under-reports adverse effects; this study is the measurement-instrument answer — the first standardized tool (the MRAES-MBP) applied to three mindfulness-based programs.

## The study

- **Design:** 96 participants in three variants of MBSR and MBCT; adverse effects assessed with a 32-item structured measure covering what participants experienced, whether it was unwanted, and whether it impaired daily functioning.
- **Key structural point:** the instrument asks about *both* valence (was it distressing?) and *impact* (did it interfere with life?) — separating "unpleasant but manageable" from "harmful."

## Findings (as stated by the authors)

- **58%** reported at least one meditation-related adverse effect.
- **37%** reported effects with **negative impact on daily functioning**.
- **6%** reported "lasting bad effects" — impairment persisting **longer than one month**.
- The authors note the rates are **comparable to other psychological treatments** — meditation is not uniquely risky, but it is not uniquely safe either.

## What it does NOT say

- **Nothing about Daoist sitting.** The sample is clinical mindfulness programs; this page cites the study for its general boundary — harms exist and can be measured — not as evidence about zuowang or jingzuo.
- **Not a verdict against practice.** The same literature shows benefit; the study's own frame is that harms should be *measured and managed*, not that practice should stop.

## Why it is here

The site's safety pages — [can sitting go wrong?](/experiences/questions/can-sitting-go-wrong/) and [zuowang safety without a teacher](/experiences/questions/zuowang-safety-without-teacher/) — need the strongest available numbers behind their caution, and this study is the methodological ceiling of the harms literature.
