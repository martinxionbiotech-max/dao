**Direct answer:** About **one in four** regular meditators — 315 of 1,232 (25.6%, 95% CI 23.1–28.0) — reported having had a particularly unpleasant meditation-related experience they thought may have been caused by their practice. The finding comes from the largest cross-sectional survey of the topic to date (Schlosser et al., *PLoS ONE* 2019), and it is the **quantitative companion** to this site's [Lindahl 2017](/research/lindahl-2017/) page (the qualitative phenomenology of the same events) and [Farias 2020](/research/farias-adverse-events-2020/) (the trial-literature review).

## What the study did

- **Sample:** 1,232 regular meditators, online survey, at least two months of practice (mean age 44.8 ± 13.8; 53.6% female).
- **Question asked:** one item about *particularly unpleasant* meditation-related experiences — anxiety, fear, distorted emotions or thoughts, altered sense of self or of the world.
- **Prevalence:** 315 (25.6%) answered yes, attributing the experience to practice.

## What predicted the experiences

The logistic models give the study its practical value — the conditions that make unpleasant experiences more likely:

- **Less likely** in women, and in religious participants.
- **More likely** with higher **repetitive negative thinking** (the trait, not the practice).
- **More likely** for practitioners who engaged **only in deconstructive types** of meditation (the family that includes open-monitoring, self-inquiry, and the dropping-practices — the tradition this site documents sits close to that family's edge).
- **More likely** when practicing **alone** rather than in a group.
- **Positively associated** with longer session length.

## Reading the finding with the right boundaries

- **Prevalence is not incidence.** A single retrospective question about "ever having had" an experience is not a rate of harm — the 25.6% measures *reporting*, in a self-selected online sample, and says nothing about duration, severity, or how many would call the experience damaging.
- **The site's own archive agrees on direction.** The practitioner reports behind the [problems](/problems/breath-stopping-anxiety/) pages and the [Lindahl](/research/lindahl-2017/) taxonomy describe the same territory: real, occasionally distressing, mostly transitory events. Schlosser 2019 is the evidence that this territory is not rare.
- **What this site does with it:** the [sitting protocol](/tools/sitting-protocol/) and every problems page carry the same practical translations of these predictors — moderate session length, group or teacher contact where possible, and attention to pre-existing anxiety-style rumination.

**Uncertainty preserved:** cross-sectional self-report; no clinical assessment of the experiences; the deconstructive-family association is an observed pattern, not a tested mechanism; the study population is experienced Western meditators, not the Chinese sitting traditions this site documents — the page reports the findings as modern research on meditation generally, and does not claim them as measurements of Daoist practice.
