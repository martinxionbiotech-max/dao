## What the study did

Lindahl and colleagues (Britton's lab) conducted the "Varieties of Contemplative Experience" (VCE) study: interviews with Western Buddhist meditators and meditation teachers across traditions — Theravada, Zen, and Tibetan — building the first systematic **taxonomy** of meditation-related challenges. The project is qualitative: its product is categories and mechanisms, not prevalence statistics.

## What it found

- **A taxonomy of difficult experiences**, spanning somatic changes (unusual sensations, energy), emotional intensification (fear, anxiety, sadness), and perceptual/cognitive shifts (including experiences practitioners found hard to integrate).
- **Timing varies enormously.** Onset of challenges ranged from the first days of practice to more than 25 years in: mean 7.1 years; 29% first encountered challenges within their first year; 45% between 1 and 10 years; 25% after more than 10 years. Some (12%) reported challenges within the first ten days.
- **Challenges arise across practice types** — not only from "advanced" methods: participants attributed challenges to sitting practices (including just-sitting, shikantaza), concentration practices, and many others.
- **Influencing factors and remedies** were themselves taxonomized: practice intensity, retreat context, prior history, and — centrally — how the experience is *framed and responded to* (the study's practical upshot: interpretation and support matter as much as the experience itself).

## How it sits in this site's evidence base

This study is the qualitative partner of the [Farias 2020 review](/research/farias-adverse-events-2020/): Farias counts occurrences; Lindahl classifies what occurs. Together they ground the site's two claims about difficulty:

1. **Difficult experiences are a real, documented feature of contemplative practice** — not legend and not illness, but a spectrum that practice traditions themselves have always named (the Daoist tradition's own vocabulary: the [seeing-light](/problems/seeing-light-in-sitting/) question, the 走火入魔 fear treated on the [can-sitting-go-wrong](/questions/can-sitting-go-wrong/) page).
2. **No part of this literature concerns Daoist sitting specifically.** The VCE study is Western Buddhist; Farias found no Daoist-specific studies. Both are cited here for what they are: the nearest evidence, explicitly not evidence about zuowang.

**Boundary, stated plainly:** this is a qualitative study of Buddhist practitioners; its taxonomy is descriptive, not causal; and it says nothing about the safety or effects of Daoist practices. It is recorded here as the best existing map of the *terrain* of difficulty — a map the Daoist tradition approaches with its own older vocabulary.
