## The question

Do meditation programs improve psychological stress and well-being? This is the question the modern research literature asks about meditation — and the closest existing proxy for questions about Daoist sitting practice, which has **no dedicated clinical literature**.

## The study

- **Design:** systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials with active controls for meditation-specific effects (47 trials, 3,515 participants).
- **Programs:** mindfulness meditation (MBSR/MBCT-style) and mantra meditation.
- **Outcomes:** anxiety, depression, stress/distress, quality of life, attention, substance use, pain, sleep, among others.
- **Grading:** GRADE evidence levels, distinguishing "low," "moderate," and "insufficient" evidence.

## Findings (as stated by the authors)

- **Moderate evidence** that mindfulness meditation programs improve anxiety (effect size 0.38 at 8 weeks; 0.22 at 3–6 months), depression (0.30), and pain (0.33) compared with controls.
- **Low evidence** for effects on stress/distress and mental health-related quality of life.
- **Insufficient evidence** that mantra meditation programs had an effect on the examined outcomes — the review could not conclude effects either way.
- **Effect sizes are small to moderate** — comparable in magnitude to many other behavioral interventions, not a dramatic cure.

## What it does NOT prove

- Nothing about **zuowang, jingzuo, or any Daoist practice** — none were among the studied programs.
- Nothing about the traditional Daoist claims: clarity of spirit, merging with the Dao, "heaven and earth all return" (Qingjing Jing). Those are traditional/religious claims, untested, and no amount of mindfulness evidence transfers to them.
- Nothing about long-term effects beyond months, or about rare harms (adverse events were poorly reported).

## Traditional interpretation

The *Qingjing Jing* states "if the mind is clear and still, the ten thousand misfortunes do not arise" (心若清靜，則萬禍不生) — a traditional claim about the benefits of stillness that overlaps *thematically* with the measured outcomes (anxiety, depression) but is framed in a different causal vocabulary. The tradition's own safety counsel (Taiping Jing: stop when restless, don't force) aligns with modern caution about unguided practice.

## Modern interpretation

Meditation research supports a modest, real effect of structured sitting practice on common forms of psychological distress — enough to justify practice for well-being, not enough to justify therapeutic replacement claims or metaphysics.

## Editorial synthesis

For this knowledge base the takeaway is methodological: **the evidence base is about mindfulness programs, not about Daoist sitting, and the two cannot be substituted.** Where the traditions' claims are empirical (settled sitting reduces distress), modest modern support exists at the family level. Where they are metaphysical (attaining the Dao), the research is silent.
