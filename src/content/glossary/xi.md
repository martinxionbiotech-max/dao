**Xi** (息, xī) — "breath," and specifically, in meditative vocabulary, the **finest of the four breath types** named by the [Xiao Zhiguan](/texts/xiao-zhiguan/): after wind (audible), panting (labored), and qi (silent but ordinary), comes 息 — the "rest" breath, fine and continuous, "as if present, as if absent." It is the target state of [tiaoxi](/practices/tiaoxi/) and the bridge word to [taixi](/practices/taixi/): the same character sits at the heart of 胎息 ("embryonic breathing"), the tradition's name for breath become so interior it seems to need no nose or mouth.

## In the primary texts

The *Xiao zhiguan*'s ladder is diagnostic, not mystical: the first three name faults to be corrected, the fourth the state to be approached. Wind is audible, panting is labored, qi is quiet but still coarse; 息 is the fourth, where the breath is neither heard nor forced. The manual's target formula, 若有若無 — "as if present, as if absent" — is what makes 息 hard to translate: it names a *quality* of breath, not a quantity of air.

The same tradition runs an earlier sequence in which 息 is the object of attention rather than its quality: in Zhiyi's *Liu miao men* ("Six Wondrous Gates"), the first two gates are 數息, counting the breath, and 隨息, following it — the breath as support for attention, then attention riding it without counting. 息 here is what is attended to; in the ladder it is the state attended *from*.

The *Zhuangzi* ch. 6 gives the term its most famous line: 真人之息以踵，眾人之息以喉 — "the true person's breathing reaches the heels; ordinary people's reaches the throat." Whatever else it means, the line treats 息 as the whole body's movement, not the nose's business. The same chapter uses the verbal sense in the funeral sequence: 夫大塊載我以形，勞我以生，佚我以老，息我以死 — "the Great Clod burdens me with form, labors me with life, eases me with old age, and *rests* me with death." Breath and rest are one word because they are one movement: what is at rest is what breathes most quietly.

## Where translations diverge

English has to choose between "breath" and "rest," and the choice damages whichever passage is not being translated. Render the ladder as "wind, panting, qi, breath" and the fourth term looks like a repetition of the third; render it "wind, panting, qi, rest" and the reader wonders why a list of breaths ends in a nap. The compound 消息 ("waning and waxing," *Zhuangzi* ch. 17) shows the same problem from the other side: "decline and increase" is correct, and the breath image vanishes. This site uses "rest breath" inside the ladder and plain "breath" elsewhere: a working compromise, not a claim that English can hold both senses.

## What it does not mean

Xi is not [qi](/glossary/qi/), though modern practice writing constantly confuses them: qi is the vital substrate, 息 is the breathing that tunes it, and the Xiao zhiguan's four rungs grade the breathing, not the qi. It is not "the breath held" — retention is a separate technique with its own literature, and the manual's target is the opposite of holding. It is not a countable unit: "a hundred breaths" is a counting convention, not a measure of 息. It is not "rest" in the sense of stopping practice; the resting breath is the body's most continuous activity, not the absence of it. And the 息 of 胎息 is not literal fetal respiration in any medical sense — it is a name for breath so refined that it is felt as continuous rather than as a series of intakes.
