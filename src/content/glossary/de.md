**De** (德, dé) — the second word of the *Daodejing*'s title, and the harder of the two. Conventionally "virtue," the term is closer to **native power**: the potency a thing has when it is fully itself, and the way that potency works on others without being asserted. Where the [Dao](/concepts/dao/) is the course, De is what the course produces in a thing — its integrity, its characteristic efficacy — the fact that it is *this* and not something else.

## In the primary texts

*Daodejing* ch. 38 opens the De jing with a paradox: 上德不德，是以有德 — "the highest De does not count as De, and for that very reason has it." The chapter then gives the formula 上德無為而無以為, "the highest De does not act and has no ground for acting." De here is defined by the subtraction of display: it is what remains when nobody is performing virtue. The same chapter's sequence — 失道而後德, "when the Dao is lost, then there is De" — makes De a second-best, a fallback that appears only after the Way has receded.

Ch. 51 supplies the cosmological grammar in two verbs: 道生之，德畜之 — "the Dao gives birth to them; De rears them." De is the sustaining side of the relation: not what makes a thing exist, but what makes it thrive *as that thing*. Ch. 21 adds the dependency — 孔德之容，惟道是從, "the form of great De follows only the Dao" — and ch. 55 gives the image: 含德之厚，比於赤子, whoever holds De thickly is like a newborn.

The *Zhuangzi* uses the word with more irony. Ch. 5, 德充符 ("The Sign of De Complete"), collects maimed teachers whose wholeness is invisible; its formula is 德不形, "De does not take form" — fullness that cannot be pointed at. Elsewhere the same text uses *de* in the ordinary Confucian sense of moral virtue, sometimes respectfully and sometimes as a target of satire, which is why context has to decide which register is live.

## Where translations diverge

Four renderings dominate, and each picks a different face of the term: Legge's "virtue," Waley's "power" (his 1934 volume is titled *The Way and Its Power*), Ziporyn's "virtuosity," Ames and Hall's "efficacy." "Virtue" drags in the moral register, and with it the Latin *virtus*; "power" invites the mana reading and can sound coercive; "virtuosity" gets the skill analogy right but sounds like performance; "efficacy" gets the working right but flattens the character. None survives ch. 38 intact, because the chapter's joke is that De cannot be held up as De and still be De.

## What it does not mean

De is not moral credit — not a tally of good deeds that earns a reward, which is the reading ch. 38 explicitly blocks. It is not a substance stored somewhere in the body, not a fluid that can be accumulated and spent, and not "energy" in the sense the modern wellness industry uses the word. It is not charisma or personal magnetism, though a person with De is often described as affecting others. It is not "the Force," and it is not the Christian theological virtue of the same English name. In modern Chinese 德 means plain moral virtue, a narrowing that has to be undone whenever the classical texts are read.
