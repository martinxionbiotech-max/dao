## The term

调息, "regulating the breath" — one third of the classical trio **调身 (body) · 调息 (breath) · 调心 (mind)** that still structures how Chinese sitting is taught.

## What the source says

The canonical source is the **Xiao zhiguan** ("Smaller Calming-and-Insight," also Tongmeng zhiguan), attributed to the Tiantai master Zhiyi (538–597) — a beginner's meditation manual compiled for a lay practitioner. Its fourth chapter, "Harmonizing" (调和), prescribes **five adjustments**: diet, sleep, body, breath, and mind.

The breath section is the model of the genre:

- after settling posture, **expel stale air** — open the mouth and breathe out slowly, without force or roughness, visualizing obstruction leaving with the breath; do this three times, then breathe in gently through the nose;
- the target state is breath that is **fine and continuous, as if present, as if absent** (气息绵绵密密，若有若无);
- the four faulty breaths are named: wind (audible), panting (labored), gasping (unfree), and sighing (uneven) — and the practitioner corrects toward the fine-and-quiet middle;
- adjustments are not one-time: at the start, during, and after sitting, one re-checks body, breath, and mind against their "harmonized" (调) vs "unharmonized" (不调) signs.

## Why it matters for this knowledge base

Three points that do real work:

1. **The vocabulary crossed traditions.** Although the manual is Buddhist (Tiantai), the tiao-shen/xi/xin trio became standard in Daoist and later Neo-Confucian sitting instruction — a case of a Buddhist formulation becoming the common technical language of Chinese sitting generally. The [sitting protocol](/tools/sitting-protocol/) page on this site draws on it.
2. **Breath regulation is adjustment, not control.** The manual's repeated verb is 调 (harmonize), and its model of failure is *forcing*: audible, labored, or held breath. The fine-as-if-absent target is approached by releasing, not by driving. This is the classical counterweight to the modern tendency to make breath-work into effort-work.
3. **It anchors the "stopped breath" reports.** The community distinction documented in [EXP-008](/experiences/exp-008-breath-stopping/) — spontaneous subsiding is fine, deliberate holding is not — is precisely the Xiao zhiguan's line, restated by practitioners.

**Boundary note:** this is a Buddhist manual's terminology functioning inside Chinese sitting culture; it is recorded as PRIMARY SOURCE with its provenance stated, not presented as Daoist doctrine.
