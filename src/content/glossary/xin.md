**Xin** (心, xīn) — "heart-mind": the single organ that classical Chinese treats as the seat of thinking *and* feeling, with no split between cognition and emotion. English forces a choice the Chinese does not make. Every major school uses the term and gives it a different job, so the entry has to be about usage rather than definition.

## In the primary texts

The *Zhuangzi* gives xin its most quoted practice use. In ch. 4 (Renjian shi), Confucius instructs Yan Hui to fast, and the fast turns out to be 心齋, "the fasting of the mind": 無聽之以耳而聽之以心，無聽之以心而聽之以氣 — "do not listen with the ear; listen with the mind. Do not listen with the mind; listen with qi." The ladder does not abolish xin; it passes through it. The reason is stated in the same passage: 聽止於耳，心止於符 — "hearing stops at the ear; the mind stops at matching." Xin is defined by that function, matching names to things, and so it is exactly the faculty that keeps a person at the level of distinctions.

The *Mengzi* uses the same word as a moral organ. 6A15: 心之官則思, "the office of the heart is to think" — and thinking is what gets things, while not thinking fails to get them. 2A6 grounds the ethics: 人皆有不忍人之心, "all people have a heart that cannot bear another's suffering," the root of the four sprouts. The *Xunzi* ch. 21 (Jie Bi) makes it the ruler of the person — 心者，形之君也，而神明之主也, "the heart is the lord of the bodily form and the master of the numinous clarity" — while insisting that it knows things only when empty, unified, and still. The *Daodejing* ch. 3 takes the political line: 虛其心，實其腹, "empty their hearts, fill their bellies."

## Where translations diverge

Burton Watson renders it "mind" in his *Zhuangzi*; "heart-mind" is a modern scholarly coinage, used by Ames and Hall and by Slingerland, that keeps both registers visible. The divergence bites hardest at *Daodejing* ch. 3. "He empties their minds" sounds like a program of anti-intellectualism; "he empties their hearts" sounds like emotional numbing; 虛其心 is about desire and knowing at once, and each English choice quietly drops half of it. The same happens in ch. 4: "fasting of the mind" is a good rendering of 心齋, but a reader who hears only cognition will miss that this is also the organ that grieves.

## What it does not mean

Xin is not "the brain," a term the classical vocabulary lacks, and it is not a private inner theatre where thoughts appear before an inner eye — that picture is early modern European, not Chinese. It is not "the subconscious," nor the Freudian mental apparatus, nor "the self." It is also not the physical heart alone: the medical literature does give xin that role (the *Suwen* calls it 君主之官, "the sovereign's officer"), but that is a different genre making a different kind of claim, and the two uses should not be merged. Finally, xin is not [xing](/glossary/xing/): xing is what one is born with, xin is the organ that can be settled, disordered, emptied, or made sincere. Collapsing the two wrecks the xingming pair.
