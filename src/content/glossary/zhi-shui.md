## The term

止水 (zhǐshuǐ) — literally "stopped water," still water. The Zhuangzi's line is short and complete:

**"People do not look at themselves in flowing water but in still water. Only the still can still the many."**
(人莫鉴于流水而鉴于止水，唯止能止众止。)

## Why the term matters

Three reasons, each structural to this site:

- **It is the mirror's twin.** The [mirror passage](/translations/yongxin-ruo-jing-passage/) (ch. 7) says what the mind's use should be like — respond and do not store. The still-water line (ch. 5) says *how*: reflection requires stillness. Flowing water does not reflect a face; a moving mind does not reflect what is there. The pair — mirror and still water — is the Zhuangzi's complete theory of the reflecting mind, and the [jing](/concepts/jing/) concept page inherits both.
- **The second half is the tradition's stillness claim in one line.** 唯止能止众止 — "only the still can still the many" — is the classical statement of what the site's sitting practice *is*: not the many things being managed one by one (the storehouse-mind of the mirror passage), but the one stillness that stills the rest. This is the [zuowang](/practices/zuowang/) lineage's founding move in miniature.
- **It names the instrument of the practice, not its goal.** The still water is *useful* — people look in it. The tradition's stillness is not withdrawal from seeing but the condition of accurate seeing. The [sitting protocol](/tools/sitting-protocol/) and the [seeing-light](/problems/seeing-light-in-sitting/) problem page both operate on this understanding: stillness is for reflecting, not for blanking.

## Usage notes

The term enters the commentarial vocabulary early (the image is picked up across the inner chapters' interpreters and reappears throughout later discourse on 定 and 静), and modern scholarship (Sun Mingjun, 2022) reads it as the working definition of the chapter's word 止 — "stop," the stilling that grounds all the other stillnesses. The site keeps the term in pinyin with the gloss "still water," per the [translation policy](/blog/translation-policy/), and never confuses it with 死水 — dead water: the tradition's still water is clear because it has *settled*, not because it has died.
