**Xing** (性, xìng) — "inborn nature": what a being is by itself, prior to what training, ritual, or society adds. The graph pairs 生 ("to be born, to live") with the heart radical, and the traditional gloss says exactly that: what one is born with. The term matters because three of the major Warring States positions are arguments about it, and they disagree less about the word than about what the word licenses.

## In the primary texts

The *Mengzi* 6A is the debate chapter. Gaozi's formula, 生之謂性 — "what is born with is called nature" (6A3) — treats xing as neutral raw material; Mengzi's reply, 人性之善也，猶水之就下也 (6A2), treats it as a tendency, since water can be splashed uphill but that is not its nature. The chapter's analogies of white feathers and white snow, and of the mountain whose trees are cut for pasture, are all attempts to keep xing from becoming either a blank slate or a finished character.

The *Xunzi* ch. 23 (Xing E, "Human Nature Is Bad") takes the same word and reverses the valuation: 人之性惡，其善者偽也 — "people's nature is bad; what is good in them is deliberate effort" (偽, literally "the humanly made"). This is not a denial of xing but a claim about what it is: raw material that culture must work, the way a warped board needs a press.

The *Zhuangzi* ch. 8 (Pianmu, "Webbed Toes") uses xing against the improvers: 長者不為有餘，短者不為不足 — "what is long is not too much; what is short is not too little." The duck's legs are short, and lengthening them brings grief; the crane's are long, and cutting them brings sorrow. Here xing is the inborn constitution that moral reformers mutilate when they measure every creature against one standard.

The *Zhongyong* supplies the cosmic charter in its first line: 天命之謂性, "what is decreed by Heaven is called xing" — the same word, now grounded in the order of things rather than in a debate about human goodness.

## Where translations diverge

"Nature" is the default (Legge, and most *Mengzi* translations), with "inborn nature," "natural disposition," and "natural tendencies" as alternatives. The disagreement is not cosmetic. A. C. Graham argued that English "nature" imports a fixed essence, given at birth and set against culture, whereas xing names something that grows and is realized over a life. The Chinese idiom makes the point: 盡性, "fully realizing one's xing," is standard in the *Zhongyong* (ch. 22) and in the *Yijing*'s Shuogua (窮理盡性以至於命). One cannot "exhaust" a finished essence; one can only complete a tendency. Rendering xing as "nature" makes 盡性 sound like a tautology.

## What it does not mean

Xing is not the modern Chinese 性 (sex, gender, or a suffix like "human-ness"), and it is not "personality" in the psychometric sense — the tradition has no inventory of traits. It is not "instinct," which imports a contrast between animal drive and rational control that these texts do not draw. It is not a fixed essence that cultivation merely uncovers, and it is not "human nature" as a species-wide abstraction: the term is distributive, predicated of each thing (the duck's xing, the crane's xing), and the arguments are about what follows from each thing having one.
