**Jiafuzuo** (结跏趺坐, jiéjiāfūzuò) — the "bound crossed-leg sitting": the classic meditation posture in which both legs are crossed and the insteps settle on the opposite thighs. The word is precise about the engineering: 结 (tie/bind) the 跏 (crossing), the 趺 (instep/sole) settled on the thighs. The manuals distinguish two standard forms — the **demon-subduing seat** (降魔坐, right leg first) and the **auspicious seat** (吉祥坐, left leg first) — a distinction about *which leg crosses on top*, not about anything more exotic.

**Usage note:** the term entered Chinese with Buddhism — its earliest received usage is Faxian's *Foguo Ji* (4th c. CE) — and was adopted across the whole Chinese sitting vocabulary, Daoist and Neo-Confucian included, exactly like the [tiaoxi](/glossary/tiaoxi/) breath ladder. This site renders the double-crossed form as "full-lotus posture," the general family as "cross-legged sitting," and keeps the [sitting protocol](/tools/sitting-protocol/) explicit that **the posture is an instrument, not a requirement**: the tradition's own manuals grade posture by stability (the half-lotus, the kneeling seat, the chair all appear in the received record), and the [leg-numbness pattern](/experiences/patterns/leg-numbness-pain/) documents the modern consensus that forcing the full form is the fastest road to injury. The form serves the sitting; the sitting does not serve the form.
