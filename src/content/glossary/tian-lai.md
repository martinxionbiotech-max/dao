## The term

天籁 (tiānlài) — 籁, a reed pipe; 天籁, the pipes of heaven. The term appears once, in the [opening scene](/translations/qiwulun-wu-sang-wo/) of ch. 2, and the passage that defines it is the site's most-quoted cosmology:

- **The three pipes.** 南郭子綦, leaning on his armrest, breathes out, and his student asks how a body can be like dead wood. The teacher's answer moves through a ladder of sound: 人籁 (the pipes of humans — bamboo flutes), 地籁 (the pipes of earth — the wind in the ten thousand hollows, 万窍怒呺: noses, mouths, ears, sockets, rings, mortars, pools, puddles, each with its own cry), and then 天籁 — which the student must ask about, and which receives the book's most famous definition:
- **The answer is a question.** 夫吹万不同，而使其自己也。咸其自取，怒者其谁邪！ — "The blowing of the ten thousand is different, and each makes *itself* sound. All take from themselves. Who is the blower?" The three-pipe ladder ends not in a description but in the refusal of one: the third pipe has no player.

## Why the term matters

- **Not a third sound.** The tradition's own note (Guo Xiang, quoted in verification) keeps the term from becoming a supernatural instrument: heaven's piping is not another thing beside the hollows and the flutes — it is those very sounds, taken with everything that lives, gathered into one heaven. The ladder is not three objects but one sound heard three ways: human-made, wind-made, and *self-made* — and the third way of hearing is the teaching.
- **The driver-less family's musical member.** 怒者其谁邪 belongs to the site's no-driver family: the [great clod](/glossary/da-kuai/) (the breather with no face — the same passage's 大块噫气), the [true steward](/glossary/zhen-zai/) (the if-steward no one can locate), the [heavenly loom](/glossary/tian-ji/) (the mechanism that runs itself). 天籁 is the family heard instead of seen: each thing sounds itself, and the book asks *who* — knowing the question is the answer. The site's [creator question](/questions/does-daoism-believe-in-a-creator/) quotes this family as its textual spine.
- **The self-sounding and the self-so.** 咸其自取 is the [ziran concept](/concepts/ziran/) in musical form: things take *of-themselves* (自取, 自己), which is exactly the 自然 grammar the site holds on its concept pages. The [pity chain](/stories/kui-xian-chain/)'s 天机 self-rotation and this chapter's 天籁 self-sounding are the same doctrine in two registers.
- **The sitting scene.** The passage opens the site's [吾丧我 translation](/translations/qiwulun-wu-sang-wo/), and its furniture is practice furniture: 隐机而坐 — leaning on the armrest, not locked upright; 仰天而嘘 — breathing out toward heaven. The deepest cosmology in the book is taught from a [leaning sit](/questions/must-i-sit-cross-legged/), by a teacher who has just *lost himself* — the book's own reminder that the term lives inside the practice, not above it.
- **The drift, flagged.** 天籁 in modern Chinese praises a beautiful voice or song. The original names the self-sounding of all things — the drift runs from *principle* to *applause*, and the site registers it per its [idiom-drift discipline](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/): the word survives, the cosmology does not.
