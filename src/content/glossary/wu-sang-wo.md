## The term

吾丧我 (wú sàng wǒ) — "I have lost my me." The book's sharpest grammatical move: 吾 and 我 are both first-person pronouns, and the sentence runs the first *through* the second — the subject (吾) announces the loss of the object he had taken himself to be (我). The state it names is the whole chapter's opening scene: Nan-Guo Ziqi sits at the armrest, breathes up to heaven, looks vacant, as if he had lost his companion (荅焉似丧其耦) — and answers his startled student's question with the sentence itself: 今者吾丧我，汝知之乎 — "just now, I have lost my me — do you understand this?"

## Why the term matters

- **The companion that is lost is the self.** 丧其耦 — the phrase the book uses for the visible symptom (looking like one who has lost a *partner*) shares its verb with 丧我: the me is the companion, and losing it has the grammar of bereavement — something familiar that is gone, and the world is then met *differently* (the chapter proceeds at once to the [three sounds](/glossary/tian-lai/) — human piping, earth piping, heaven piping — audible only after the loss). The site's [butterfly page](/stories/butterfly-dream/) is the same chapter's other half: what is lost and found across the transformation.
- **The symptom pair, held exactly.** 形如槁木，心如死灰 — body like withered wood, mind like dead ash: the chapter's *diagnostic* description of the state, and the site keeps both halves honest — the words describe the appearance of a state, not a state to imitate (the student's question proves even the appearance is legible from outside: 今之隐机者，非昔之隐机者也 — "the one at the armrest today is not the one at the armrest before"). The [drowsiness pattern](/experiences/patterns/drowsiness-vs-stillness/) is the site's standing warning here: dead ash is not sleep, and sleep is not dead ash — the tradition's own texts insist the difference is the point.
- **The family of self-loss.** 吾丧我 is the *state*; [坐忘](/glossary/zuowang/) is its *method* (drop the body, drop the knowing); [心斋](/glossary/xinzhai/) is its *organ* (hear with the qi); the [empty room](/glossary/xushi-sheng-bai/) is its *place*. The site holds the four as one constellation with different doors — and the Qiwu Lun's opening as the tradition's own naming: the I that loses is the one that stays.
- **The Guo Xiang reading, registered.** The commentary tradition compresses the sentence to 自忘 — self-forgetting — and the site marks the compression as the tradition's own: 丧我 is *not* the annihilation of the subject (the sentence's grammar forbids it — an annihilated 吾 could not announce anything) but the dropping of the *claim*, the acquired me: name, role, appetite, opinion. The [Qiwulun translation](/translations/qiwulun-wu-sang-wo/) carries the full passage; this page carries the term.
- **The recommendation.** Render it "I have lost my me" (the Watson tradition renders "I have lost myself" — idiomatic, but it dissolves the 吾/我 distinction the sentence exists to make). Keep the Chinese in parentheses on first use; the term is the book's own, and the pronoun-split does not survive any single English word.
