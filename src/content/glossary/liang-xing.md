## The term

两行 (liǎngxíng) — "walking two roads." The term arrives at the end of the Qi Wu Lun's central sequence, as its named conclusion: after the monkeys of the [morning-and-evening story](/stories/three-in-the-morning/) (the keeper changes nothing in name or substance — three in the morning and four at night, or four and three — and the monkeys' joy and anger do the rest), the chapter says: 是以圣人和之以是非而休乎天钧，是之谓两行 — therefore the sage harmonizes right and wrong *with* them, and rests on the heavenly balance. This is called walking two roads.

## Why the term matters

- **The monkey story is the definition, not the decoration.** 两行 sits immediately after the three-in-the-morning joke, and the site holds the order as doctrine: the keeper does not *abolish* the monkeys' preferences, nor *join* them — he holds both (three-four and four-three are the same nuts) while letting each monkey keep its own road. 两行 is the name for that motion: the sage moves *with* right and wrong (和之以是非) rather than against them, and rests in the [heavenly balance](/glossary/tian-jun/) rather than in either side. The site's [qiwu page](/glossary/qiwu/) holds the standing warning this term completes: the leveling is not relativism — two roads are walked, but the walker is on the pivot, not on one road.
- **The companion of the ring's center.** The term pairs precisely with the site's [huan-zhong page](/glossary/huan-zhong/) (the center of the ring, from the passage's earlier half): 环中 is the *position* (the hinge comes to rest at the ring's center and answers the endless) and 两行 is the *motion* (from that center, both roads are walkable at once). The tradition keeps the pair joined — 休乎天钧 is the resting that makes the walking possible — and the site keeps them as two pages of one doctrine: the stance, and the stride.
- **The grammar of the harmonizing.** 和之以是非 is easy to misread as "compromise" — and the site's [mirror page](/translations/yongxin-ruo-jing-passage/) holds the corrective image: the perfect man's heart is a mirror, 应而不藏 — it responds to everything and stores nothing. The sage in 两行 does not average the sides; he *responds* to both, the way the mirror responds to every face without keeping any. The [true ruler](/glossary/zhen-zai/) page holds the same family of images for the self that is not one of the parties.
- **The book's own speech obeys the term.** The [zhi-yan page](/stories/zhi-yan/) holds the Yu Yan chapter's acceptance theory — 与己同则应，不与己同则反 — agree with us and we respond, disagree and we resist: the book's own words walk two roads, and the site's [translation policy](/blog/translation-policy/) inherits the term's discipline (register the readings, refuse to adjudicate, let each road stay open). 两行 is, in the site's own practice, not only a term to explain but a procedure to follow.
- **The recommendation.** "Walking two roads" — kept literal, because the metaphor does the work (and the modern Chinese idiom that survives is not 两行 but the opposite habit: 非此即彼, either-this-or-that — the term's drift is instructive: the language kept the habit the term diagnoses, and dropped the term).
