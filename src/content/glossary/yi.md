**Yi** (意, yì) — "intention," the mind's directedness: not the content of a thought but the aiming of the mind, where attention points and how long it stays. The graph puts 音 ("sound") over 心 ("heart"), and the *Shuowen* glosses it with 志, "intent." The term matters for practice because the manuals locate the work in exactly this faculty: not in stopping thought, but in intending.

## In the primary texts

The *Daxue* (Liji ch. 42) makes yi the hinge of its sequence: 欲正其心者，先誠其意 — "wanting to rectify their heart, they first made their intention sincere." Sincerity is then defined negatively, as 毋自欺, "not deceiving oneself," with its test at 慎獨, "watchfulness when alone." On this account yi is the level at which a person is or is not honest with themselves, and the whole moral sequence turns on it — which is why the text spends more words on 誠意 than on any other step.

The *Zhuangzi* ch. 26 (Waiwu) uses yi for what language carries: 言者所以在意，得意而忘言 — "words exist for the sake of yi; get the yi and forget the words." The fish-trap and snare images follow: the trap is for the fish, and once the fish is caught the trap can be set aside. Here yi is the meaning a text delivers, and the point is instrumental — the vehicle is disposable once the aim is reached.

Wang Bi's *Zhouyi lueli*, in the chapter "Mingxiang" (明象, "Clarifying the Images"), extends the structure one level up: 得意忘象, "get the yi and forget the image." Words yield images, images yield yi, and the argument is that the classics are ladders to be climbed and left behind. This filiation matters for later practice literature, which inherits the same shape when it says that intention leads qi and then must be released rather than held.

Buddhist scholasticism uses the character for different machinery: 意 renders *manas*, the mental faculty behind 意識 ("mind-consciousness"), and Yogācāra adds 末那識 (manas) as a seventh, afflicted consciousness. Same graph, different architecture — a reminder that this entry tracks a Chinese word, not a pan-Asian concept.

## Where translations diverge

Legge's *Daxue* renders 意 as "thoughts" — "they first sought to be sincere in their thoughts" — while modern translators prefer "intention" or "will." The choice is conceptual, not stylistic. "Thoughts" makes 誠意 a matter of holding correct propositions; "intention" makes it a matter of the direction of the whole person, which is what 毋自欺 describes. Meanwhile Buddhist translators render the same character as "mind" (manas), so one glyph appears as "thoughts" in one canon and "mind" in another. A reader comparing translations without the Chinese will not guess that they are the same word.

## What it does not mean

Yi is not willpower or effort — it is aim, not force, and the practice manuals treat a gripped intention as the characteristic error rather than the goal. It is not a thought-content: that is closer to 念 (niàn) in Buddhist usage, a discrete mental event, where yi is the direction such events serve. It is not desire (欲, yù), though the two are often entangled. And it is not "meaning" in the semantician's sense, even though *Zhuangzi* ch. 26 uses it that way: there yi is what a text is *for*, not a property of its sentences.
