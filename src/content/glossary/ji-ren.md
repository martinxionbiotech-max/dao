## The term

畸人 (jīrén) — 畸, uneven, irregular (as in a field whose furrows do not line up); 人, person. The term appears in the [Sang Hu passage](/stories/sanghu-friends/) of ch. 6, in the answer to Zigong's last question, "May I ask about the odd ones?":

畸人者，畸于人而侔于天。故曰：天之小人，人之君子；人之君子，天之小人也。

"The odd ones: **odd among people, matched with Heaven.** So it is said: Heaven's small man is the world's gentleman; the world's gentleman is Heaven's small man."

## Why the term matters

- **It is the book's self-designation.** 畸人 is the Zhuangzi's *own* name for its cast — the [three friends](/stories/sanghu-friends/) singing at a corpse, the [four friends](/stories/four-friends/) welcoming their own deformities, the outsiders 游方之外 (beyond the bounds). The word carries the whole point in its shape: oddness is **measured against people**, and the same person is a perfect match **measured against Heaven** — 侔于天, made equal to Heaven. The irregularity is not a defect the tradition tolerates; it is the *definition* of the position.
- **The two scales in one breath.** The closing line reverses the scales explicitly — 天之小人，人之君子：Heaven's small man is the world's gentleman, and the world's gentleman is Heaven's small man. The site keeps this because it is the book's cleanest statement of its [translation policy's](/blog/translation-policy/) deepest problem: every term of praise has two scales, and the tradition is written on the Heaven one.
- **The drift warning.** Modern usage has flattened the term: 畸 today most often appears in 畸形 (deformity, malformation) and 畸人 is read, if at all, as "abnormal person" — a drift from *measured against the wrong scale* to *wrongly shaped*. The site records this as an [idiom-drift note](/stories/fish-in-the-dry-spring/): the original 畸 is not a medical judgment but a scale-change, and the translation must not inherit the modern flattening.

## Usage notes

The term stays in pinyin with the gloss "the odd one"; the two-scale closing is always quoted with it; the modern 畸形 association is flagged whenever the term appears, so English readers do not import a medical reading the original does not have.
