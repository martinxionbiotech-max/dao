**Direct answer:** Daoyin (导引, "guiding and pulling") is the classical art of guiding the body and breath — stretching movements paired with breathing, aimed at suppleness and long life. The Zhuangzi already knows it as a practice class in the 4th century BCE, naming its signature moves: "the bear's strides and the bird's stretch." The Mawangdui silk chart (sealed 168 BCE) shows 44 figures in movement postures — the oldest surviving visual record of a Chinese movement practice. Daoyin is the ancestor of what the modern world calls qigong.

## What the sources say

- **Zhuangzi ch. 15:** the text lists daoyin among the "nurturers of the body" — practitioners who "blow and breathe, expel the old and draw in the new" and move in animal patterns, "all for long life." The passage is a description, not an instruction — but it shows the practice was already *named and recognizable* in the 4th century BCE.
- **The Mawangdui chart (168 BCE):** a silk manuscript from a Han tomb shows 44 human figures in postures, many imitating animals, with captions. It is the earliest material witness that the tradition *moved* the way the Zhuangzi says it did.

## The shape of the practice

- **Movement plus breath.** The defining pair: the body is guided (stretched, turned, extended) while the breath is tuned — the same breath hierarchy the [tiaoxi](/glossary/tiaoxi/) entry treats, applied to motion.
- **Animal patterns.** The Zhuangzi's bear and bird became a whole vocabulary — the Five Animal Frolics (wuqinxi) attributed to the Han physician Hua Tuo descend from exactly this line.
- **Health, not transcendence.** The Zhuangzi's phrase is "all for long life." Daoyin is the health-cultivation cousin of the seated practices, not a rival to [zuowang](/concepts/zuowang/).

## The honest boundary

- The classical sources *describe* daoyin; the specific sequences practiced today are reconstructions and lineages, not transcriptions of the Zhuangzi.
- The modern term **qigong** (1950s) absorbed daoyin into a larger modern system — the [qigong page](/practices/qigong/) states that relationship.

 The term layer for this practice lives in the [daoyin glossary entry](/glossary/daoyin/).

## Desk simulation: how long does one daoyin routine take, and what does a slower pace change?

> [Simulation] This section is a desk calculation from stated assumptions. It is not a measurement, a clinical finding, or advice about health.

**Assumptions**

- A routine is modelled as 8 movements. Eight is a modelling choice for this calculation, not a claim about the 44 figures on the Mawangdui chart or about any lineage's sequence.
- Each movement occupies exactly 2 minutes at the baseline pace, start to finish, with no transition time between movements.
- A round is one pass through all 8 movements. A 2-round session repeats the same 8 movements.
- Rest is a single 5-minute block per session, taken after the final movement. Rest between rounds, if a practitioner takes it, is not counted — so a 2-round session here is a floor, not the longest plausible version.
- The slower pace is exactly 90 seconds (1.5 minutes) per movement, applied uniformly to all 8; the 5-minute rest is unchanged.

**Parameters**

| Parameter | Value | Range | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Movements per round | 8 | fixed | Assumption (modelling choice) |
| Minutes per movement | 2.0 | 1.5–3.0 | Assumption; 2.0 baseline, 1.5 = 90 s pace, 3.0 slow case |
| Rounds per session | 1 | 1–2 | Assumption |
| Rest minutes per session | 5 | 0–5 | Assumption |
| Session length | derived | 17–53 min | Arithmetic |

**Calculation**

Minutes per round = 8 × minutes per movement. Session minutes = (rounds × minutes per round) + rest.

- Baseline movement block: 8 × 2 = 16 minutes per round.
- 1 round: 16 + 5 = 21 minutes.
- 2 rounds: (2 × 16) + 5 = 32 + 5 = 37 minutes.
- 90-second pace, movement block: 8 × 1.5 = 12 minutes per round.
- 90-second pace, 1 round: 12 + 5 = 17 minutes.
- 90-second pace, 2 rounds: (2 × 12) + 5 = 24 + 5 = 29 minutes.
- Pace effect: 21 − 17 = 4 minutes at one round; 37 − 29 = 8 minutes at two rounds.

**Result**

| Case | Movement block | Rest | Session total | Rest share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 round, 2 min/movement | 16 min | 5 min | 21 min | 23.8% |
| 2 rounds, 2 min/movement | 32 min | 5 min | 37 min | 13.5% |
| 1 round, 90 s/movement | 12 min | 5 min | 17 min | 29.4% |
| 2 rounds, 90 s/movement | 24 min | 5 min | 29 min | 17.2% |

One round at the baseline pace is **21 minutes**; two rounds, **37 minutes**. At 90 seconds per movement the same routine is **17** and **29 minutes** — 4 and 8 minutes shorter respectively.

**Sensitivity**

- **Pace is linear in the movement count.** Each 15-second change per movement moves a round by 15 s × 8 = 120 s = 2 minutes. A 30-second-per-movement change moves it by 4 minutes, at any round count.
- **Percentage effect of the 90-second pace.** One round: 4 ÷ 21 = 19.0% shorter. Two rounds: 8 ÷ 37 = 21.6% shorter. The percentage is larger at two rounds because the rest block does not shrink with the movement block.
- **Slow case.** At 3 minutes per movement the block is 8 × 3 = 24 minutes: 29 minutes for one round and 53 for two, i.e. 38.1% longer than baseline at one round (29 ÷ 21).
- **Rest.** Dropping the 5-minute rest gives 16 and 32 minutes; it is 23.8% of a one-round session but 13.5% of a two-round session, so its relative weight falls as the session lengthens.
- **Round count.** Adding a second round adds exactly one movement block — 16 minutes at baseline, 12 minutes at the 90-second pace — because the rest is shared rather than repeated here. The marginal round is therefore cheaper than the first in proportional terms.
- **Weekly extension.** At three sessions a week, one round is 63 minutes a week and two rounds 111 minutes (21 × 3 and 37 × 3).

**Limits**

- All figures are arithmetic on the assumptions above. Nothing here was timed, observed, or drawn from a study; no study is cited because none is used.
- The 8 movements, the 2-minute pace, and the 5-minute rest are modelling inputs. They are not prescriptions, and they are not historical claims: the sources describe that daoyin existed and what postures it used, not how long anything took.
- Uniform pace is a simplification. Real practice varies movement by movement, with holds, repetitions, and instruction.
- A real session also contains warm-up, transitions, and any rest taken between rounds; all of these lengthen it relative to this model.
- Duration is not dose. A longer or shorter session is not thereby better or worse, and no health, injury, or training effect is claimed or implied.
- The model does not represent any specific lineage's set, including the Five Animal Frolics, whose movement count and timings differ.

## In our view

We would keep daoyin as what the Zhuangzi calls it: movement and breath for long life, the health-cultivation cousin of the seated work rather than a substitute for it. The page's own reason is that the text asks for suppleness and long life, not transcendence, and pairs every movement with tuned breath. The exception is medical: readers arriving with an injury or a rehabilitation goal are outside what a fourth-century-BCE description can speak to, and that decision belongs with a clinician.
