**Direct answer:** Zuowang exists in two documented forms. The first is the classical description from *Zhuangzi* ch. 6 — a state in which body-awareness, sense-engagement, and conceptual knowledge fall away and one "merges with the Great Thoroughfare." The second is the Tang systematization by Sima Chengzhen (647–735), the *Zuowang lun*, which organizes the approach into seven graduated stages. This page describes both, keeping the text's own distinctions.

## Form 1: The classical state description

The *Zhuangzi* passage (see the [concept page](/concepts/zuowang/)) is a description of progression, not a manual:

1. Forgetting social virtues (ren, benevolence)
2. Forgetting ritual forms (li)
3. Sitting in oblivion: dropping body, sense-engagement, and knowledge; merging with *datong* (the Great Thoroughfare)

The text gives no posture, no schedule, no breathing instruction. Reading technique into it is an interpretive act, and modern reconstructions that claim to teach "Zhuangzi's zuowang technique" go beyond the source.

## Form 2: Sima Chengzhen's seven stages (Tang)

The *Zuowang lun* is a real method text, and its architecture is explicit:

| # | Stage | Chinese | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reverent faith | 敬信 | Confidence that the practice is possible and worth doing |
| 2 | Severing conditions | 断缘 | Reducing entanglements and social busyness |
| 3 | Collecting the mind | 收心 | Withdrawing the scattered mind; the heart of the work |
| 4 | Simplifying affairs | 简事 | Reducing commitments to what is necessary |
| 5 | True observation | 真观 | Seeing things as they are, without projective attachment |
| 6 | Profound stability | 泰定 | Settled absorption beyond disturbance |
| 7 | Attaining the Dao | 得道 | Union with the Dao, described as the natural issue of the preceding stages |

The stage names show the method's double heritage: Daoist aims (attaining the Dao) organized in a Buddhist-influenced contemplative ladder (*guan*, observation, is borrowed vocabulary). Sima Chengzhen presents the seven as an ordered sequence, each presupposing the earlier ones.

## How the two forms relate

Scholarship (Kohn 2010) reads the Tang system as the religious systematization of the classical ideal: the *Zhuangzi* supplied the name and the end state; Shangqing Daoism supplied the technique culture (visualization, bodily practices) and Buddhism the contemplative vocabulary. The historical continuity between the Warring States state-description and the Tang method is a traditional and scholarly reconstruction — not a documented transmission line.

## What the tradition claims — and does not

- **Claims:** the practice leads from scattered attention to collected attention, and ultimately to union with the Dao; the early stages are accessible to ordinary practitioners.
- **Does not claim:** instant results; that the stages can be skipped; that the practice is safe for everyone regardless of circumstance. The Daoist tradition, like the *Taiping Jing* before it, assumes the practitioner's life circumstances matter (hence stage 2 and 4 — the preconditions are partly lifestyle).

## Contemporary caution

Modern secular readers sometimes take zuowang as "Daoist mindfulness." The analogy has limited value: zuowang's stated aim is not present-moment attention but the dissolution of the boundary between self and Dao — a religious/philosophical claim outside the scientific register. No empirical research validates the traditional account; the stages are testable only as descriptions of what practitioners historically reported.

## Editorial synthesis

Zuowang-as-practice is the clearest example in the Chinese tradition of a *classical ideal converted into a graduated method*. Its historical importance: it shows Tang Daoism building a complete contemplative program — preliminaries, main practice, signposts, and goal — in explicit dialogue with Buddhist technique. For the modern knowledge map it connects the *Zhuangzi* (text), xinzhai and wuwei (concepts), and the later Daoist/Neo-Confucian sitting traditions (jingzuo) into one lineage of seated practice.
