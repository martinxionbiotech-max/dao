**Direct answer:** Neiguan (内观, "inner observation") is the Daoist practice of turning observation inward to clarify the mind — "observe one's own body, clarify one's heart" (內觀己身，澄其心也). Its founding text is the *Neiguan Jing* (see the [text page](/texts/neiguan-jing/)), and the Tang tradition later identified the practice with zuowang. This page describes the historical method and marks where modern reconstructions go beyond the sources.

## Historical practice: the Neiguan Jing's method

The scripture states the method in one line, then names its goal:

> "The way of inner observation: still the spirit and settle the mind, so that no wild thoughts arise and nothing crooked invades" (內觀之道，靜神定心，亂想不起，邪妄不侵).

Three features of the historical method matter:

1. **Observation, not visualization.** The verb is 觀 (to observe) — the text instructs watching what is already there, not constructing images. This is the point of contrast with [cunsi](/practices/cunsi/), the visualization tradition that fills the inner field with palace-and-deity imagery.
2. **Clarification, not acquisition.** The goal is 澄其心 — making the heart clear — a removal model, consistent with [xu](/concepts/xu/) and [xinzhai](/concepts/xinzhai/). Nothing is added; obscuration is taken away.
3. **The self is the object.** 內觀己身 — observing one's own body-mind — not deities, not heavens. The practice is introspective in the modern sense.

A Tang definition from the *Dingguan Jing* commentary sharpens the term: "The wisdom-mind illuminating inward is called neiguan" (慧心內照名曰內觀).

## Later development: identification with zuowang

The *Daoshu* (道枢, 12th c.) records Zhongli Quan answering what neiguan is: "It is what is called zuowang" (是所謂坐忘者也). The identification is significant: by the Tang-Song period the observation practice and the emptying practice were treated as the same work — observing inward until there is nothing left to observe. See [zuowang](/practices/zuowang/).

## Modern practice and the boundary

Modern reconstructions present neiguan as a seated introspective practice: settle the body, turn attention inward, observe thoughts without pursuing them, return to stillness. That description is *compatible* with the scripture's method but is a modern reconstruction — the *Neiguan Jing* gives no posture, no session structure, and no graduated stages. This site keeps the distinction explicit: what the text says, and what modern teachers add.

Two boundaries are also worth stating:

- **Not the Buddhist term.** 内观 is also the standard modern Chinese rendering of *vipassanā* (insight meditation). The Daoist *Neiguan Jing* and Buddhist vipassanā share a translation word but are different traditions with different texts; this page concerns the Daoist one only.
- **Lights and phenomena.** The question of visual phenomena during practice is treated separately on the [why-do-i-see-lights](/questions/why-do-i-see-lights/) page; the *Neiguan Jing* itself is cited there for its own line about observing the body.

## Uncertainty

- **Dating.** Scholarly consensus places the scripture at the Sui–Tang transition (隋唐之际); some sources place it in the Wei–Jin period. The disagreement is registered, not adjudicated; either way the practice vocabulary (觀, 靜, 澄心) is much older, reaching back to the *Zhuangzi*.
- **Attribution.** The text is attributed to Taishang Laojun — a standard Daoist revelation frame, not a historical authorship claim.

## Editorial synthesis

Neiguan is the site's bridge practice: it links the classical emptying vocabulary (xu, xinzhai) to the later systematization (zuowang), and it gives the *observing* verb that the modern contemplative tradition still uses. The page keeps three layers separate: what the scripture says, what the later tradition identified it with, and what modern teachers reconstruct.
