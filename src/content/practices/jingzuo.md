**Direct answer:** The Song sources describe jingzuo as simple: sit quietly, let the mind settle, do not force thoughts away, and do not chase them — then return to study and conduct. They give almost no detailed physical instructions, and this page refuses to invent any. What follows is what the sources say, separated from what they do not.

## What the sources actually say

### 1. Posture
The early sources say essentially nothing about posture. The word itself (静坐) specifies only that the practice is done *sitting* and *quietly*. Later Ming systematizers (e.g., Gao Panlong) describe seated posture in more detail — straight, still, breathing natural — but the foundational Song texts do not. This is itself information: the practice was simple enough that teachers did not need to specify it.

### 2. The mind's work
- Cheng Yi: whoever sits quietly is "good at learning" — the valued element is the collected mind, not a particular experience.
- Zhu Xi: the purpose is that the mind, "when there are no idle and scattered thoughts, becomes orderly and fluent." He emphasizes *convergence* (收斂): the mind is normally scattered outward among affairs; sitting gathers it back.
- Li Tong's teaching (Zhu Xi's teacher): sit and seek the state of the mind *before* feelings arise (未發) — a specific experiential aim, distinct from Chan's "no-thought."

### 3. Duration and schedule
- Zhu Xi's famous formula: half a day sitting, half a day reading — but this was prescribed to one student (Guo Deyuan) whose mind was scattered, recorded in *Zhuzi Yulei* juan 116. It is not a universal rule and Zhu Xi does not present it as one.
- The general Neo-Confucian pattern is balance: sitting prepares for reading and conduct; it is never the whole program.

### 4. What distinguishes it from Chan (per the tradition itself)
Zhu Xi preferred Cheng Yi's *jing* (敬, "reverence/seriousness") to Zhou Dunyi's *jing* (靜, "stillness") as the name of the practice's essence — an explicit move to separate Confucian sitting from Buddhist meditation. The Confucian sits to become *more* fit for study, relationships, and duties, not to exit the world.

## Historical cautions from the tradition

- **Do not force.** The *Taiping Jing* (an ancestor practice) instructs leaving the chamber if restless; the Neo-Confucians likewise treat strain as a sign of wrong practice.
- **Sitting alone is not the path.** The tradition repeatedly warns against quietism: a settled mind must return to affairs.
- **Ming critics' warning.** Yan Yuan attacked "half day sitting" as producing weak, useless scholars — a critique of excess, not of sitting itself.

## How to read this page responsibly

This is a *historical description*, not a prescription. The site's policy: never present a reconstructed universal technique as the tradition's own teaching. Anyone wanting to practice should treat these texts as primary material to study, not as a substitute for a qualified teacher — and should consult a clinician before starting any sustained sitting practice if there are pre-existing mental-health concerns.

## Editorial synthesis

Jingzuo as practice is best characterized as *stabilized attention in service of a life program*. Its historical importance is that it made seated contemplative work acceptable and ordinary in the Confucian mainstream — which is why a knowledge base about Chinese contemplative traditions must treat it as a first-class practice rather than a footnote to Daoism or Chan.

## Desk simulation: does session length or shift interval decide the weekly ranking?

> [Simulation] This section is a desk calculation from stated assumptions. It is not a measurement, a clinical finding, or advice about health.

**Assumptions**

1. One session per day, six days per week; the seventh day is skipped. This is an author assumption — the page gives no weekly schedule, and the one figure it does quote (Zhu Xi's "half a day sitting, half a day reading", *Zhuzi Yulei* juan 116) is a per-day instruction to one student.
2. Three candidate session lengths of seated time: 20, 40, and 60 minutes.
3. A "posture shift" is a brief break in seating, counted at 20 seconds, during which no seated minutes accumulate. The number of shifts in a session of *L* minutes at interval *N* is *k* = ⌊(*L* − 1)/*N*⌋ — the count of shift points strictly inside the session, so a 20-minute session with *N* = 20 contains no shift, and a 40-minute session with *N* = 30 contains one.
4. Shifts are timed exactly and are fully effective: each one ends a loaded block for free.
5. A "long block" is one unbroken seated stretch of ≥ 30 minutes. The 30-minute threshold is an author convention chosen only to make the comparison binary; no source on this page sets it.

**Parameters**

| Parameter | Value | Range | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Session length *L* | 20 / 40 / 60 min | 20–60 min | author assumption (page gives no duration other than Zhu Xi's "half a day") |
| Sessions per week | 6 | 5–7 | author assumption |
| Shift interval *N* | 20 / 30 / 45 min | 10–45 min | author assumption |
| Shift pause | 20 s | 10–60 s | author assumption |
| Long-block threshold | 30 min | 20–45 min | author convention |
| "Half a day", for comparison | 5 h | 4–6 h | page content (*Zhuzi Yulei* juan 116, the formula itself) + author's gloss of "half a day" into hours |

**Calculation**

Weekly seated minutes, *M* = 6*L*: 6 × 20 = **120**, 6 × 40 = **240**, 6 × 60 = **360**.

Loaded minutes per session, *A* = *L* − 0.333*k* (20 s = 0.333 min), with blocks *N*, *N*, …, remainder:

| *L* | *N* | *k* | Blocks (min) | Longest block | *A* (min) | Weekly *A* (min) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 20 | 0 | 20 | 20 | 20.00 | 120.0 |
| 20 | 10 | 1 | 10 + 10 | 10 | 19.67 | 118.0 |
| 40 | 20 | 1 | 20 + 20 | 20 | 39.67 | 238.0 |
| 40 | 30 | 1 | 30 + 10 | 30 | 39.67 | 238.0 |
| 40 | 45 | 0 | 40 | 40 | 40.00 | 240.0 |
| 60 | 20 | 2 | 20 + 20 + 20 | 20 | 59.33 | 356.0 |
| 60 | 30 | 1 | 30 + 30 | 30 | 59.67 | 358.0 |
| 60 | 45 | 1 | 45 + 15 | 45 | 59.67 | 358.0 |

Worked example, *L* = 60, *N* = 20: *k* = ⌊59/20⌋ = 2 shifts, at minutes 20 and 40, giving three 20-minute blocks. Loaded time = 60 − 2(0.333) = 59.33 min; weekly = 6 × 59.33 = 356.0 min.

Weekly count of long blocks (≥ 30 min):

- *N* = 20: 0 / 0 / 0 for 20, 40, 60 — no block reaches 30 minutes.
- *N* = 30: 0 / 6 / 12 — the 40-minute session yields one 30-minute block per session, the 60-minute session two.
- *N* = 45: 0 / 6 / 6 — the 40-minute session becomes a single 40-minute block; the 60-minute session yields one 45-minute block plus a 15-minute stub.

**Result**

The two questions have different answers, and that is the finding.

- **By volume**, the ranking is 60 > 40 > 20 at every shift interval (360 > 240 > 120 seated minutes per week). Shifting barely moves it: within the table the largest volume cost is 2 × 0.333 = 0.67 min in a 60-minute session at *N* = 20 (1.1%), and even at the range's extreme (*N* = 10, five shifts) it is 5 × 0.333 = 1.67 min, or 2.8%.
- **By concentration**, session length stops mattering once *N* ≤ 20: all three lengths tie at zero long blocks. At *N* = 30 the ranking is 60 > 40 > 20 (12 > 6 > 0). At *N* = 45 it collapses to a tie between 60 and 40 (6 = 6), because a 40-minute session kept unbroken reaches the threshold without any help from length.

So: session length buys volume; the shift interval buys concentration. Anyone who reads a longer session as automatically more concentrated has it backwards at *N* = 45, where the shorter session reaches the same weekly long-block count.

One arithmetic coincidence worth flagging, not a claim about the text: the whole simulated week at 40 or 60 minutes × 6 (240–360 min) equals *one day* of Zhu Xi's formula read as 4–6 hours of sitting. The two figures sit in the same range; nothing follows from that about what Zhu Xi intended, and the page is explicit that his formula was not a universal rule.

**Sensitivity**

Move only *N*, holding *L* = 60. At *N* = 29: *k* = ⌊59/29⌋ = 2, blocks 29 + 29 + 2, longest 29, long blocks **0**. At *N* = 30: blocks 30 + 30, long blocks **12**. At *N* = 31: *k* = 1, blocks 31 + 29, long blocks **6**. The conclusion flips between *N* = 29 and *N* = 30, and the 12-per-week figure exists at *N* = 30 alone — one minute either way and it halves or vanishes. A "60 beats 40" ranking is therefore not robust: it holds only where the 60-minute session divides evenly into qualifying blocks.

Second move, the threshold: at 45 minutes instead of 30, a 40-minute session never produces a long block at any *N* (0/week), while a 60-minute session produces one at *N* ≥ 45 (6/week) — the ranking changes from 12 > 6 to 6 > 0. Since the threshold is an author convention, so is the ranking built on it.

**Limits**

- **Minutes are not equal units.** The model counts clock time and nothing else. It says nothing about tissue load, comfort, attention quality, or any health outcome, and it cannot be evidence for or against any of them. The page's own caution — strain as a sign of wrong practice — has no representation here at all.
- **The shift is assumed free and fully effective.** A 20-second break is treated as ending a block at no cost. Whether 20 seconds changes anything is outside the model, and the model cannot settle it.
- **No accumulation across days.** Day six is computed exactly like day one, so any cumulative effect, in either direction, is invisible.
- **Exact timing is assumed.** Someone shifting "about every 20 minutes" lands anywhere in 15–25 min. Around the flip point that error is large enough to move the weekly count between 0 and 12.
- **The schedule is invented.** Six days per week, the 30-minute threshold, and the 20-second pause are all author conventions. Zhu Xi's formula is per-day and, as the page says, was prescribed to one student; this simulation does not recover a schedule from it.
- **No new citations.** The section uses only the page's own quote (juan 116) and arithmetic; it adds no empirical claim and no source.

## In our view

Sit for a duration you can repeat tomorrow, not the longest session you can survive once. The sources treat consistency as the point, and a 20-minute daily sit is easier to defend from them than an hourly session twice a month. The exception is a retreat, where longer blocks are the container rather than the goal.
