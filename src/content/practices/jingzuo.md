**Direct answer:** The Song sources describe jingzuo as simple: sit quietly, let the mind settle, do not force thoughts away, and do not chase them — then return to study and conduct. They give almost no detailed physical instructions, and this page refuses to invent any. What follows is what the sources say, separated from what they do not.

## What the sources actually say

### 1. Posture
The early sources say essentially nothing about posture. The word itself (静坐) specifies only that the practice is done *sitting* and *quietly*. Later Ming systematizers (e.g., Gao Panlong) describe seated posture in more detail — straight, still, breathing natural — but the foundational Song texts do not. This is itself information: the practice was simple enough that teachers did not need to specify it.

### 2. The mind's work
- Cheng Yi: whoever sits quietly is "good at learning" — the valued element is the collected mind, not a particular experience.
- Zhu Xi: the purpose is that the mind, "when there are no idle and scattered thoughts, becomes orderly and fluent." He emphasizes *convergence* (收斂): the mind is normally scattered outward among affairs; sitting gathers it back.
- Li Tong's teaching (Zhu Xi's teacher): sit and seek the state of the mind *before* feelings arise (未發) — a specific experiential aim, distinct from Chan's "no-thought."

### 3. Duration and schedule
- Zhu Xi's famous formula: half a day sitting, half a day reading — but this was prescribed to one student (Guo Deyuan) whose mind was scattered, recorded in *Zhuzi Yulei* juan 116. It is not a universal rule and Zhu Xi does not present it as one.
- The general Neo-Confucian pattern is balance: sitting prepares for reading and conduct; it is never the whole program.

### 4. What distinguishes it from Chan (per the tradition itself)
Zhu Xi preferred Cheng Yi's *jing* (敬, "reverence/seriousness") to Zhou Dunyi's *jing* (靜, "stillness") as the name of the practice's essence — an explicit move to separate Confucian sitting from Buddhist meditation. The Confucian sits to become *more* fit for study, relationships, and duties, not to exit the world.

## Historical cautions from the tradition

- **Do not force.** The *Taiping Jing* (an ancestor practice) instructs leaving the chamber if restless; the Neo-Confucians likewise treat strain as a sign of wrong practice.
- **Sitting alone is not the path.** The tradition repeatedly warns against quietism: a settled mind must return to affairs.
- **Ming critics' warning.** Yan Yuan attacked "half day sitting" as producing weak, useless scholars — a critique of excess, not of sitting itself.

## How to read this page responsibly

This is a *historical description*, not a prescription. The site's policy: never present a reconstructed universal technique as the tradition's own teaching. Anyone wanting to practice should treat these texts as primary material to study, not as a substitute for a qualified teacher — and should consult a clinician before starting any sustained sitting practice if there are pre-existing mental-health concerns.

## Editorial synthesis

Jingzuo as practice is best characterized as *stabilized attention in service of a life program*. Its historical importance is that it made seated contemplative work acceptable and ordinary in the Confucian mainstream — which is why a knowledge base about Chinese contemplative traditions must treat it as a first-class practice rather than a footnote to Daoism or Chan.
