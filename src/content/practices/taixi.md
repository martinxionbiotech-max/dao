**Direct answer:** *Taixi* (胎息, "embryonic breathing") is the classical Daoist ideal of breath become so fine, continuous, and interior that it seems to have returned to the condition of the womb — "breathing without nose or mouth." It is the far end of [tiaoxi](/glossary/tiaoxi/)'s continuum: from audible, labored breath (the "wind" fault) to breath "as if present, as if absent," and beyond that, taixi. The classical texts describe it as an *arrival* — the result of long practice and quieting — not as a breath-holding technique, and the tradition itself attaches diet and patience conditions to it.

## The classical definition

- **The Baopuzi (4th c.)** gives the definition the whole tradition repeats: "The essential thing is taixi. One who has attained taixi can breathe without nose or mouth, as in the womb — then the Dao is complete" (其大要者，胎息而已。得胎息者，能不以鼻口嘘吸，如在胞胎之中，则道成矣).
- **The same chapter's conditions:** taixi belongs to the practice of "moving qi" (行气), and the text attaches dietary rules — "do not eat much, nor raw vegetables or rich fresh foods; they make the qi strong and hard to close" (令人气强难闭). The goal is the *opposite* of strong, forceful breath: the text wants breath so soft it closes without resistance.
- **The deeper root:** the Zhuangzi's phrase for the true person's breathing — "the true person breathes from the heels" (真人之息以踵), while ordinary people breathe from the throat — is the lineage ancestor: breath that has descended from the upper body into deep, slow, whole-body movement.

## What taixi means in practice — and what it does not mean

- **It is a criterion, not a drill.** The texts state the result; they do not prescribe "hold your breath and count." Every later manual that transmits taixi (the Daoshu includes it among its practice chapters) treats it as the *symptom of achieved quiet*, not a technique to seize.
- **It is not breath-holding.** The modern distinction matters and the tradition agrees: the goal is breath so fine it is nearly imperceptible — "as if present, as if absent" (若有若无, from the Xiao Zhiguan's breath section) — not the retention of air. Practitioners who force retention are doing the "wind" fault at intensity: audible, labored, resisted.
- **The spontaneous version shows up in sitting.** Community reports describe moments when breathing becomes so shallow it seems to stop by itself, without strain (see [EXP-008](/experiences/notes/exp-008-breath-stopping/)). The tradition has a name for exactly this — and the distinction that page draws (spontaneous, comfortable, reversible vs. forced retention) is the classical one stated in modern terms.

## Modern boundary, stated plainly

No modern research validates taixi as physiology; "breathing without nose or mouth" is traditional language for a felt state, not a measurement. The safety frame this site applies: quiet breathing is safe; forcing, retaining, or fighting the breath is not advisable and has no classical warrant. The [sitting protocol](/tools/sitting-protocol/) therefore keeps breath work at the level of *regulation* (调), never forcing.

**Uncertainty preserved:** the Baopuzi's definition is rhetorical in part (breathing "as in the womb" is imagery, and the text treats the actual attainment as the end of the path, 道成); the page reports the ideal as the tradition states it, not as an achievable checklist.
