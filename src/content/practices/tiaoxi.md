**Direct answer:** *Tiaoxi* (调息, "regulating the breath") is the breath-work layer of Chinese sitting: the [Xiao Zhiguan](/texts/xiao-zhiguan/)'s graded method of bringing the breath from its audible, labored forms down to the fine continuous "rest" breath — with one rule governing every level: **harmonize, never force** (the glossary entry [here](/glossary/tiaoxi/)). It is the daily-workable middle between ordinary breathing and the [taixi](/practices/taixi/) ideal, and it is the first thing the manuals tell a beginner to adjust, before the body or the mind will settle.

## The breath ladder, from the manual

The Xiao Zhiguan (6th c., by the Tiantai master Zhiyi) names four kinds of breath and ranks them as a descending scale of coarseness:

1. **Wind (风)** — audible, labored, resisted: breath with sound and struggle. The fault state.
2. **Panting (喘)** — audible but less obstructed; still noisy, still not fine.
3. **Qi (气)** — silent but not yet fine; the ordinary breath, technically sound but without subtlety.
4. **Rest (息)** — fine, continuous, "as if present, as if absent": the goal of tiaoxi, the condition in which the mind can begin its own quieting.

The practice is to *recognize* where the breath sits and let it descend the ladder — the manual's verb is 调 (regulate, tune, harmonize), a musician's word: adjustment toward smoothness, never seizure of the instrument.

## The classical root

The Zhuangzi's image of the true person — "the true person breathes from the heels" (真人之息以踵), while ordinary people "breathe from the throat" — is the ladder's ancestor: breath that has descended from the shallow upper body into deep, whole-body movement. Tiaoxi is this descent, made into steps. And the same chapter's vocabulary warns the tradition's direction: the whole point of fine breath is that it *supports* the deeper work ([zuowang](/practices/zuowang/), [shouyi](/practices/shouyi/)) — the breath is the carrier, not the destination.

## Practice notes, from the tradition

- **The order of adjustments.** The Xiao Zhiguan's five adjustments run food, sleep, body, breath, mind — breath comes after posture and before mind: the body is settled first, then the breath is tuned, then the mind is addressed *through* the tuned breath. Breath is the bridge layer of the whole system.
- **The anti-forcing rule, everywhere.** The manual's breath instruction and the [Baopuzi](/texts/baopuzi/)'s taixi conditions agree: foods that make the qi "strong and hard to close" are avoided; effort that makes the breath fought is the wind fault at intensity. The [breath-anxiety problem page](/problems/breath-stopping-anxiety/) applies this rule to modern reports: spontaneous fine breath is the ladder's top; held or raced breath is the ladder's bottom.
- **The modern consensus aligns.** Beginner guidance across sitting traditions (and the community reports this site's [experiences](/experiences/notes/exp-008-breath-stopping/) archive) converges on the same geometry: start by *noticing* the breath, let it slow by settling, never by controlling.

## Modern boundary, stated plainly

Slow breathing's physiological effects (on arousal, heart rate, and stress) are reasonably well documented in modern research generally — but no studies test the classical tiaoxi ladder specifically, and the four-breath typology itself is traditional phenomenology, not a measured taxonomy. This page therefore states the method as method (checkable against the manual) and the claims as traditional (not measured).

**Uncertainty preserved:** the Xiao Zhiguan is a Buddhist manual adopted into the common Chinese sitting vocabulary — the borrowing is documented on the [text page](/texts/xiao-zhiguan/), and this practice page inherits that framing: vocabulary borrowed, Buddhist metaphysics not adopted.

## Desk simulation: how many breaths does a 20-minute session contain, at 4 s-in/6 s-out versus 5 s-in/5 s-out?

> [Simulation] This section is a desk calculation from stated assumptions. It is not a measurement, a clinical finding, or advice about health.

**Assumptions**

1. A session is 20 minutes of continuous counting: 20 × 60 = 1200 seconds, with no pauses and no lapse in counting.
2. One "breath" is one full inhale–exhale cycle. Counting the inhale and the exhale as separate breaths would double every figure below; this section counts cycles throughout.
3. Pace A is 4 s inhale / 6 s exhale; Pace B is 5 s inhale / 5 s exhale. Both are author-chosen round numbers — the Xiao Zhiguan names four *qualities* of breath (wind, panting, qi, rest) and gives no seconds anywhere, so no duration here comes from the page.
4. A comparison pace of 12 breaths per minute, i.e. a 5-second cycle, held with the same 2:3 inhale-to-exhale ratio as Pace A (2 s in / 3 s out).
5. Pace is constant: no breath-to-breath variation, no drift over the session.
6. Breaths are tallied in groups of ten, a "10-breath cycle" of 10 breaths.

**Parameters**

| Parameter | Value | Range | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Session length | 20 min (1200 s) | 15–30 min | author assumption |
| Pace A, inhale / exhale | 4 s / 6 s | 3–5 s / 5–8 s | author assumption |
| Pace B, inhale / exhale | 5 s / 5 s | 4–6 s / 4–6 s | author assumption |
| Comparison pace | 12 breaths/min (5 s cycle) | 10–14 breaths/min | author assumption |
| Definition of "breath" | one inhale–exhale cycle | — | standard convention |
| Tally unit | 10 breaths | — | author assumption |

**Calculation**

Pace A: cycle = 4 + 6 = 10 s → rate = 60 / 10 = **6.00 breaths/min**. In 1200 s: 1200 / 10 = **120 breaths** = 120 / 10 = **12 ten-breath cycles**.

Pace B: cycle = 5 + 5 = 10 s → rate = 60 / 10 = **6.00 breaths/min**. In 1200 s: 1200 / 10 = **120 breaths** = **12 ten-breath cycles**. Identical, because only the cycle total enters the count.

Comparison: cycle = 60 / 12 = 5 s → in 1200 s: 1200 / 5 = **240 breaths** = **24 ten-breath cycles** — exactly double.

Where the minutes sit, per phase:

- Pace A: 120 × 4 = 480 s inhaling (8.00 min); 120 × 6 = 720 s exhaling (12.00 min); 480 + 720 = 1200 s ✓.
- Pace B: 120 × 5 = 600 s inhaling (10.00 min); 120 × 5 = 600 s exhaling (10.00 min); 600 + 600 = 1200 s ✓.
- 12/min at the same 2:3 ratio: 240 × 2 = 480 s in, 240 × 3 = 720 s out — the same phase totals as Pace A, split into twice as many breaths.

**Result**

- 4 s-in/6 s-out and 5 s-in/5 s-out both give **6.00 breaths/min, 120 breaths, and 12 ten-breath cycles in 20 minutes**. Moving a second from the inhale to the exhale does not change the count at all; it changes only how the 20 minutes are distributed across the two phases (8/12 min versus 10/10).
- A 12 breaths/min pace gives **240 breaths and 24 ten-breath cycles** in the same 20 minutes: double the count, half the cycle, unchanged phase totals if the ratio is held.
- A short identity worth having: at a 20-minute session, tallies = 2 × the breaths-per-minute rate (6 → 12; 12 → 24). The factor 2 exists only because 20 minutes is twice ten breaths at one breath per minute — change the session length and it stops being 2.
- Phase totals depend on the inhale:exhale ratio and elapsed time, not on pace: 480 + 720 = 1200 either way. Pace decides *how many* breaths; ratio decides *where the seconds go*.

**Sensitivity**

Move session length only, pace held at 6/min: 150 breaths is reached at 150 × 10 s = 1500 s = **25.00 min**. So "a 20-minute session is about 120 breaths" is a statement about a 20-minute session, not about the practice — the count is linear in time, and the 12/min doubling is scale-free (at 30 min: 180 versus 360, still exactly 2:1).

Move the counting convention: if inhale and exhale are each counted as a breath, the same 20 minutes at 6 cycles/min reads as **240** — indistinguishable from the 12/min figure. Two different facts (a slow pace, a doubled convention) collapse into one number, so a count without a stated convention cannot be converted back into a pace. That is the flippable parameter, and it is a definition, not a measurement.

Move timing accuracy by one second: a cycle of 9 s gives 1200 / 9 = **133** breaths and a cycle of 11 s gives 1200 / 11 = **109** — roughly a tenth either way, which is enough to read as 5.45 or 6.67 breaths/min instead of 6.

**Limits**

- **The model has no variable breaths.** Real intervals vary from breath to breath; the constant 10-second cycle is a fiction the model imposes to make the arithmetic possible.
- **It cannot represent the ladder at all.** The Xiao Zhiguan's criteria are qualitative — audible and forced (wind), audible (panting), silent but coarse (qi), fine and continuous (rest). A clock cannot distinguish a fine 10-second cycle from a forced one, so the model is blind to precisely the distinction the page insists on: *harmonize, never force*.
- **No physiology.** Nothing here concerns oxygen, carbon dioxide, heart rate, or any health outcome, and no figure is evidence for or against any of them. The page's remark that slow-breathing effects are "reasonably well documented in modern research generally" is deliberately unused: no number in this section comes from a study, and none should be read as if it did.
- **Counting is assumed perfect.** A lost tally, a silent pause, or a mental clock that drifts all bias the count downward without announcing themselves.
- **Every duration is invented.** The 4/6 and 5/5 splits, the 20-minute session, and the 10-breath tally are author conventions. The page supplies no seconds, and this section adds no citation — it is arithmetic on stated assumptions, nothing more.

## In our view

We would start tiaoxi with noticing, letting the breath descend the ladder by settling rather than by control — the manual's verb is a musician's word, adjustment toward smoothness, never seizure of the instrument. That is also why the five adjustments place posture before breath and mind after it. The exception: where breath-focused attention itself provokes anxiety, the ladder is the wrong entry point, and the breath-anxiety page is the better door.
