**Direct answer:** *Tiaoxi* (调息, "regulating the breath") is the breath-work layer of Chinese sitting: the [Xiao Zhiguan](/texts/xiao-zhiguan/)'s graded method of bringing the breath from its audible, labored forms down to the fine continuous "rest" breath — with one rule governing every level: **harmonize, never force** (the glossary entry [here](/glossary/tiaoxi/)). It is the daily-workable middle between ordinary breathing and the [taixi](/practices/taixi/) ideal, and it is the first thing the manuals tell a beginner to adjust, before the body or the mind will settle.

## The breath ladder, from the manual

The Xiao Zhiguan (6th c., by the Tiantai master Zhiyi) names four kinds of breath and ranks them as a descending scale of coarseness:

1. **Wind (风)** — audible, labored, resisted: breath with sound and struggle. The fault state.
2. **Panting (喘)** — audible but less obstructed; still noisy, still not fine.
3. **Qi (气)** — silent but not yet fine; the ordinary breath, technically sound but without subtlety.
4. **Rest (息)** — fine, continuous, "as if present, as if absent": the goal of tiaoxi, the condition in which the mind can begin its own quieting.

The practice is to *recognize* where the breath sits and let it descend the ladder — the manual's verb is 调 (regulate, tune, harmonize), a musician's word: adjustment toward smoothness, never seizure of the instrument.

## The classical root

The Zhuangzi's image of the true person — "the true person breathes from the heels" (真人之息以踵), while ordinary people "breathe from the throat" — is the ladder's ancestor: breath that has descended from the shallow upper body into deep, whole-body movement. Tiaoxi is this descent, made into steps. And the same chapter's vocabulary warns the tradition's direction: the whole point of fine breath is that it *supports* the deeper work ([zuowang](/practices/zuowang/), [shouyi](/practices/shouyi/)) — the breath is the carrier, not the destination.

## Practice notes, from the tradition

- **The order of adjustments.** The Xiao Zhiguan's five adjustments run food, sleep, body, breath, mind — breath comes after posture and before mind: the body is settled first, then the breath is tuned, then the mind is addressed *through* the tuned breath. Breath is the bridge layer of the whole system.
- **The anti-forcing rule, everywhere.** The manual's breath instruction and the [Baopuzi](/texts/baopuzi/)'s taixi conditions agree: foods that make the qi "strong and hard to close" are avoided; effort that makes the breath fought is the wind fault at intensity. The [breath-anxiety problem page](/problems/breath-stopping-anxiety/) applies this rule to modern reports: spontaneous fine breath is the ladder's top; held or raced breath is the ladder's bottom.
- **The modern consensus aligns.** Beginner guidance across sitting traditions (and the community reports this site's [experiences](/experiences/exp-008-breath-stopping/) archive) converges on the same geometry: start by *noticing* the breath, let it slow by settling, never by controlling.

## Modern boundary, stated plainly

Slow breathing's physiological effects (on arousal, heart rate, and stress) are reasonably well documented in modern research generally — but no studies test the classical tiaoxi ladder specifically, and the four-breath typology itself is traditional phenomenology, not a measured taxonomy. This page therefore states the method as method (checkable against the manual) and the claims as traditional (not measured).

**Uncertainty preserved:** the Xiao Zhiguan is a Buddhist manual adopted into the common Chinese sitting vocabulary — the borrowing is documented on the [text page](/texts/xiao-zhiguan/), and this practice page inherits that framing: vocabulary borrowed, Buddhist metaphysics not adopted.
