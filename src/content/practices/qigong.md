**Direct answer:** Qigong (气功, "qi-work") is a *modern* name for an *old* family of practices. The word gained its current institutional sense in the 1950s, when the breathing-and-movement arts — classical [daoyin](/practices/daoyin/), [tiaoxi](/glossary/tiaoxi/), and related health practices — were gathered under one heading for teaching, therapy, and standardization. The term's youth is a fact, not a criticism: it names real practices with real ancient roots, but it also bundles things the classical sources kept distinct.

## The historical layering

| Layer | When | What it contains |
|---|---|---|
| Ancient roots | 4th c. BCE onward | Daoyin, tiaoxi, taixi — named practices in the Zhuangzi and later manuals |
| The modern term | 1950s | The standardization of these arts under "qigong" for therapy and mass teaching |
| Today | — | A wide family: health qigong, martial qigong, seated breath work |

## Why the site treats qigong carefully

- **The term postdates the sources.** When the Zhuangzi says "bear's strides and bird's stretch," it says *daoyin* — not qigong. This site keeps the classical names for the classical practices and uses "qigong" for the modern umbrella, because the two carry different claims.
- **The umbrella blurs evidence.** "Qigong research" is real but heterogeneous — studies of slow breathing (see the [Zaccaro review](/research/zaccaro-2018/)) are not studies of the modern qigong package, and neither is evidence for classical daoyin. The [research](/research/) collection's rule applies: match the evidence to the exact practice studied.

## The honest boundary

- The classical texts describe the *roots* of qigong, not the modern system; specific modern qigong forms (Baduanjin, Wuqinxi and the like) have their own modern histories this page does not retell.
- **Health claims require modern evidence** — the tradition's longevity language is the tradition's language, not a clinical finding. See the [cure question](/experiences/questions/can-meditation-cure-illness/) for the boundary.
