**Direct answer:** "Non-action" is the standard translation of wuwei and a standing distortion. Wuwei does not mean doing nothing — the texts pair it with the claim that through it "nothing is left undone" (无为而无不为). It means acting *without forcing*: without imposition, without agenda-pushing against the grain of things, the way water is effective precisely because it does not strive. The [wuwei concept page](/concepts/wuwei/) holds the full treatment; this comparison isolates what the mistranslation costs.

## The structured comparison

| Dimension | Wuwei (as the texts use it) | Non-action (as the English suggests) |
|---|---|---|
| Literal reading | "No *forcing*" — 为 here means imposed, contrived action | Doing nothing; passivity |
| The texts' evidence | Paired with 无不为: "nothing left undone" | Contradicted by the pairing itself |
| Image | Water, the uncarved block, the effortless sage | Inertia, withdrawal |
| Cost of the error | The practice loses its point: it becomes resignation | The texts become quietism |

## Why the texts refuse "non-action"

The Laozi says the pursuit of the Dao is *subtraction* (ch. 48: learning adds daily, the Dao subtracts daily) — but the subtraction is of imposition, not of action. The proof is in the pairing the text repeats twice (chs. 37, 48): through not-forcing, *nothing is left undone*. An "action" that is actually inaction would leave everything undone. The text is describing effortless effectiveness, not retirement.

## What the error costs in practice

Read as quietism, wuwei licenses passivity — the opposite of the tradition's meaning. Read correctly, it names the discipline at the heart of the practice pages: [zuowang](/concepts/zuowang/)'s non-interference, [xinzhai](/concepts/xinzhai/)'s non-imposition, the breath work's letting-settle instead of forcing. The "let" moves on this site are wuwei in operation; none of them is doing nothing.

The naturalness term that pairs with wuwei is [ziran](/concepts/ziran/).

## What is uncertain

The translation debate itself is the uncertainty: most translators since the nineteenth century have kept "non-action" for tradition's sake while footnoting its failure, and a minority have proposed alternatives ("non-coercive action," "effortless action") without winning the field. The interpretive question is settled enough in the texts' own pairing (无为而无不为); the translational question is not.
