**Direct answer:** Neiguan and vipassana are both *observation* practices — inward-looking, image-free, aimed at seeing the mind-body as it is — and they are frequently conflated, partly because modern Chinese Buddhist usage renders "vipassana" with the same two characters (内观). The conflation is historically backwards: the Daoist neiguan appears in its named form by the Sui–Tang period, while "vipassana meditation" as a named modern technique is a 19th–20th century Burmese construction on Pali-Canon foundations. The two practices share a verb; they do not share a lineage, an aim, or a curriculum.

## The structured comparison

| Dimension | Neiguan 内观 (Daoist) | Vipassana (modern Theravada-derived) |
|---|---|---|
| Terminology | "Inner observation" — 慧心内照, the wisdom-mind shining inward | "Insight"/"clear seeing" — the *fruit* of practice in the Canon; a *technique* only in modern usage |
| Historical origin | Neiguan Jing, Sui–Tang; Tang identification with zuowang (Daoshu) | Satipatthana Sutta (MN 10) as textual base; named "vipassana meditation" by Ledi Sayadaw, 19th–20th c. Burmese reform |
| Practice | Observing one's own body-mind; stillness of spirit, settled mind, scattered thoughts not arising | Bare insight: sustained mindfulness of body, feelings, mind, and phenomena |
| Goal | Clarifying the mind (澄其心) — and, in the Tang reading, emptying into zuowang | Insight into the three marks of existence; the beginning of awakening |
| Attention | An inward light, steady on what is already there | Moment-by-moment mindfulness across the four foundations |
| Phenomenology | The observed content settles; the Tang tradition reads it as the same work as zuowang | Impermanence of sensations becomes directly evident |
| Texts | Neiguan Jing; Dingguan Jing commentary; Daoshu | Satipatthana Sutta; Visuddhimagga; modern manuals |
| Scholarly interpretation | The observation pole of the Tang emptying tradition | A modern revival: the Canon's insight-as-fruit was re-built into insight-as-technique |
| Modern interpretation | A bridge practice linking classical emptying vocabulary to modern contemplative language | The ancestor of much of the modern mindfulness movement |

## Similarity

Both are observation practices that forbid image-building (the contrast with [cunsi](/practices/cunsi/) runs the same way in both systems); both locate the work in watching the body-mind rather than picturing it; both carry the claim that sustained observation changes what is observed.

## Difference

The aims differ in a way the sources state plainly: neiguan's purpose verb is 澄 — *to clarify, to let settle* — inside a Daoist frame that ends (per the Daoshu) in zuowang's dissolution; vipassana's aim is insight into the three marks of existence inside a soteriology of awakening. And the chronologies are inverted from the folk assumption: the named Daoist practice is the older one; the named modern technique is the younger one.

## False equivalence

- **"Neiguan is basically Chinese vipassana."** Backwards on history (the Sui–Tang practice predates the modern Burmese term by over a millennium) and wrong on aim (clarifying-into-emptiness versus insight-into-impermanence). The shared characters are a modern translation artifact, not a shared lineage.
- **"Both are just watching the breath."** Neither practice is breath-centered; both watch the *whole* body-mind field, and neiguan's scriptural anchor is the body-mind as such (己身), not the breath in particular.
- **"They can be substituted for each other."** The curricula, the aims, and the surrounding doctrines are system-bound; substitution claims replace two systems with one vocabulary.

## Uncertainty

What the comparison cannot settle is practice-level equivalence: the Neiguan Jing gives principle and state, not a minute-by-minute protocol, while modern vipassana is precisely a technique culture. Any claim that the two *feel* identical is practitioner testimony, not established comparison — the site's evidence discipline requires that layer be marked as such.
