**Direct answer:** Cunsi and zuowang are the two poles of Daoist meditation — cunsi *fills* the inner field with learned images, zuowang *empties* it entirely — and the tradition itself did not treat them as rivals. The Baopuzi states the relationship outright: among thousands of visualization methods, guarding the One makes the rest unnecessary. The [full treatment of the choice](/experiences/questions/cunsi-or-zuowang/) is a separate question page; this comparison lays out the two poles side by side.

## The structured comparison

| Dimension | Cunsi 存思 | Zuowang 坐忘 |
|---|---|---|
| Terminology | "Holding-and-visualizing" (思存念作) | "Sitting and forgetting" |
| Historical origin | Baopuzi (4th c.); Huangting Jing (3rd–4th c.); Shangqing systematization | Zhuangzi ch. 6 (4th–3rd c. BCE); Zuowang lun (8th c.) |
| Practice | Building and stabilizing inner images: palaces, colors, directions, resident spirits | Subtracting: dropping the body, expelling the senses, merging with the Great Thoroughfare |
| Goal | Protection and transformation; constructing the inner map | Union with the Dao through dissolution of the self-construction |
| Attention | Concentrated, constructive — holding one image steady | Released — attention dissolves with its objects |
| Phenomenology | A vivid, structured inner landscape | The absence of content: "drop the body, expel the senses" (堕肢体，黜聪明) |
| Texts | Baopuzi, Huangting Jing, Shangqing scriptures | Zhuangzi, Zuowang lun |
| Scholarly interpretation | The imagistic pole of early medieval Daoism; Shangqing's central method | The emptying tradition; later systematized and (Tang) identified with neiguan |
| Modern interpretation | Structured visualization with traditional aims; no modern research on cunsi specifically | The site's center of gravity; ancestor of modern "meditation" vocabulary |

## Similarity

Both are sitting practices aimed at changing the practitioner's relation to the self; both assume a curriculum and (in the received forms) a teacher; both are preserved in the same texts — the Baopuzi describes them in one breath, as options on one shelf.

## Difference

The direction of the work is opposite. Cunsi constructs: the body must first be *built* as an organized inner world before it can be forgotten. Zuowang deconstructs: forgetting the body presupposes a body worth forgetting. The tradition's geometry, stated in the Baopuzi's ranking, is that the two belong to **one sequence** — build, then dissolve — and that the midpoint between them is shouyi: one thing held instead of the whole library.

## False equivalence

- **"Cunsi is just guided imagery."** It is imagery, but imagery with traditional doctrine attached — palaces, spirits, and aims (protection, transformation) that guided imagery as a modern therapy does not claim. The comparison flattens the doctrine into a technique.
- **"Zuowang is just emptying the mind."** The Zhuangzi's phrase is body *and* mind dropped together — a loss of the body-sense, not a cognitive task of clearing thoughts. The full translation is on the [zuowang passage](/translations/zuowang-passage/) page.
- **"One is beginner, one is advanced."** The Baopuzi's ordering is not a beginner/advanced ladder; it is an economy claim — the One replaces the library. Both poles appear across the history of the tradition without a fixed pedagogical order.

## Uncertainty

The sources state the relationship (build, dissolve, or hold the One) but do not legislate a curriculum; any modern claim that a specific sequence — cunsi for N years, then zuowang — is *the* traditional order goes beyond what the texts say. The practical reading on the [choice question page](/experiences/questions/cunsi-or-zuowang/) is stated as editorial synthesis, not as scripture.
