## The event

In the second half of the 6th century, the Tiantai master **Zhiyi** (538–597) composed the *Xiao zhiguan* ("Smaller Calming-and-Insight") — a beginner's meditation manual written for a lay practitioner. Its fourth chapter codified the **five adjustments** (调五事): diet, sleep, body, breath, and mind.

The chapter's method set the template for Chinese sitting instruction ever after: settle the posture in nine graded steps; expel stale breath slowly (three times) before breathing in; pursue the breath that is "fine and continuous, as if present, as if absent" (气息绵绵密密，若有若无); correct the four faulty breaths (audible, labored, unfree, uneven); and re-check body, breath, and mind before, during, and after sitting.

## Why it matters

- **A Buddhist manual's vocabulary becomes the common language of Chinese sitting.** The tiao-shen/xi/xin trio was adopted across Daoist and Neo-Confucian instruction — the clearest documented case of cross-tradition technical borrowing in this field.
- **Regulation, not control.** The manual's repeated verb is 调 (harmonize), and its failure-model is forcing. This is the classical warrant behind this site's [sitting protocol](/tools/sitting-protocol/) and its stance against breath-forcing.
- **Sleep appears as a practice variable.** The five adjustments formally recognize that the body's rest-debt shows up on the cushion — the pre-modern ancestor of the modern fatigue reading of meditation drowsiness (see [drowsiness](/problems/drowsiness-in-sitting/)).
