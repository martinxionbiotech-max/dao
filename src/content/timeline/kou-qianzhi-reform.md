## The event, in layers

The reform that made Daoism a northern state institution, anchored date by date:

- **c. 415 CE — the revelation.** On Songshan, the deified Laozi descended to Kou Qianzhi with the *Lao-jun yinsong jiejing* — the "hymned commandments" — commissioning him to take the Celestial Master seat and **purge the tradition**: abolish the hereditary libationer offices, forbid the grain-and-coin tax, and remove the "three Zhangs' false methods" — above all the sexual "union of breaths" practice — replacing them with fasts, liturgical music, and a Confucian-flavored ethics. (A second commission follows in the tradition's record, per the *Weishu*.)
- **c. 423/424 CE — the court.** Kou Qianzhi emerges to the Northern Wei court at Pingcheng (the sources split the year by one), sponsored by the minister Cui Hao, an aristocrat from a Celestial Master family. The emperor orders offerings sent to Songshan and welcomes the master's students to the capital.
- **431 CE — the tower.** Construction of the Jinglun heavenly palace begins — the monument meant to touch the gods, its height "so that no cock-crow or dog-bark could be heard" (recorded in the *Weishu*).
- **440 CE — the title.** At Songshan, the emperor is said to receive the deified Laozi's dark bestowal of the title **"Great Peace True Lord"** (太平真君), and the reign name is changed accordingly.
- **442 CE — the registers.** In the first month of the third year of the Great Peace True Lord reign, Emperor Taiwu comes in full state to the Daoist altar and **receives the registers** — the banners all blue, the Daoist color — establishing the rite by which every later Northern Wei emperor would be initiated at accession. Daoism has become a state religion of a northern dynasty.
- **446 CE — the counter-reform resisted.** Cui Hao engineers the Buddhist suppression; Kou Qianzhi opposes it (per the record, even interceding against mass executions of monks) — and dies two years later, aged eighty-three.

## Why this event matters to the site

Because it is the site's boundary-stone between **practice and institution**. Everything on this timeline is institutional: revelations, taxes, altars, registers, reign names. Nothing here changes how anyone sits — yet every later sitting lineage in the north sits inside the frame this reform built, and the site's [fourth-century map](/people/ge-hong/) (Ge Hong in the south, the Shangqing corpus, and this northern state church) is unintelligible without it. It is also the tradition's own first great **purification precedent**: the line drawn against the "three Zhangs' false methods" is the earliest large-scale case of the tradition defining its own boundaries — the same boundary discipline the site's [evidence policy](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/) continues editorially.

**Uncertainty preserved:** the revelation is the tradition's account, not a historical event; the emergence year (423/424) is kept as a range; the "false methods" are named by the reformers, whose description of the Zhangs is polemical rather than neutral.
