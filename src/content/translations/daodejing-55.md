## Chinese text

含德之厚，比于赤子。毒虫不螫，猛兽不据，攫鸟不搏。骨弱筋柔而握固。未知牝牡之合而朘作，精之至也。终日号而不嗄，和之至也。知和曰常，知常曰明。益生曰祥。心使气曰强。物壮则老，谓之不道，不道早已。

## Literal rendering

The one who holds virtue thick is comparable to a newborn. Poisonous insects do not sting him, fierce beasts do not seize him, birds of prey do not strike him. His bones are weak and his sinews soft, yet his grip is firm. He does not yet know the union of male and female, yet his member stirs — essence at its height. He cries all day without going hoarse — harmony at its height. Knowing harmony is called the constant; knowing the constant is called clarity. Over-nourishing life is called ominous. The heart driving the breath is called force. What peaks in strength grows old; this is called not-the-Way; what is not the Way ends early.

## Term-by-term notes

- **The newborn as the book's model of the whole body.** 赤子 — the newborn — is the chapter's specimen, and the chapter inventories it without sentimentality: soft bones, soft sinews, a grip that will not open (骨弱筋柔而握固); crying all day without hoarseness (终日号而不嗄). The two verdicts are named states: 精之至也, essence at its height; 和之至也, harmony at its height. The site's [ch. 10 translation](/translations/daodejing-10-shouyi/) holds the book's other infant-formula (专气致柔，能婴儿乎 — concentrating the breath to softness, can you be an infant?), and its [essence-breath-spirit page](/glossary/jing-qi-shen/) holds the triad this chapter runs on: the infant is the *unspent* body — nothing has been drawn down, which is what 厚德 (thick virtue) means in this chapter's physiology.
- **The immunity trio, registered.** 毒虫不螫，猛兽不据，攫鸟不搏 — the three "not harmed" clauses — are the chapter's most contested lines, and the site registers the variants (攫鸟 vs 攫鸷; the Zhihu text's 蜂虿虺蛇 opening) without adjudicating, and the reading discipline without endorsing: the traditional claim is that thick virtue is itself the protection (the child is not attacked because nothing in it provokes attack — the same grammar as the [dithering bird](/stories/yi-dai-niao/): what nothing can get a purchase on is not harmed), while modern readers treat the trio as description of the unspent state rather than a promise. The site's [qi page](/glossary/qi/) holds the boundary: the claims are registered as the text's physiology, not as testable protection.
- **The double 之至也 and its honesty.** 精之至也 and 和之至也 name what the infant *has* — and the chapter does not pretend the reader has it. The infant's grip is the palmar reflex (modern pediatric observation notes the newborn's strong grasp — a coincidence registered, not a confirmation); the infant's erect member (未知牝牡之合而朘作) is the text's rawest sentence, and its point is precisely that this happens *without knowledge* — essence operating on its own. The book's word for that is 至, the height: the infant is not trying.
- **The knowing-ladder.** 知和曰常，知常曰明 — knowing harmony is called the constant, knowing the constant is called clarity — is the chapter's positive half, and the site's [ch. 16 translation](/translations/daodejing-16/) holds the companion formula (复命曰常，知常曰明): the two chapters share the same two rungs, and this chapter adds the first rung they share: 和 — harmony — is what returns to the constant. The site's [still-water family](/glossary/zhi-shui/) holds the verb-side (stop, and the mirroring begins); ch. 55 is the noun-side: the constant that knowing harmony knows.
- **The warnings, and the trap word 祥.** 益生曰祥。心使气曰强。物壮则老，谓之不道，不道早已 — the negative ladder: over-nourishing life, the heart driving the breath, the strength that peaks — and the site flags the trap: 祥 in modern Chinese means *auspicious*, and popular translations render 益生曰祥 as "nourishing life is auspicious"; the traditional commentarial reading takes 祥 here as 妖祥, *ill-omen* (what the chapter's own close condemns: 物壮则老, what peaks in strength grows old). Both readings are registered; the chapter's internal logic — three warnings followed by the death-sentence of the over-strong — supports the warning-reading. The site's [ch. 40 translation](/translations/daodejing-40/) holds the companion claim (弱者道之用 — the Way works through the weak), and this chapter is its physiological case-file: the infant is the book's demonstration that *the height is the unspent state*, and every addition after the height is the beginning of spending it.
