## The chapter

**Four verbs, four agents, one assembly line.** Chapter 51 is the book's most explicit division of labor between its two central powers: the dao gives life (道生之), the de nurtures it (德畜之), *things* give it shape (物形之), and circumstances complete it (势成之). Four different subjects, one per verb — the chapter is careful not to let the dao do everything. The dao starts; the de — its power as it runs through a particular thing, the site's [de](/concepts/de/) entry — holds and feeds it; the material world gives it its individual form; and the situation it lands in finishes the job. A thing's completion is a four-handed process, and the last hand is not the least: 势, the same word as the *momentum* that a river finds or an army rides, names the luck of placement.

**Honor without a decree.** The middle of the chapter states the consequence and then guards it with the book's most characteristic clause: all the ten thousand things honor the dao and value the de — *and no one commands this; it is constantly of themselves* (夫莫之命而常自然). The word at the end is 自然, the site's [ziran](/concepts/ziran/): not "nature" as a forest, not spontaneity as an impulse, but the order that maintains itself without a boss. Honor, the chapter says, is exactly like that — the one kind of reverence that does not need a decree, because it is the recognition of what is already doing the work.

**The middle four verbs.** 长之育之，亭之毒之；养之覆之 — grows them, raises them, settles them, matures them, nourishes them, shelters them. The third pair is the chapter's most contested spot: the received Wang Bi text reads 亭之毒之 — settle and mature, 亭 from the word for a pavilion standing straight, 毒 in its ancient sense of gathering to ripen (the same 毒 that means "to mature" in old agricultural usage). The Heshang Gong tradition reads 成之熟之 — bring to completion, ripen — and the site registers both readings without adjudicating: either way, the line lists the de's ongoing work after birth, and the point is that it *keeps working* — the de is not a one-time endowment but a continuing custody.

**The second signature.** The chapter ends with the four-character formula the book has used before and will use again, each character a refusal: **生而不有，为而不恃，长而不宰** — gives life without owning, acts without relying, grows without ruling. And names it: **是谓玄德** — *this is called the dark de.* The site's [daodejing-02](/translations/daodejing-02/) page holds the same formula in the book's second chapter; its appearance here, at the end of the chapter that defined the de, is the book's own confirmation that the formula *is* the definition. The "dark" (玄) is the same darkness as [chapter 1's](/translations/daodejing-01/) 玄之又玄 — not obscurity but depth past naming: the de is called dark precisely because its owner refuses to be visible as an owner.

## Why this chapter matters for sitting

The chapter's four-handed picture of any given life — dao, de, things, circumstances — is the classical form of the site's honesty rule: the sitting tradition's claims about [qi](/concepts/qi/), the channels, and the repair schedules belong to the de-layer of the tradition's own map, and this chapter is where the map is drawn. And the final formula, 长而不宰 — *grows without ruling* — is the oldest statement of the site's [practice policy](/guides/chinese-meditation-explained/): the tradition at its best treats the practitioner the way the dao treats things — it feeds, it does not own.
