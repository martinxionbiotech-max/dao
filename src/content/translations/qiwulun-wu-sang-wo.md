## The passage

南郭子綦隐机而坐，仰天而嘘，嗒焉似丧其耦。颜成子游立侍乎前，曰："何居乎？形固可使如槁木，而心固可使如死灰乎？今之隐机者，非昔之隐机者也。"子綦曰："偃，不亦善乎，而问之也！今者吾丧我，汝知之乎？……"

## Translation (with notes)

**Nanguo Ziqi sat leaning on his armrest** (南郭子綦隐机而坐), **looked up at heaven and breathed out slowly** (仰天而嘘), **dispersed and distant, as if he had lost his counterpart** (嗒焉似丧其耦).

**His disciple Yancheng Ziyou stood waiting before him and said: "What is this? Can the body really be made like dead wood** (形固可使如槁木) **and the heart like dead ashes** (心固可使如死灰乎)? **The man leaning on the armrest now is not the man who leaned on it before!"** (今之隐机者，非昔之隐机者也)

**Ziqi said: "Yan, how good that you ask! Just now I lost myself** (今者吾丧我). **Do you understand that? …"**

## Word-by-word notes

- **隐机而坐** — "sitting leaning on the armrest" (隐几, the low stand). The Inner Chapters' characteristic sitting posture is *relaxed leaning*, not upright discipline — worth noticing against the later manuals' graded posture (see the [sitting protocol](/tools/sitting-protocol/)). The Zhuangzi's sitting is a *slump of presence*, not an exercise in symmetry.
- **仰天而嘘** — "looked up to heaven and slowly breathed out." 嘘 is the soft, audible exhalation — the one breath the Inner Chapters ever describe, and it is an *out*-breath. The chapter's stillness begins with release, not intake.
- **嗒焉似丧其耦** — "dispersed, as if he had lost his counterpart/mate" (耦). 耦 is the body-mate of the self — what it is paired with. The phrase says: his awareness is no longer paired with its usual object (his own body-person). The "loss" is of the counterpart, not of consciousness itself.
- **形如槁木，心如死灰** — "body like dead wood, heart like dead ashes." The disciple's words — the four-character compounds (槁木死灰) became proverbial for contemplative depth. They matter here because they appear *first in the disciple's mouth, as a question*: "can it really be like this?" The text answers with the teacher's own sentence:
- **今者吾丧我** — "just now I lost myself" — the most analyzed sentence in the book's contemplative vocabulary. Two different words for the self stand in the same breath: 吾 (wú) and 我 (wǒ). The standard reading, following Guo Xiang's commentary and preserved by the whole tradition: 吾 is the *agent* who speaks, 我 is the *constructed* self — the accumulated identity of name, position, opinion, desire. "I lost my I": the speaker remains (someone is reporting the loss), while the narrated, defended self is gone. Zuowang's "I forget my body" ([ch. 6](/translations/zuowang-passage/)) and the craftsman's "I have forgotten my four limbs" ([ch. 19](/translations/artisan-qing-passage/)) are both later unfoldings of this single sentence.
- **今之隐机者，非昔之隐机者也** — the disciple notices the *change*: the person at the armrest now is not the person from before. The chapter's first claim about practice is diagnostic — depth of sitting shows, visibly, to another person.

## Why this translation matters

This is the Zhuangzi's *opening* statement of what sitting does, placed at the head of its most philosophical chapter. Before any doctrine, the book shows a person at an armrest who has lost the constructed self — and a second person who can see it. The chapter then spends its length dismantling the machinery of the 我 (judgments, opinions, the self-other split) that sitting has, in this vignette, already dropped. The translation choices above (吾 as agent, 我 as constructed identity) are the tradition's, going back to Guo Xiang; modern scholars vary, and the page records the standard reading as such.

**Translation problems:** 丧其耦's 耦 has been read as "mate," "pair," even "soul-mate of the body" — the rendering "lost his counterpart" preserves the ambiguity deliberately. And the 吾/我 distinction is *untranslatable* into English's single "I"; the note above states the two-word reading and its source rather than pretending the English can carry it.
