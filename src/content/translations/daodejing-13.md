## Chinese text

宠辱若惊，贵大患若身。何谓宠辱若惊？宠为下，得之若惊，失之若惊，是谓宠辱若惊。何谓贵大患若身？吾所以有大患者，为吾有身；及吾无身，吾有何患？故贵以身为天下，若可寄天下；爱以身为天下，若可托天下。

## Pinyin

Chǒng rǔ ruò jīng, guì dà huàn ruò shēn. Hé wèi chǒng rǔ ruò jīng? Chǒng wéi xià, dé zhī ruò jīng, shī zhī ruò jīng, shì wèi chǒng rǔ ruò jīng. Hé wèi guì dà huàn ruò shēn? Wú suǒyǐ yǒu dà huàn zhě, wéi wú yǒu shēn; jí wú wú shēn, wú yǒu hé huàn? Gù guì yǐ shēn wéi tiānxià, ruò kě jì tiānxià; ài yǐ shēn wéi tiānxià, ruò kě tuō tiānxià.

## Literal rendering

Favor and disgrace both startle; honor great trouble as your own body. What does "favor and disgrace both startle" mean? Favor is the lower thing — getting it startles, losing it startles: this is called favor and disgrace both startling. What does "honor great trouble as your own body" mean? The reason I have great trouble is that I have a body; when I have no body, what trouble do I have? Therefore: one who honors the body as the world may be entrusted with the world; one who loves the body as the world may be given the world in trust.

## Term-by-term notes

- **The first line's double diagnosis.** 宠辱若惊: the chapter's opening claim is that *both* directions startle — the gift and the blow — and the Guangming study (registered in verification) notes the chapter is the source of *two* surviving idioms at once: 受宠若惊 (overwhelmed by favor — the startled half) and 宠辱不惊 (unmoved by either — the cured half). The modern language kept both the illness and the prescription from one verse, which is why the site's translation keeps 若惊 as "startle" throughout: the flinch is the term.
- **宠为下 — the reading the editions split on.** The received Wang Bi text reads 宠为下 ("favor is the lower thing" — the subordinate's state, the one who *receives* favor), and the collation records the variant 宠为上 in later editions. The site registers both without adjudicating, per its [variant discipline](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/), and notes the received reading's sharper edge: the whole startle-response belongs to the lower position — the favored one is startled precisely because the favor is not his own.
- **无身 — the trap the whole chapter is.** 及吾无身，吾有何患 — and here the commentary tradition, quoted in the verified study, does its most important work: 无身 is *not* body-elimination (the Guangming study registers the Feng Youlan reading — no-self, selfless, 至人无己 in the Zhuangzi's phrase — and rebuts the Ren Jiyu literal reading, which would contradict the book's own 长生久视 of ch. 59). The site's [wu-sang-wo page](/glossary/wu-sang-wo/) holds the Zhuangzi twin of the same move: what is lost is the *me*, not the body — the grammar of 吾丧我 preserves the subject precisely because the loss is of the possessive self. The chapter's own proof is in its final couplet: the body is to be *honored* and *loved* — just held for the world rather than for oneself.
- **The counterfactual, not the prescription.** 及吾无身 ("*when* I have no body") is the chapter's conditional, and the site keeps it conditional in translation: the verse does not command self-removal; it names the condition under which trouble ends. The [ch. 7 translation](/translations/daodejing-07/) holds the same logic in the book's own practice-form (后其身而身先，外其身而身存 — put yourself behind and you come out ahead), and the Guangming study explicitly links the two chapters. The site's [illness question](/experiences/questions/should-i-meditate-when-sick/) draws the practical line from the same source: the body is not the enemy of the practice; it is the instrument the chapter finally entrusts with the world.
- **The trust formula.** 贵以身为天下，若可寄天下 — the closing couplet is the chapter's real subject: the body, held lightly and completely, is the qualification for holding *anything*. The site keeps the word order's point (the body is honored *as* the world — the scale of the honor is the world's scale), and registers the silk-text variant (贵为身于为天下 — honoring the body *in* working for the world) as the earlier reading of the same formula, not a different doctrine.
- **The word's later life.** The pair 宠辱不惊/受宠若惊 survives in the modern language with the chapter's sense intact — one of the book's least-drifted idiom exports — and the site notes the asymmetry: the language kept the *startle* (the illness) and the *unstartled* (the cure) but dropped the middle term, 无身, which is where the chapter's work is done.
