## The chapter

**The water theorem, for the last time in full form.** Chapter 78 is the book's final statement of the water doctrine — and its only chapter that runs the theorem all the way from physics to the throne room. The opening repeats the familiar claim with a new verb: water does not merely *win* against the hard and strong; nothing can 易 it — *exchange for it, take its place, substitute for it*. The hardness of rock is beatable because water's position is unsubstitutable: wherever the hard thing stands, water can stand there too, and the hard thing cannot stand where water is.

Then the chapter turns to the audience, and the turn is the chapter's real subject: **everyone knows this, and no one practices it** (天下莫不知，莫能行). The book has said this before — the knowing-doing gap is the same complaint the [whole book](/texts/daodejing/) registers about its own reception, and the water thesis itself is [chapter 8's](/translations/daodejing-08/) — but here the gap gets a political price tag. The sage's saying is quoted, not invented: *whoever receives the country's filth is called the lord of the altars* (受國之垢，是謂社稷主). The ruler's qualification is precisely the work no one wants — the dirt, the disgrace, the bad omens, the blame. This is the water thesis translated into office: the lowest position is the unsubstitutable one, because nothing can *take its place* downward.

**正言若反 — straight words seem reversed.** The chapter ends with the book's most famous self-description, and the chapter is its own example: the claim that weakness wins is a *straight* word that *seems* reversed — seems, not is. The [ch. 22](/translations/daodejing-22/) cluster (bent-then-whole, hollow-then-full) runs the same grammar item by item. The sentence is also a warning label the book attaches to itself: if the Laozi's words sound upside-down to you, the chapter says, check which side of the knowing-doing gap you are standing on.

## Translation notes

- **无以易之.** The pivot of the chapter, and the hardest clause to render. Literally: *using it, nothing exchanges/substitutes [for it]*. The standard "nothing can overcome it" flattens 易 into 胜 — but the text deliberately switches verbs. Water is not merely victorious; it is *unreplaceable* — you cannot pay anything to take its position. Recommended: "for nothing can take its place."
- **社稷主.** 社稷 (she-ji) is the altars of the soil-god and grain-god — the state's cultic core, hence "the realm" by metonymy. 主 (zhu) is lord/master — but in this chapter the office is defined by the verb 受 (shou, receive/take on). The lord is the one who *takes on* the filth — the title follows the verb, not the other way around.
- **正言若反.** Kept literal ("straight words seem reversed") rather than the fluent "true words seem paradoxical," to preserve the contrast 正/反 (straight/reversed) that the chapter's whole argument plays on. The words are *straight*; the reversal is in how they *seem* — the text does not admit that its speech is crooked.
- **The political vocabulary is the practice vocabulary.** 垢 and 不祥 are not decorative: they are the same register as [ch. 13's](/translations/daodejing-13/) "favor and disgrace are both startling" — the body's exposure to shame. The chapter's wager is that the lowest seat and the highest office are one seat; the site's [wuwei](/concepts/wuwei/) page notes the same structure (the last place is the one that cannot be displaced), and the [water-weakness question](/questions/what-is-stillness/) holds the practice reading: stillness is not the absence of force but the position nothing can exchange for.
