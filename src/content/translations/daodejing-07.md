## Chinese text

天长地久。天地所以能长且久者，以其不自生，故能长生。是以圣人后其身而身先，外其身而身存。非以其无私邪？故能成其私。

## Pinyin

Tiān cháng dì jiǔ. Tiāndì suǒyǐ néng cháng qiě jiǔ zhě, yǐ qí bù zì shēng, gù néng cháng shēng. Shì yǐ shèngrén hòu qí shēn ér shēn xiān, wài qí shēn ér shēn cún. Fēi yǐ qí wúsī yé? Gù néng chéng qí sī.

## Literal rendering

Heaven is long, earth enduring. The reason heaven and earth can be long and enduring is that they do not give life to themselves — so they can give long life. Therefore the sage puts his person behind, and his person comes first; puts his person outside, and his person is preserved. Is it not because he has no self-interest? So he completes his self-interest.

## Term-by-term notes

- **The example is not a metaphor.** 不自生 — "does not give life to itself": heaven and earth persist precisely because they are not in the business of their own persistence. The chapter opens with the book's [ziran claim](/concepts/ziran/) in its most austere form: what lasts is what is not trying to last. The [ch. 2 pairs](/translations/daodejing-02/) — being and non-being rising together — are here applied to *time*: selfless and lasting are the same motion.
- **The two reversals.** 后其身而身先 (put the person behind — it comes first), 外其身而身存 (put the person outside — it is preserved): the chapter's double inversion, each verb answered by its opposite. The cadence is the book's signature — the site holds the same turn in [ch. 22](/translations/daodejing-22/) (bent, then whole) and [ch. 8](/translations/daodejing-08/) (does not contend, so no fault) — and this chapter is its purest single verse: the self abandoned is the self kept.
- **The paradox formula, stated honestly.** 非以其无私邪？故能成其私 — the chapter ends on the word 私 (private interest) and claims that selflessness *completes* it. The commentary tradition splits here, and the site registers the split: the political reading (the ruler who yields stands first — the Taiwan Education Ministry's gloss, tied to the book's later chapter 66) and the mystical reading (forget the self entirely — Jiao Hong's Laozi yi, quoted in the same source, connecting to ch. 13's 吾所以有大患者，为吾有身: the self is the great trouble). The site's own line: read the chapter as the *ethics of non-claiming* — the practice-layer reading the [progress question](/experiences/questions/what-counts-as-progress/) applies — rather than as a technique for getting by giving up, which would make the last line a con.
- **The three-teachings note, registered.** The traditional commentators (Wang Huai, in the same dictionary entry) read the chapter beside the Confucian 毋意毋必毋固毋我 and the Buddhist breaking of self-attachment — the cross-tradition reading the site holds at arm's length per its [borrowing discipline](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/): the commentators' convergence is their own; the chapter itself is the [Daoist](/glossary/dao/) document.
- **The variants, registered.** The Mawangdui silk texts read 退其身 (steps back) for received 后其身 (places behind), and 不以其无私与 for 非以其无私邪 — printed in the verification sources; the reversals are unaffected.
