## Chinese text

将欲歙之，必固张之；将欲弱之，必固强之；将欲废之，必固兴之；将欲取之，必固与之。是谓微明。柔弱胜刚强。鱼不可脱于渊，国之利器不可以示人。

## Pinyin

Jiāng yù xī zhī, bì gù zhāng zhī; jiāng yù ruò zhī, bì gù qiáng zhī; jiāng yù fèi zhī, bì gù xīng zhī; jiāng yù qǔ zhī, bì gù yǔ zhī. Shì wèi wēimíng. Róuruò shèng gāngqiáng. Yú bù kě tuō yú yuān, guó zhī lìqì bù kě yǐ shì rén.

## Literal rendering

What is to be gathered in must first be spread open; what is to be weakened must first be strengthened; what is to be abolished must first be raised; what is to be taken must first be given. This is called the subtle brightness. The soft and weak overcomes the hard and strong. The fish must not leave the deep; the sharp tools of the state must not be shown to people.

## Term-by-term notes

- **The four reversals, one verb each.** 歙/张 (gather/spread), 弱/强 (weaken/strengthen), 废/兴 (abolish/raise), 取/与 (take/give): each pair runs the same direction — the *later* state is reached by way of its opposite first. The structure is the book's signature inversion cadence, the same machine as [ch. 22](/translations/daodejing-22/) (bent-then-whole) and [ch. 2](/translations/daodejing-02/) (the co-rising pairs), and the site reads the chapter's claim as *natural rhythm* — the tide that must recede to rise — before any application is drawn from it.
- **微明 — the subtle brightness.** The name the chapter gives its own doctrine: 微, faint/hidden; 明, light. The site holds it beside [葆光](/glossary/bao-guang/) (the light that is stored, not shown): both are the book's *hidden light* words — the brightness that works precisely because it is not displayed. The chapter's closing line says the same thing in state-language: what works must not be shown.
- **The disputed chapter, and the three readings.** This is the chapter the tradition has argued about longest, and the site registers all three readings without adjudicating: (1) the *statecraft reading* — Han Fei's Yu Lao and the Legalist tradition took the four reversals as the ruler's technique, and the Confucian critics of the book (most famously the Cheng brothers) called it the dark arts; (2) the *natural reading* — the pairs as the world's own rhythm (what is at its height begins its decline), the reading Wang Bi's line of commentary supports; (3) the *inner reading* — the later inner-alchemy tradition read 国之利器 as the practitioner's own inner instrument that must not be leaked outward. The [robber's chapter](/stories/dao-yi-you-dao/) in this site's archive quotes the closing line in exactly the statecraft sense — the book's own later chapter already knew how this chapter would be used.
- **The two images, and their two fates.** 鱼不可脱于渊 — the fish must not leave the deep: the site's [fish pages](/stories/fish-in-the-dry-spring/) run the same image in two registers (the stranded fish begging for water; the fish debating joy), and this chapter's use is the third — the ruler's *element*. 国之利器不可以示人 — the sharp tools of the state: the chapter's most quoted line, borrowed by [Robber Zhi's chapter](/stories/dao-yi-you-dao/) as political warning. The site's own discipline echoes it from the practice side: the [experience archive](/experiences/) documents the tradition's standing rule that inner work is not for display — the same line, held as [practice hygiene](/experiences/notes/exp-021-prostrations-channel-circuit/) rather than statecraft.
- **The variants, registered.** The received text reads 取之 (take); some editions read 夺之 (seize) — noted in the verification sources; the reversals are unaffected. The chapter is one of the book's most-quoted and most-argued; the site's translation keeps the verbs neutral (gather, spread, weaken, strengthen) so the three readings stay visible in the text itself.
