## Chinese text (opening + core line)

大道無形，生育天地；大道無情，運行日月；大道無名，長養萬物。吾不知其名，強名曰道。……人能常清靜，天地悉皆歸。

## Pinyin

Dàdào wú xíng, shēngyù tiāndì; dàdào wú qíng, yùnxíng rìyuè; dàdào wú míng, zhǎngyǎng wànwù. Wú bù zhī qí míng, qiǎng míng yuē Dào. …… Rén néng cháng qīngjìng, tiāndì xī jiē guī.

## Literal rendering

"The great Dao has no form, yet gives birth to heaven and earth; the great Dao has no feeling, yet moves the sun and moon; the great Dao has no name, yet nourishes the myriad things. I do not know its name, so I force a name on it and call it Dao. …… If people can be constantly clear and still, heaven and earth all return to them."

## Term-by-term problems

- **強名曰道 (force a name, call it Dao):** the text is quoting *Daodejing* ch. 25 almost verbatim — a deliberate borrowing that marks the scripture's genre (commentarial scripture built from the classic).
- **清靜 (qīngjìng, "clear and still"):** the two characters are the text's title and thesis: 清 = limpid/clear (of the spirit), 靜 = still/quiet (of the mind). "Purity and tranquility" is the common English rendering; "clarity and stillness" is recommended here because 清 in this text means *unmuddied* rather than ritually pure.
- **天地悉皆歸 (heaven and earth all return to [one]):** the promise at the heart of the text. The "return" is the spontaneous order of things settling when the mind settles — a traditional claim about the macrocosm-microcosm resonance, not an empirical statement.
- **無情 (no feeling):** the Dao's "emotionlessness" here is impartial operation (sun and moon run without preference), not coldness. The same text elsewhere makes the *Dao* the model of what the practitioner should resemble.

## Traditional usage

Chanted as part of the daily morning and evening recitations (玄門日誦早晚功課經) since at least the Ming canon — meaning this opening has been internalized by countless Daoists as a recitation formula, a fact about the text's function that matters for understanding its contemplative role.

## Western misunderstandings

- The negative theology ("no form, no feeling, no name") is sometimes read as nihilism. The triple "yet gives birth / moves / nourishes" makes it apophatic — the Dao is beyond predicates *because* it is the source, not because it is nothing.
- "Heaven and earth all return" is sometimes read as magical control over nature. In the text's own logic it is the *spontaneous* result of clarity — resonance, not command.

## Recommended rendering (this site)

"The great Dao has no form, yet gives birth to heaven and earth; has no feeling, yet moves the sun and moon; has no name, yet nourishes the myriad things. I do not know its name; forcing a name, I call it Dao. …… If people can be constantly clear and still, heaven and earth all return to them."
