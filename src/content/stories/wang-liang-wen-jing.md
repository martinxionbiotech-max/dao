## The story

The penumbra asked the shadow: "Just now you were walking, now you stop; just now you were sitting, now you rise. **Why no constancy of your own?**"

The shadow said: "Do I depend on something to be as I am? **Does what I depend on depend on something to be as it is?** Do I depend on the snake's belly-scales, the cicada's wings? **How would I know why it is so? How would I know why it is not?**"

## Framing: the chapter's penultimate scene, and what it refuses

- **The shortest fable, the hardest questions.** Four lines, three questions, no answer: the shadow does not *explain* its dependence; it walks the chain one link (吾有待而然者邪) and then a second (吾所待又有待而然者邪), names the obvious physical candidates (snake-scales, cicada-wings — the borrowed movement), and stops at the refusal: 恶识所以然？恶识所以不然？ — how would I know why it is so, or why it is not? The site's [tian-ji glossary](/glossary/tian-ji/) holds the term the commentators used for exactly this: Cheng Xuanying's 天机自张，莫知其宰 — "the heaven-mechanism spreads itself; no one knows its driver." The shadow is the book's figure for the same self-running the [centipede](/stories/kui-xian-chain/) described with ten thousand feet: moved, and no mover known.
- **The pair with the butterfly.** The passage sits immediately before the [butterfly dream](/stories/butterfly-dream/) — the chapter's two closing images, and the site now holds both: the **shadow** (the dependence question, refused) and the **butterfly** (the transformation question, left open as 物化). The order is the argument: first the chain of dependencies is walked and abandoned, *then* the dream is possible — the book's "how would I know" is the doorway into its "now I do not know which is which."
- **The shadow answers the chapter's opening.** The study the site verified reads the fable as the book answering its own first scene: [南郭子綦's 吾丧我](/translations/qiwulun-wu-sang-wo/) — "I have lost myself" — gets its round halo here, because the shadow *at ease in its dependence* (虽看似有待却如无待, "seemingly dependent, yet as if without dependence") is what "losing the self" looks like from the outside: not independence, but the end of the anxiety about dependence. Lu Deming's one-character gloss (丧, "loss") and Liu Fengbao's reading are recorded as the tradition's two anchors; the study's own reading is recorded as scholarly interpretation.
- **The penumbra is the anxious one.** 罔两 (the penumbra — the shadow's own shadow, the faint edge) scolds the shadow for having no 特操 (constancy of its own) — and the fable's comedy is that the *least* substantial thing in the room demands autonomy. The chain of the anxious: each one blames the next one down for being moved, and the fable's last word is the moved one's refusal to claim knowledge of the mover.

## Why it belongs in this knowledge base

Because it is the tradition's meditation on dependence itself — the site's [practice questions](/questions/what-is-stillness/) constantly meet this exact scolding ("why can't I keep my sitting steady?"), and the shadow's answer is the tradition's: the sitting is moved by what moves it, and the question of the mover ends in 恶识 — "how would I know?" — which is not ignorance but the end of the search.

**Uncertainty preserved:** an *inner* chapter passage (earliest stratum), verified against two editions; the three traditional readings (Lu Deming, Cheng Xuanying, Liu Fengbao) are recorded side by side without adjudication, per the [disagreement rule](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/); the modern reading follows the verified PKU study, marked as scholarly interpretation.
