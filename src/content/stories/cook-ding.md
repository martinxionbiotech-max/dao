## The story

A cook named Ding was carving an ox for Lord Wenhui. Every touch of the hand, every lean of the shoulder, every step of the foot, every bend of the knee — crack, whoosh — the blade went through with a sound like music, matching the dance of the Mulberry Grove, keeping time with the Jingshou movement.

The lord, astonished, said: "Ah! Wonderful! How has skill reached this point?"

Ding laid down his knife and answered: "What your servant loves is the Dao, which is beyond mere skill. When I first began carving oxen, I saw nothing but whole oxen. After three years, I no longer saw the whole ox. Now I meet it with the spirit and do not look with the eyes; perception and knowing have stopped, and the spirit moves as it will. Following heaven's own grain, I enter the great crevices, guide through the great hollows, going by what is already so. I have never touched the ligaments and tendons, let alone the great bones.

"A good cook changes his knife once a year — he cuts. A mediocre cook changes it once a month — he hacks. My knife has served nineteen years and carved several thousand oxen, and its blade is as fresh as from the whetstone. The joints have spaces, and the blade has no thickness; what has no thickness enters what has space — how generously the blade wanders, with room to spare. That is why after nineteen years the blade is as new.

"Still, at every knot of muscle and bone, where the work is difficult, I grow wary and alert. My gaze stops, my movements slow; I move the blade minutely — and with a sigh of release the ox is undone, like earth crumbling to the ground. Then I stand with the knife raised, look all around, linger in satisfaction, wipe the blade, and put it away."

Lord Wenhui said: "Excellent! From Cook Ding's words I have learned how to nourish life."

## Framing: what this story is

This is a **parable** — one of the Zhuangzi's invented dialogues (the king of Wei is a stock character; the cook's name means simply "the cook named Ding"). The chapter it opens, *Yangsheng zhu* ("The Master of Nourishing Life"), is not a butchering manual; the ox is the world's complexity, the blade is the mind or self, and the lesson is the book's core contemplative claim: **skill matures from cutting to following** — from seeing things as solid wholes, to perceiving their natural seams, to moving by "what is already so" (固然) rather than by force.

## Why it belongs in this knowledge base

Cook Ding's three-stage account — whole ox, no whole ox, spirit-movement without eyes — is the Zhuangzi's most practical description of what trained attention becomes: the beginner meets a solid object; the practiced hand meets structure; the mature practitioner meets *space*, and the work happens in the spaces. The sitting analogues are explicit elsewhere in the book: "listening stops at the ears; the mind stops at matching; qi is empty and waits on things" (see the [xinzhai translation](/translations/xinzhai-passage/)). Cook Ding is xinzhai applied to work — the same non-forcing described with a knife instead of a breath.

**Uncertainty preserved:** the story is literature, not biography; nothing suggests a historical Cook Ding. Its teaching status rests on the tradition's two-millennium reading, recorded here as such.
