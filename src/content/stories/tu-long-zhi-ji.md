## The story

Zhu Pingman studied dragon-slaying under Zhili Yi, spent his family's thousand gold to the last coin, and after three years had the skill complete — and no way to use his craft (朱泙漫学屠龙于支离益，单千金之家，三年技成而无所用其巧).

That is the whole story: three sentences, one joke. And the chapter around it supplies the answer — a few lines earlier: "Knowing the Way is easy; not speaking it is hard. Know it and do not speak — that is the way of heaven; know it and speak — that is the way of men." And a few lines later, the chapter's own portrait of the alternative: "The skilled labor, the clever worry — the one without ability has nothing to seek; fed and full, he wanders at ease, drifting like an unmoored boat (汎若不系之舟)."

## Framing: the joke and its two readings

- **The name is the joke's first half.** 朱泙漫 — a made-up name with no pedigree, and 支离益 — "the Crippled Gain": his teacher belongs to the book's 支离 family (the [cripple Shu](/stories/zhili-shu/) of ch. 4, whose brokenness is his survival). To pay a crippled master for the skill of killing what does not exist, until the family fortune is gone (单=殚, spent to the bottom) — the chapter is a *miscellaneous chapter* (declared per the [layering rule](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/)), and this is its comedy-of-waste in three lines: the [Lord Ren fishing](/stories/ren-gongzi-fishing/) story's exact mirror. Ren baited for a fish that existed and came; Pingman trained for a dragon that does not and never will. Same scale of commitment, opposite scale of object.
- **The two readings, registered.** The tradition splits, and the site keeps both without adjudicating: (1) the *plain reading* — do not spend your substance on skills with no object; the idiom 屠龙之技 survives in modern Chinese with exactly this sense (impressive technique, no practical use); (2) the *Daoist reading*, noted in the close-reading source — the plain reading ("learn useful skills") is advice the book would never give; the story sits between "knowing the Way is easy, not-speaking is hard" and "the skilled labor, the clever worry" — so the *completed-and-unused* skill is the chapter's portrait of 得道无为: the craft finished, and then not drawn, because the dragons are gone. The site notes that the second reading is the tradition's own defense of the joke's placement, and that the idiom has long since settled on the first.
- **The chapter's answer: the unmoored boat.** 巧者劳而知者忧，无能者无所求，饱食而敖游，汎若不系之舟 — the skilled labor, the clever worry; the abilityless seek nothing; fed, they drift like an unmoored boat. The chapter's alternative to Pingman is not a *better* skill but no skill at all — the same answer as the [useless tree](/stories/useless-gourd/), now stated as a human type. The site holds the line against its own [craft pages](/stories/chui-gou-zhe/): the book honors the hook-forger's *kept* skill and laughs at the dragon-slayer's *objectless* skill — the difference is not skillfulness but what the skill is *for*. The [progress question](/experiences/questions/what-counts-as-progress/) takes the point: a practice whose object is real is a practice; a stage pursued for its own certificate is a dragon.
- **The drift, clean and instructive.** 屠龙之技 today means "impressive but unusable expertise" — the idiom kept the joke and dropped the chapter's second reading. The site registers it per its [idiom discipline](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/): what modern usage lost is the joke's other half — that the book which laughs at unusable skill also praises the abilityless man, and that the difference between the two is not the skill but the object.
