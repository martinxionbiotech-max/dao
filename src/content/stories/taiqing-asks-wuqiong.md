## The story

**Taiqing asked the Infinite: do you know the dao?** The Infinite said: *I do not know.* He then asked the Non-Doing, and the Non-Doing said: *I know the dao.* — And do you know it with a number? — Yes. — What is the number? — *I know the dao can be noble, can be base, can be gathered, can be dispersed. This is the number by which I know the dao.* (吾知道之可以貴，可以賤，可以約，可以散，此吾所以知道之數也).

So Taiqing took the Non-Doing's answer to the Beginningless and asked for the verdict: between the Infinite's not-knowing and the Non-Doing's knowing, *which is right and which is wrong?* The Beginningless's ruling is the chapter's second-ranking system stated in four plain words: **not-knowing is deep, knowing is shallow; not-knowing is inside, knowing is outside** (不知，深矣；知之，淺矣。弗知，內矣；知之，外矣). Then Taiqing raises his head — the received text says 中而叹, sighed in the middle; Lu Deming's textual note records that the Cui edition reads 卬, raised his head — and delivers the chapter's sharpest line, which is also its best-known proverb:

**弗知乃知乎！知乃不知乎！孰知不知之知？** — *Not-knowing, that is knowing! Knowing, that is not-knowing! Who knows the knowing of not-knowing?*

The Beginningless is not finished. Three negations follow, each in the same form: **the dao cannot be heard — what is heard is not it; the dao cannot be seen — what is seen is not it; the dao cannot be spoken — what is spoken is not it.** And the turn that ties the knot: *do you understand that what forms the formed is itself unformed? The dao does not fit a name* (知形形之不形乎，道不當名). The last paragraph escalates from the object to the question itself: *one who answers a question about the dao does not know the dao. Even one who asks has not yet heard the dao. The dao has no question, and a question has no answer. To ask what cannot be asked is to ask into emptiness; to answer what cannot be answered is to have nothing within* (道无問，問无應。无問問之，是問窮也；无應應之，是无內也). And the closing sentence draws the map of the failure: whoever does this *does not see the cosmos from outside and does not know the great beginning from inside — that is why he never gets past Mount Kunlun, never roams the great void* (不過乎崐崘，不遊乎大虛).

## Why it is told here

Because this is the chapter's **second ranking of knowledge**, set one step deeper than the first. The chapter opened with [Zhi asking Wuweiwei](/stories/zhi-wen-wuwei/) — three questions about knowing the dao, answered by silence, by a forgotten answer, and by the Yellow Emperor's eloquent reply, with the verdict that the silent one was truly right and the eloquent one farthest away. That first story ranked *speech against silence*. This one ranks *claims against each other*, and the winner is not even silence but a *denial*: the Infinite's flat 吾不知 — I do not know. The word 无穷 (infinite) is itself the plot: the dao's own name for itself is the one who says he does not know it.

- **The numbers are the trap.** The Non-Doing's answer is not wrong content — noble, base, gathered, dispersed, as Cheng Xuanying's commentary glosses it: valuable as emperor, base as servant; gathered into life, dispersed into death. It is a *list*, and the list is what disqualifies it. The verdict says the person with a numbered knowledge is *outside* the thing he knows; the one with no numbers is *inside*. This is the site's [dao](/concepts/dao/) page in narrative form: the dao that can be spoken is not the constant dao — the Laozi's opening line is exactly what the Non-Doing did not learn.
- **The questioner gets the best line.** 弗知乃知乎 — the sigh that became a proverb — is Taiqing's, not a sage's. The chapter lets the person who asked the naive question complete the theory himself, the same structure as the [Dongguo Zi dialogue](/stories/dongguo-zi-asks/), where the puzzled questioner's escalating 何其下邪 pushes the answer down to excrement. The book's questioners are never fools; they are the engine.
- **Three negations, one grammar.** Cannot be heard, seen, spoken — each verb takes its sense-organ and closes it. The [light-brightness story](/stories/light-brightness-asks-nothingness/) that follows in the same chapter is the same grammar done with eyes: he *looked* at the answer and saw nothing, and called it the summit. And 道不当名, the dao does not fit a name, is the site's [translation policy](/blog/translation-policy/) as a six-word theorem — the reason every translation page on this site marks its terms as recommendations, not equivalences.
- **Kunlun is the ceiling.** The final sentence is not mystical travel-writing; it is a diagnosis. 昆仑, the world's highest mountain in the geography of the day, stands for the boundary of the visible; 太虚, the great void, for what lies past it. To ask a question that cannot be asked is to stay permanently on this side of the mountain — the chapter says the wrong question is not just useless, it is a *location*, and a low one.

**The two rankings, side by side.** The chapter opened with a story where speaking too well was the error (the Yellow Emperor); it closes this one where speaking at all is the error. Between them sits the whole epistemology: silence beats speech, denial beats list, and the sigh of a confused student beats both.
