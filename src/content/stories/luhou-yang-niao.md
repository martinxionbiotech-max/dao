## The story

Once a seabird alighted outside the Lu capital. The Marquis of Lu welcomed it into the ancestral temple and offered it wine; he had the *Nine Shao* dances performed to delight it, and laid out a full ox-sheep-pig sacrifice as its food. The bird — eyes dazed, grieving — could not eat a single morsel, could not drink a single cup, and in three days it died.

**This is raising the bird by one's own way of raising oneself — not the bird's way of raising the bird.**

## Framing: the hospitality that kills

- **The kindest cruelty in the book.** No one in this story is evil. The marquis does *everything* a Lu court knows how to do for an honored guest — and the passage's verdict is the book's sharpest: 此以己养养鸟也，非以鸟养养鸟也 — *raising the bird by the way you raise yourself, not by the bird's way of raising a bird.* The [hundun story](/stories/hundun/) is this fable's cosmic twin (gratitude that bores holes and kills); the seabird is its everyday twin: hospitality that never once asked what a seabird needs. The two pages bracket the book's warning about well-meaning intervention — one comic-royal, one cosmic.
- **The Nine Shao detail.** The music named is the *Shao*, the ceremonial music of the sage-emperor Shun — the highest thing Lu culture possessed, offered with both hands. The passage's point is that the *height* of the offering is the weight of it: the same gift that would honor a Lu guest is exactly what a seabird cannot bear. The site's [ziran concept page](/concepts/ziran/) holds the general form: each thing runs itself; the marquis's error is the running of someone else's nature by his own.
- **The bird-family, continued.** The site's [bird pages](/stories/peng-bird/) now hold four: the Peng (dependence made visible), the [phoenix](/stories/phoenix-and-owl/) (refusal as diet), the [marsh pheasant](/stories/marsh-pheasant/) (freedom at the price of hardship — ten pecks, a hundred drinks, no cage), and this seabird — the one that dies when freedom is *replaced* by privilege. The marsh pheasant chooses hardship over the cage; the seabird is shown what the cage looks like when the cage is a temple.
- **The idiom.** 鲁侯养鸟 survives in modern Chinese as the standing phrase for "smothering kindness," "loving something in the wrong way" — one of the site's [idiom records](/stories/fish-in-the-dry-spring/), and here the meaning has **not drifted**: the modern phrase still means what the passage meant, which the site flags as the rarer case.
- **Against the Analects' echo.** A modern teaching essay pairs this passage with Confucius's 己所不欲，勿施于人 ("do not do to others what you would not want done to you") and extends it: 己所欲，亦勿施于人 — "what you *do* want, do not force on others either." The extension is the essayist's, recorded as modern commentary — but the pairing is fair and the site keeps it, because the seabird is precisely the case the Analects' negative rule cannot catch: the marquis would *love* the Nine Shao. The bird's death is the positive rule's shadow.

## Why it belongs in this knowledge base

Because it is the tradition's cleanest statement of the [practice boundaries](/experiences/questions/can-sitting-go-wrong/) the site polices everywhere: the best practice for *you* may be the death of someone else's — the site's posture doctrine (the tradition's own graded system of [sitting forms](/glossary/jiafuzuo/)) is this passage applied to knees, and its teacher-warning pages are this passage applied to advice.

**Uncertainty preserved:** an *outer* chapter passage (later stratum, declared per the [layering rule](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/)); the marquis's death-by-hospitality is fable, not chronicle; the Analects pairing is a modern essayist's extension, recorded as such.
