## The story

Yan He is to become tutor to the heir-apparent of Wei — a young man whose nature, he says, "is to kill." To instruct him with principle endangers the state; to go along with him endangers Yan He himself. The wise Qu Boyu answers: first, straighten yourself; outwardly **comply** without entering, inwardly **harmonize** without emerging — go along with him, but never let the going-along in. Then three fables, in one breath:

"Have you never heard of the **mantis**? It rages and raises its arms against the chariot-track, not knowing it cannot bear the task — *it is the pride of its own talent*. Careful, careful! To pile up your brilliance and press it on him is danger. Have you never heard of the **tiger-keeper**? He dares not feed the tiger live prey, lest the killing rouse its rage; dares not give it whole prey, lest the tearing rouse it. He times its hunger and fullness, and follows its fierce nature through. The tiger is a different species from man, yet it fawns on its keeper — because he goes *with* its nature; those it kills are those who go *against* it. And the **horse-lover**? He caught the droppings in a fine basket, the urine in a jeweled shell — and when a gadfly lit on the horse and he swatted it at the wrong moment, the horse snapped its bit, smashed its headstall, burst its chest-strap. **The intent was love, and the love was lost.** How can you not be careful!"

## Framing: the fable's original target is the talented reformer

The idiom's modern life — 螳臂当车, "the mantis blocking the chariot," the standard insult for foolhardy overreach — is a **drift from the source**, and the page keeps both senses:

- **In the source, the mantis is admired.** The clause 是其才之美者也 — "it is the pride of its own talent" — makes the mantis the image of exactly the person the passage is trying to save: Yan He, the *talented and well-meaning* tutor. The danger is not stupidity but brilliance: talent presses itself on the unteachable and is destroyed. The idiom dictionaries and the state's own study column record the drift: the *Huainanzi* retold the story and made the mantis a hero of courage (Duke Zhuang of Qi swerves his chariot for it); the modern derogatory sense grew out of the single word 不胜任, "cannot bear the task."
- **The tiger-keeper is the alternative to the mantis.** Against the unteachable prince, Qu Boyu's program is the keeper's: 时其饥饱，达其怒心 — *time* the hunger, *follow* the nature. No reform by confrontation; the going-with (就) is a camouflage for the going-under (和), and both are disciplines of restraint: 就不欲入，和不欲出 — comply without entering, harmonize without emerging. This is the chapter's practical [wuwei](/concepts/wuwei/): influence through timing, not force.
- **The horse-lover is the warning against love itself.** 意有所至而爱有所亡 — the intent reaches, and the love is lost. Even kindness, mistimed, breaks what it holds. The three fables are one arc: talent (the mantis), method (the keeper), and motive (the lover) — the three ways a good person fails a bad situation, and the chapter's answer to each.

## Why it belongs in this knowledge base

Because the site's own counsel pages — the [sitting protocol](/tools/sitting-protocol/), the [posture question](/questions/must-i-sit-cross-legged/) — are keeper's manuals, not mantis manuals: they work by timing and following, and they exist because the tradition itself recorded what happens to the brilliant who press. The [Hundun story](/stories/hundun/) is this chapter's distant cousin — improvement administered as force — and the mantis is its personal-scale version.
