## The story

Kui pities the centipede; the centipede pities the snake; the snake pities the wind; the wind pities the eye; the eye pities the mind.

Kui said to the centipede: "I hop along on my one foot — there's nothing I can do about it. You drive ten thousand feet — how do you manage?" The centipede said: "Not so. You've seen a man spit? Sprayed, the big drops are like pearls, the small like mist, falling mixed, beyond count. I move **my heaven-mechanism** and do not know how it works."

The centipede said to the snake: "I walk with a host of feet and can't keep up with you, who have none. Why?" The snake said: "How can what **the heaven-mechanism moves** be exchanged? What use would I have for feet?"

The snake said to the wind: "I move by working my spine and ribs — at least I have a form. You rise roaring from the North Sea and roar into the South Sea, and yet you seem to have nothing. How?" The wind said: "True — I rise roaring from the North Sea into the South Sea. But point a finger at me and you defeat me; kick at me and you defeat me. And yet — **breaking great trees, blowing great roofs over, only I can do that.**"

**So: win the great victory by losing many small contests. To win the great victory, only the sage can do it.**

## Framing: the chapter's coda, and the two answers

- **The chain's grammar.** Each link pities the one below it — one foot, ten thousand feet, no feet, no form — and each answer is the same word: **天机** (the heaven-mechanism). The centipede moves it without knowing how; the snake says it cannot be exchanged. The [ziran concept page](/concepts/ziran/) holds the family: what is given works *itself*, and the question "how do you manage" is the wrong question — the point is precisely that nobody manages.
- **The wind's arithmetic.** The wind concedes both small defeats — the finger and the kick defeat it — and then claims the great victory: breaking trees, lifting roofs. 以众小不胜为大胜: the passage's own formula, stated after the dialogue as its conclusion. The tradition's reading (the [Shidian guji commentary](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/), quoted in verification) is that the small defeats are not tolerated but *conceded* — 著于体者相怜 (those bound to body pity each other's bodies) versus the wind, 有体而实无体 (having a body yet really bodiless), which is the only one that wins the great victory. The site keeps the formula because it is the [wuwei](/concepts/wuwei/) logic written as contest-rules: win by not entering the contest.
- **Why the chain stops.** The wind pities the eye; the eye pities the mind — and there the text goes silent: no dialogue, no answer. The commentary's note (verified on the passage) reads this as deliberate: the eye sees in a glance what the wind reaches only by roaring, and the mind "turns and in a blink has cradled the four seas — beyond all description, so nothing is said." The book's own chain ends at the thing that cannot be asked about: the mind, which has no form to pity and no mechanism to name.
- **Where it sits in the chapter.** The [river-god's lesson](/stories/wangyang-xingtan/) — the chapter's long opening dialogue — teaches scale through the sea's humility; the pity chain is the chapter's *coda*, teaching the same lesson through bodies: 夔 is one foot, and the chain dissolves body-logic step by step until body-logic itself is left behind. The chapter that began with the biggest water ends with the smallest creatures' self-description — and the [peng-bird](/stories/peng-bird/)'s ladder from ch. 1 is its mirror image: there the small mock the big; here the small pity the smaller, and the chain runs the other way, down to no form at all.

## Why it belongs in this knowledge base

Because it is the tradition's anti-ranking argument in pure form: every body is a different mechanism, none is the right one, and the one that wins is the one that has nothing to compare — the site's [practice pages](/practices/jingzuo/) are written on exactly this refusal to rank one sitting body above another.

**Uncertainty preserved:** an *outer* chapter passage (the [layering rule](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/) applies — not the earliest stratum); the figures are fable (the Shanhai jing's one-footed Kui is noted by the commentary as the creature's source); the closing formula's reading follows the commentary, recorded as tradition, not as the text's own statement.
