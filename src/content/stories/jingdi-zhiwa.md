## The story

The chapter opens with the autumn floods. The river god — proud that "all the beauty under heaven is in me" — reaches the sea and discovers he is nothing; and the spirit of the Northern Sea teaches him with the chapter's famous maxim:

"You cannot speak of the sea to a well frog — it is **bound by its hollow**. You cannot speak of ice to a summer insect — it is **bound by its season**. You cannot speak of the Way to a village scholar — he is **bound by his schooling**. Now that you have come out past your banks and seen the great sea, you know your own smallness, and I can begin to speak of great principles with you."

And the same chapter ends with the fable itself, told by Prince Mou to the logician Gongsun Long: the frog of a caved-in well, who bragged to a sea turtle of the East about his kingdom — hopping along the well's rim, resting in the hollow of its broken bricks, swimming in water that came up to his armpits, mud up to his ankles, "lord of a whole gully of water — what happiness could top this? Why don't you come in and see?" The turtle tried. His **left foot went in before his right knee stuck** on the edge, and he backed away. He told the frog of the sea: a thousand leagues cannot hold its breadth, a thousand fathoms its depth; in Yu's time there were nine floods in ten years and it did not rise; in Tang's time eight droughts in seven years and its shore did not fall. "That," he said, "is the great joy of the Eastern Sea." And the well frog, hearing it, was shocked to the bone, and lost in himself.

## Framing: the limits are the structure, not the insult

The modern idiom 井底之蛙 ("a frog at the bottom of a well") is an insult — a name for someone whose ignorance is also complacency. The source passage is doing something different, and the page keeps the difference:

- **The three limits are the anatomy of all knowing.** 拘于虚 — bound by place; 笃于时 — bound by season; 束于教 — bound by schooling. Every knower stands inside all three: the frog's well is a *condition*, not a defect — and the chapter's opening movement (the river god learning his smallness from the sea) puts *us* in the frog's position, not the turtle's. The tradition's [ziran](/concepts/ziran/) claim inherits the point: knowledge is situated, and the first move of wisdom is discovering one's own well.
- **The turtle's knee is the whole argument in one image.** 左足未入而右膝已絷 — the sea cannot *fit* into the well. The truth doesn't fail to be true; it fails to enter. The fable's real subject is the *conveyance* problem the [Handan walk](/stories/handan-walk/) tells from the other side: between two worlds of different size, neither can move into the other's frame.
- **The frog's shock is recorded, not mocked.** 适适然惊，规规然自失 — "shocked to the bone, and lost in himself": the fable ends with the frog's genuine disorientation, the exact moment the river god had at the sea. Even the smallest frame can receive the news — and that reception is the chapter's definition of the teachable moment, the same 从容 the [fish debate](/stories/fish-happiness/) names.

## Why it belongs in this knowledge base

Because the knowledge base itself is built on the maxim's third limit — 束于教, bound by schooling — and exists to widen the well: the [reading order](/tools/reading-order/), the [source policy](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/), and every [comparison page](/comparisons/zuowang-vs-mindfulness/) are the site's version of the sea turtle's report, told as something the frog can receive.
