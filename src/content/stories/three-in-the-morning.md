## The story

A keeper of monkeys gave out chestnuts and said: "Three in the morning, four in the evening." The monkeys were furious.

"All right," he said, "four in the morning, three in the evening." The monkeys were delighted.

The book's own gloss: **the name and the amount lost nothing, yet anger and delight changed places** (名实未亏而喜怒为用) — and so the sage "reconciles right and wrong and rests on the potter's wheel of heaven" (和之以是非而休乎天钧) — **"this is called walking two roads at once"** (是之谓两行).

## Framing: the fable's own diagnosis

The story sits inside one of the book's hardest passages — the argument that "those who strain the spirit to make things one, without knowing they are already the same, are called 'three in the morning'" (劳神明为一而不知其同也，谓之朝三). Three things make it a sitting-practice text, not just a logic joke:

- **The keeper's trick is the mind's own trick.** The monkeys are not fooled by a lie; they are fooled by *the arrangement*. Everything real stayed constant — total chestnuts, keeper, monkeys — and emotion flipped entirely on sequence. The fable's target is the mind's factory setting: preference for arrangement over substance, for the *shape* of the offer over the offer. The [zuochi](/glossary/zuochi/) page documents the same machine at full run in sitting: the mind galloping through arrangements while nothing real changes.
- **"Two roads at once" (两行) is the stilled alternative.** The sage's move is not to pick the right arrangement but to *stop needing one*: right and wrong are harmonized and rested on the potter's wheel (天钧 — the leveling wheel that is the sky). The image is equilibrium as rotation: the wheel turns, the center holds. The [jing](/concepts/jing/) page's definition — the mind moving without being moved — is this image made a practice instruction.
- **The keeper is the teacher.** The fable's unstated punchline: the monkeys could be freed by *seeing* the trick, and seeing it is exactly what [guan](/glossary/guan/) and the sitting tradition train. The chestnuts, like the [fish](/stories/fish-happiness/)'s joy, were there to be seen the whole time.

## The idiom's drift, noted

The phrase 朝三暮四 has since drifted in modern usage to mean "fickle, changeable, inconsistent." The Zhuangzi's own use is nearly the opposite: not changeableness but *changelessness misread* — the same total, angrily refused, then joyfully accepted. This page keeps the original sense, per the [translation policy](/blog/translation-policy/): the source text's meaning first, the idiom's later career second.

**Uncertainty preserved:** an *inner* chapter passage (the earliest stratum — see the [compilation timeline](/timeline/zhuangzi-compilation/)); a fable, not a report; and the surrounding argument (the "making things one" passage) is among the most debated in the book — the page's reading is the traditional one, stated as such.

The chapter term this anecdote supports is [Moruo Yiming (莫若以明)](/glossary/mo-ruo-yi-ming/).
