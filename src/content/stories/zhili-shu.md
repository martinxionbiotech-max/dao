## The story

Zhili Shu — his chin was hidden in his navel, his shoulders rose above the crown of his head, his topknot pointed at the sky, his five organs crowded upward, his two thighs doubled as his ribs. He sewed and washed clothes, and it was enough to feed himself; he winnowed and sifted grain, and it was enough to feed ten.

When the state called up soldiers, Zhili Shu strolled among the recruiters, sleeves rolled up. When the state levied great corvées, Zhili Shu was excused, his permanent infirmity a fact of record. When the state distributed grain to the sick, he drew his allotment — three zhong of millet and ten bundles of firewood.

And the chapter's verdict: **"One whose form is broken can still nourish his own body and live out his natural years — how much more one whose virtue is broken?"** (夫支离其形者，犹足以养其身，终其天年，又况支离其德者乎！)

And the chapter ends, after the madman of Chu's song to Confucius, with its own summary: the hill tree destroys itself, the lamp-oil fries itself; the cassia is felled because it is edible, the lacquer tree is cut because it is usable. **"Everyone knows the use of the useful, and no one knows the use of the useless."** (人皆知有用之用，而莫知无用之用也。)

## Framing: the deformed body as the safe body

- **The draft and the hunchback.** The chapter (ch. 4, the book's chapter on surviving the human world) runs one proof twice, and this passage is the second half: the [scrap trees](/stories/mountain-tree-goose/) and the [useless gourd](/stories/useless-gourd/) are the vegetable form of the argument; Zhili Shu is its human form. The body that cannot be drafted cannot be killed in battle; the form that cannot do corvée cannot be worked to death — and the passage's comedy is its precision: the same brokenness that exempts him from duty *entitles* him to relief. 攘臂而游于其间 — he strolls through the conscription crowd *with sleeves rolled up*, the one man who cannot be taken making a point of his presence. The [layer discipline](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/) applies: this is an *inner* chapter, the book's earliest stratum, so the argument is not a later school's gloss but the book's own.
- **支离其德 — the twist that outruns the fable.** The verdict's last clause is the passage's real engine, and the sub-commentary the site verified (Cheng Xuanying's) unpacks it: 支离其形，犹忘形也；支离其德，犹忘德也 — "broken in form is the same as having *forgotten the form*; broken in virtue is the same as having *forgotten virtue*." The fable begins as survival strategy (the deformed body is safe) and ends as practice doctrine (the deliberately broken virtue is *freer*): the [zuowang concept](/concepts/zuowang/) is this clause's descendant, and the [liang-wang glossary](/glossary/liang-wang/) (两忘, the two forgettings) is its proper name. The site keeps the two readings of 支离其德 registered together — the irony (let your virtue look broken, and no one will conscript it) and the practice (forget form and virtue alike) — without choosing, because the text itself runs on both.
- **The chapter's double ending.** The passage is followed in the same chapter by the madman of Chu's song (凤兮凤兮 — "phoenix, phoenix, how your virtue has decayed!"), and the chapter closes with the tree-oil-cassia-lacquer series. The site registers the structure: ch. 4 proves the use of uselessness *three times* — the deformed man, the madman's warning, the four dying things — and the phrase the whole tradition now quotes, 无用之用 ("the use of the useless"), is the chapter's own last line. The [mountain-tree-goose page](/stories/mountain-tree-goose/) (ch. 20) holds the argument's late complication — between the useless goose and the useless tree, *position* decides — and the two pages are cross-linked as the book's first and last word on the theme.
- **What the site will not do with it.** The fable is not a celebration of disability and not a life-hack manual; the site keeps it as what it is — the book's most uncomfortable application of its own doctrine, in which the deformed body's safety indicts the world that drafts the sound ones. The modern idiom survives intact (支离破碎, "shattered, broken to pieces" — from this very passage's word 支离, via the received reading), and the site notes the drift honestly: the modern word kept the *brokenness* and lost the *safety*.

## Why it belongs in this knowledge base

Because it is the book's own answer to the question the site's [practice pages](/practices/zuowang/) meet constantly — *what is the useless body of practice good for?* — and the answer is the chapter's: for living out the years it has, without being conscripted by everything that wants a piece of it.

**Uncertainty preserved:** an *inner* chapter passage (ch. 4, earliest stratum, declared per the [layering rule](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/)); two independent texts agree, with the navel-character and winnowing-phrase variants recorded, not adjudicated; Cheng Xuanying's sub-commentary is quoted as commentary; the passage is rendered as fable, its practice reading registered as the tradition's, not the book's own assertion.
