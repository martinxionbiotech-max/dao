## The story

There was a man of Song named Cao Shang, sent by the King of Song on a mission to Qin. He went with a few chariots; the King of Qin took to him and added a hundred more. Back in Song, he came to Zhuangzi and said: "As for dwelling in a cramped lane, poor, weaving sandals, with a gaunt neck and a sallow face — that is what I, Shang, am short at. One awakening of a ten-thousand-chariot lord, and a hundred chariots follow — that is what I am long at."

Zhuangzi said: "When the King of Qin falls ill, he summons physicians. The one who lances his boils gets one chariot; the one who licks his piles gets five chariots. **The lower the place cured, the more chariots.** Did you cure his piles? How else so many chariots? On your way!"

## Framing: the price list

- **The satire's mechanism is an index.** 所治愈下，得车愈多 — the lower the place, the higher the pay — is the whole argument, and it needs no adjective: payment is indexed to degradation, and Cao Shang's own boast supplies the index. Five chariots for the lowest place: the book's arithmetic of servility, stated as a fee schedule.
- **The boast contains the truth.** Cao Shang's portrait of Zhuangzi — 织屦 (weaving sandals), 槁项黄馘 (gaunt neck, sallow face) — is the book's poverty self-portrait delivered by an enemy, and the insult is accurate: the book lets it stand. What gets answered is not the poverty but the pride — the description is conceded, the valuation is not.
- **The awakening-word in the flatterer's mouth.** Cao Shang's 一悟万乘之主 takes the book's own word for awakening and claims it for winning a lord's favor. The reply's structure is re-description: what you call awakening, the price list calls licking. The satire is not name-calling; it is a translation table between two vocabularies for the same trip.
- **子行矣 — on your way.** The shortest ending in the book: no argument, no consolation — a price list and a door. Against the [river warden](/stories/hezhe-zhi-fu/) (who meant well and was shown the structure of deferral) and the [Marquis of Lu](/stories/luhou-yang-niao/) (who meant well and killed a bird with kindness), Cao Shang is the book's one full portrait of the man who *took the job* — and the door closes behind him.
- **The worth-arithmetic family.** The site's refusal pages hold the other side of this coin: the [turtle in the mud](/stories/turtle-in-the-mud/) (the office refused — the turtle chooses mud over the temple), the [phoenix and the owl](/stories/phoenix-and-owl/) (the minister's suspicion — an owl guarding a rotting rat), the [marsh pheasant](/stories/marsh-pheasant/) (freedom costs ten steps per peck). Cao Shang completes the set: the chariots exist, and so does their price.
- **The idiom, intact.** 舐痔 survives in modern Chinese (吮痈舐痔 — shameless flattery of the powerful) with its meaning **unbent** — like the [dried-up rut](/stories/hezhe-zhi-fu/)'s carp, the image is too precise for twenty-three centuries of use to soften it.
