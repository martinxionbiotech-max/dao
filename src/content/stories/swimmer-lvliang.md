## The story

Confucius was viewing the Lüliang falls: the water plunges thirty fathoms, its spray travels forty li, and no turtle, crocodile, fish, or softshell can swim there. He saw a man swimming in it — took him for one in pain who wished to die — and sent his disciples along the bank to rescue him. Some hundred paces downstream the man came out, hair streaming, and strolled along the bank singing.

Confucius followed and asked: "I took you for a ghost; looking closely, you are a man. May I ask — do you have a **way** for treading water?"

"No," he said. "**I have no way.** I began in what was native, grew in what was nature, and completed in what was allotted. I go in with the whirlpool's downrush, come out with the surge, following the water's way and doing nothing of my own. That is how I tread it."

Confucius said: "What do you mean — began in the native, grew in the nature, completed in the allotted?"

"Born in the hills, I rest easy in the hills — that is the native. Raised in the water, I rest easy in the water — that is the nature. **I do it without knowing why I do it — that is the allotted.**"

## Framing: the anti-method, in three stages

- **"I have no way."** The chapter's skill-pages have each supplied a method — the [cicada catcher's](/stories/cicada-catcher/) stacked pellets, the [boatman's](/stories/boatman/) forgotten water — and this one begins by *refusing the question*. 亡，吾无道: the swimmer denies having anything teachable, then immediately gives the most precise three-stage account in the chapter. The site reads the refusal and the account together: what cannot be taught is a *history*, not a technique — and the history has a shape.
- **始乎故，长乎性，成乎命 — native, nature, allotted.** Three stages, each one a settlement: born in the hills, at rest in the hills (故, the native); raised in the water, at rest in the water (性, the grown nature); and doing it without knowing why (命, the allotted). The word 命 is the same one the [drumming-basin passage](/translations/drumming-basin-passage/) uses for the given order of transformation — and the [ziran](/concepts/ziran/) concept page keeps the family resemblance: the swimmer's 命 is self-so order lived from inside. Note the progression's direction — it goes *deeper into rest*, not further into skill.
- **与齐俱入，与汩偕出 — joining, never resisting.** The swimmer enters with the downrush and exits with the surge; 从水之道而不为私 — follows the water's way and does nothing of his own. This is the chapter's water-version of the [ferryman's forgetting](/stories/boatman/): the diver *forgets* the water, the swimmer *joins* it — two water-sages, one chapter, and the site's two-pole map of practice (the filled attention and the emptied self) shows up again inside a single river.

## Why it belongs in this knowledge base

Because the swimmer is the site's answer to its own most common question — "what should I be *doing*?" ([how long](/experiences/questions/how-long-should-i-sit/), [which practice](/experiences/questions/cunsi-or-zuowang/)) — and the answer is not a program but a criterion: the practice is working when it becomes the thing you do without knowing why you do it. The [sitting protocol](/tools/sitting-protocol/) is written to be outgrown into exactly this.

**Uncertainty preserved:** an *outer* chapter passage (middle stratum), fully verified in two editions; the swimmer and the dialogue are fable; Confucius appears as the tradition's literary questioner, as throughout the chapter; the three stages are the passage's own vocabulary, not a general theory.
