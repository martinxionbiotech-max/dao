## The story

Hui Shi said to Zhuangzi: "The king of Wei gave me seeds of a giant gourd. I planted them and they bore a fruit weighing five bushels. It could not hold water — it was too heavy to lift full. Split into ladles, it was so big there was no vessel to hold *it*. It is not that the thing was not huge — but I smashed it, because it was useless."

Zhuangzi said: "You are simply clumsy with bigness. There was a man of Song who could make a salve that kept hands from chapping in the water; for generations his family used it to bleach silk for a few coins. A traveler heard of it and bought the formula for a hundred gold. He took it to the king of Wu, and when Yue attacked, the king made him a general; the winter water-battle was won, and he was given a fief. The same salve — one family gets a fief, another goes on bleaching silk: the *use* differs. Now you have a five-bushel gourd — why not lash it into a raft and float on the rivers and lakes, instead of worrying that it fits no vessel? Your heart is stuffed with weeds."

And then Hui Shi tried once more, with a tree: "I have a great tree — men call it the useless ailanthus. Its trunk is gnarled beyond the plumb line, its branches twisted beyond the square; it stands by the road and no carpenter looks at it. Your words are like that: great, and of no use, and everyone walks past."

Zhuangzi answered: "Have you not seen the wildcat? It crouches and waits, leaps east and west — and dies in the trap. The yak is vast as a cloud hanging from the sky, and it cannot catch a mouse. Now you have a great tree and grieve that it is useless — **why not plant it in the village of nowhere, in the wilds of the boundless, and wander doing nothing at its side, and lie down to sleep beneath it**? No axe will cut it short; nothing will harm it. What is there to trouble you?"

## Framing: uselessness as the last lesson of the first chapter

The chapter that opened with the [Peng bird](/stories/peng-bird/) — the biggest instrument flying the biggest journey — closes with the useless tree, and the two endings are one point:

- **The salve is the hinge.** Same formula, two careers: silk-bleaching and a fief. The gourd's uselessness is *use unimagined* — the raft that carries a man across rivers is the exact shape Hui Shi could not see because he asked only "what does it fit?" The fable's verb is 拙于用大 — "clumsy with bigness": the failure is not in the thing but in the *asking*.
- **The tree gets the chapter's title.** The answer to the useless tree is 无何有之乡 (the village of nowhere) and 逍遥乎寝卧其下 (lie down beneath it, free and easy) — the word *xiaoyao*, the book's title-word, appears here, inside the uselessness itself. The [wuwei](/concepts/wuwei/) family's deepest claim: what is useless to the world of traps and measures is exactly what cannot be harmed by that world.
- **The wildcat and the yak.** The chapter's last image-pair: the agile hunter dies in the snare; the vast yak cannot catch a mouse. Usefulness is *situation-bound*, and the tradition's stillness practices — the [zuowang](/practices/zuowang/) dropping, the useless sitting — are the tree planted in the village of nowhere: no function, and therefore no vulnerability.

## Why it belongs in this knowledge base

Because the knowledge base itself is a gourd Hui Shi would smash: an encyclopedia of a practice that no measure requires, kept precisely because "of no use" is not the same as "worthless." The [translation policy](/blog/translation-policy/) and the [reading order](/tools/reading-order/) pages are this fable in editorial form — instruments, not ends, floated on their own rivers.

**Uncertainty preserved:** the *inner* chapter's closing dialogue (earliest stratum); the characters are the book's standing pair, and the scene is literary. The "five bushels" and the fief are fable quantities, not records.
