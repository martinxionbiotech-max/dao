## The story

Tian Kaizhi was received by Duke Wei of Zhou. The duke asked: "I hear Zhu Shen studied life-cultivation. You studied with Zhu Shen — what have you heard?" Tian Kaizhi answered with his teacher's formula: "The one who is good at nurturing life is like a shepherd — he watches for the sheep that lags and whips it (善养生者，若牧羊然，视其后者而鞭之)." Asked what this meant, he told the two deaths:

"In Lu there was Shan Bao: he lived in the cliffs and drank the water, shared no profit with the people, and at seventy still had a baby's complexion. Unfortunately he met a hungry tiger — the hungry tiger killed and ate him. There was Zhang Yi: no grand gate, no screen — there was nowhere he did not go (高门县薄，无不走也) — and at forty he died of an inner-heat illness. Bao nourished his *inner* and the tiger ate his *outer*; Yi nourished his *outer* and the illness attacked his *inner*. Both of these men — they did not whip the sheep that lagged."

Then Confucius' rule, appended to the account: "Do not go in so deep that you hide away, and do not come out so bright that you show — stand like dry wood in the middle (无入而藏，无出而阳，柴立其中央). If the three are achieved, the name is at its height." And the chapter's own close, on the arithmetic of fear: where one in ten dies, fathers and sons and brothers warn each other and only travel armed — but what men should truly fear, the bed and the table (衽席之上，饮食之间) — there, no one takes precautions. That is the error.

## Framing: the balance doctrine

- **The shepherd's rule is the whole.** 视其后者而鞭之 — the teacher's formula is not "push the strong sheep" but "find the lagging one": cultivation as *correction of the lagging side*, not amplification of the leading one. The two deaths are the two lagging-sheep cases: Bao's lag was the outer life (he hid so deep the world's tiger found him defenseless), Yi's lag was the inner (he climbed so fast the body's heat killed him). The site's [progress question](/experiences/questions/what-counts-as-progress/) takes this formula as the book's own definition of progress: what lags is what counts.
- **The two deaths, in parallel.** 豹养其内而虎食其外，毅养其外而病攻其内 — the chapter's diagnosis sentence, balanced like the ledger lines the site has tracked in [Bole](/stories/bole-horses/) and [Robber Zhi](/stories/dao-yi-you-dao/): inner cultivation alone dies to the outer world; outer cultivation alone dies to the inner one. One-sidedness is the killer — not the direction.
- **Confucius' middle, quoted by the book.** 无入而藏，无出而阳，柴立其中央 — the [Confucius-in-the-book device](/translations/xinzhai-passage/) again: the balance rule spoken by the moralist the book elsewhere parodies, taken seriously here. 柴立 (stand like dry wood) is the image: alive but unadorned, present but unperforming — the book's own middle way between hiding and showing. The site's [mountain-tree-goose page](/stories/mountain-tree-goose/) carries the same doctrine from the 山木 chapter (between the useful and the useless); this passage gives it the cultivation version.
- **The bed and the table.** The close is the chapter's darkest arithmetic: men arm themselves for the road where one in ten dies, and take no precautions on the bed and the table — where the book says the real danger is. The line is the site's bridge to the [practices pages](/tools/sitting-protocol/): the tradition's own warning that the danger of practice is not the spectacular (the road) but the ordinary (bed, table — the appetites and the comforts). Registered as the book's claim, not medical advice.
- **The drift, small and instructive.** 内热 survives in traditional medicine as "inner heat" — a real clinical term in TCM. The chapter uses it as *diagnosis-by-metaphor* (the climber's burnout, in the book's body-language); the site keeps the chapter's usage and marks the medical sense as a later layer. 鞭策 (whip-and-goad) also survives — as the modern word for *encouragement*, the shepherd's verb domesticated into office vocabulary.
