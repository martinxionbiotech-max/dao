## The story

Duke Huan of Qi was hunting in the marshes, with Guan Zhong driving his chariot, when he saw a ghost. He grasped Guan Zhong's hand: "Zhongfu, what do you see?" "I see nothing," Guan Zhong answered. The duke returned home, lost his wits (诶诒 — the gloss: "his hun-spirit scattered"), fell ill, and did not come out for days.

A Qi scholar, Huangzi Gaoao, came to him: "Your Grace is wounding himself — how could a ghost wound you? Look: the congested qi of rage, scattered and not returning, makes for depletion; rising and not descending, it makes a man quick to anger; sinking and not rising, it makes him quick to forget; neither rising nor sinking, lodged in the middle against the heart, it makes him ill."

"Then are there ghosts?" asked the duke. "There are," said Huangzi. "The mud has the Li; the hearth has the Ji. The filth inside the door is occupied by the Thunderer; in the northeast the Bei'a and the Gui Long leap; in the northwest the Yi Yang dwells. The water has the Wangxiang; the hills have the Shen; the mountains have the Kui; the wilds have the Panghuang; and the marshes have the Weiyi."

"What is the Weiyi like?" asked the duke. "The Weiyi," said Huangzi, "is as big as a wheel-hub, as long as a carriage-pole, in a purple robe with a vermilion cap. As a creature it hates the sound of thunder and of cartwheels — at the sound it holds its head and stands upright. And whoever sees it is close to becoming hegemon."

Duke Huan smiled broadly: "That is exactly what I saw." He straightened his cap and robes, sat with the scholar — and before the day was out, he did not know when the illness had left him (不终日而不知病之去也).

## Framing: the diagnosis in a ghost catalog

- **The cure is the catalog.** The chapter is an *outer chapter* (declared per the [layering rule](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/)), and the story's engine is Huangzi's double move: first the qi-lecture (the physiology of the stuck heart — rage's qi scattering, rising, sinking, lodging: the illness is the qi's route, not the ghost's act), then — instead of denying the ghost — a *complete* catalog of the world's spirits, ending with a description that matches whatever the duke saw and means *hegemony*. The cure is not skepticism; it is re-naming. The site holds the gloss tradition's own frame (Guo Xiang, in the jijie: worry comes and entangles life — that is not understanding; the trouble leaves and the nature recovers — that is the pattern).
- **"The duke is wounding himself."** 公则自伤，鬼恶能伤公 — the first sentence is the chapter's thesis, and the story is its demonstration: the self-harm was real (the scattered qi, the days indoors), and it healed the moment the vision got a good name. The site's [seeing-light page](/problems/seeing-light-in-sitting/) holds the same line for practice: the thing seen in stillness is registered, not chased; and the [false-dhyana archive page](/experiences/notes/exp-020-false-first-dhyana/) records the modern teacher's version of Huangzi's move — "whatever you see, ignore it" — the two cures agreeing across twenty-three centuries: the content of the vision matters less than what you call it.
- **The ghost catalog, kept intact.** The chapter's list (the Li, the Ji, the Thunderer, the Wangxiang, the Shen, the Kui, the Panghuang, the Weiyi) is the book's own evidence that its world is *crowded* — the same point the [creator question](/experiences/questions/does-daoism-believe-in-a-creator/) holds: no single maker, many presences, and the marsh spirit among them. The site registers the catalog as the text's taxonomy, not the site's: the gloss tradition (Lu Deming, Sima Biao) is quoted for the names' shapes, per the [source discipline](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/).
- **The body's own answer.** The cure's mechanism is the story's quietest line: the duke straightens his robes and sits *with the scholar*, and the illness leaves unnoticed — 不知病之去也, he never learns when it went. The book's [death-and-illness pages](/stories/qinshi-mourning/) hold the same signature: the untying is not an event one catches; it is noticed only after. For the [bed question](/experiences/questions/can-i-sit-in-bed/)'s family of posture pages, the story adds the reminder the tradition kept: the duke healed sitting up, dressed, in company — not lying in the dark.
- **The idiom, lightly drifted.** 齐桓公见鬼 survives in the language chiefly as a ghost-story reference; the chapter's real gift to modern Chinese is elsewhere (the [wooden rooster](/stories/wooden-rooster/) from the same chapter). The site notes the drift per its [idiom discipline](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/): what survived is the spook; what the chapter meant survives only in the full reading — the ghost is the qi's own route, mislabeled.
