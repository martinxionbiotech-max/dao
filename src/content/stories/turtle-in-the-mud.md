## The story

Zhuangzi was fishing on the Pu River. The King of Chu sent two ministers ahead to him, saying: "We would burden you with all within our borders."

Zhuangzi, without turning from his rod, said: "I have heard that Chu has a sacred turtle, dead now three thousand years, which the king keeps wrapped in a cloth, in a box, in his ancestral temple. Would that turtle rather be dead with its bones kept precious — or alive, dragging its tail in the mud?"

The two ministers said: "Alive, dragging its tail in the mud."

Zhuangzi said: "Go home, then. I will drag my tail in the mud."

## Framing: the refusal in one comparison

- **The turtle is the argument.** No doctrine, no debate: the question puts the choice in the ministers' own mouths (宁生而曳尾涂中 — "better alive, tail in the mud"), and their answer *is* Zhuangzi's. The chapter's commentary chain, preserved in the Yihai Zuanwei, records what each generation saw in it: Guo Xiang — 性各有所安也, "each nature has its own place of rest"; the 鬳斋 reading — "the dead-kept-bones, live-dragged-tail comparison is truly extraordinary" — and the editor's own addition, which the site keeps as the passage's best footnote: **"of old, Tao the Recluse painted two oxen to answer the imperial summons — one cramped under whip and rope, one at ease among water and grass; this is the same idea."** The [Tao Hongjing](/people/tao-hongjing/) page's two-oxen painting is this passage's descendant: the turtle and the oxen are one gesture, a millennium apart.
- **The episode and the biography agree, and the site keeps both registers.** The [Zhuang Zhou page](/people/zhuang-zhou/) records the *Shiji's* version — King Wei of Chu, hearing of Zhuang Zhou's worth, sent envoys with rich gifts to make him minister — and this passage is the book's own telling of the same refusal. The site's rule: the offer is historical (the Shiji documents it), the turtle is literature, and the refusal itself is the one thing both registers report.
- **The rod is the point of the posture.** 持竿不顾 — he keeps the rod and does not turn. The refusal is not an argument against office but an occupation already chosen; the chapter's own gloss elsewhere is the [phoenix's three refusals](/stories/phoenix-and-owl/), and the pair together is the chapter's answer to every question about what the tradition means by not wanting the world: it does not hate the world; it has a rod.

## Why it belongs in this knowledge base

Because the site's own [reading-order](/tools/reading-order/) and [protocol](/tools/sitting-protocol/) pages are built on the turtle's logic: the practice is chosen for being *alive*, and every honor the tradition invented for the practice — from the [Shangqing bureaucracy](/timeline/shangqing-revelations/) to the [Zhengao's ranks](/people/tao-hongjing/) — is the cloth and the box. The turtle's question is the site's standing test for its own content.

**Uncertainty preserved:** an *outer* chapter passage (middle stratum), fully verified; the ministers' dialogue is literary; the Chu king's identity follows the Shiji's account (King Wei) while the passage itself says only "the King of Chu"; the turtle's three thousand years are fable arithmetic.
