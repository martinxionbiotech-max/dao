## The story

Autumn floods. All the hundred rivers pour into the Yellow River, so wide that one cannot tell a horse from a cow across its banks. The River Lord is delighted: "All the beauty under heaven is in me." He travels east with the current to the Northern Sea; he faces east and **sees no edge of the water**. And only then does he turn his face around — and look up, dazed, to sigh at Ruo of the sea:

"'A country saying has it: *he who has heard the Way a hundred times thinks no one equals him* — that is me. I have heard of those who belittle Confucius's learning and make light of Boyi's righteousness, and I never believed it. Now that I have seen how inexhaustible you are — if I had not come to your gate, I would have been in danger, and I would long have been laughed at by **the house of the great standard**.'"

And Ruo of the Northern Sea teaches him the chapter's anatomy of limits — the [well frog, the summer insect, the village scholar](/stories/jingdi-zhiwa/) — and then the arithmetic of scale: all the waters under heaven are nothing next to the sea, which the ten thousand rivers feed without filling and the Weilu drain without emptying; yet even the sea, set against heaven and earth, is a small stone or a small tree in a great mountain — and the four seas are a mound of pebbles in a great marsh, and the Middle Kingdom is a grain of millet in a great granary, and a single human among the ten thousand things is a hair-tip on a horse's body.

## Framing: the sigh is the beginning, not the lament

The idiom's modern drift is registered and set aside. 望洋兴叹 today means sighing helplessly at what one cannot reach — frustration, resignation. In the source, the sigh is the **most fortunate moment of the chapter**: the river god's flood had made him *unable to see himself*; the sea's endlessness is what returns him his own edges. The sigh at the sea is the sigh of a diagnosis arriving — and the chapter's word for where he has arrived is 大方之家, "the house of the great standard": the community of those who hold a measure that the flood cannot fool. Two source-text details matter:

- **望洋 is not "gazing at the ocean."** The two characters are a binomial describing the face's motion — looking up, dazed, at a loss. The idiom's later folk-reading as "looking at the ocean (洋)" is a happy accident of sound; the passage itself is about *seeing nothing* — 不见水端, no edge — and the daze that follows.
- **The teaching is measurement, not humiliation.** Ruo's answer does not mock; it *instructs in ratios*: sea to heaven-and-earth, four seas to the marsh, the Middle Kingdom to the granary, one person to the horse's hair. The [well-frog maxim](/stories/jingdi-zhiwa/) and this arithmetic are one lesson: every self-estimate has a frame, and the frames nest. The [commentary tradition itself](/stories/peng-bird/) reads the passage as of a kind with the peng and the quail — the scale fable of ch. 1, replayed with water instead of air.

## Why it belongs in this knowledge base

Because the chapter's later doctrine — 以道观之，物无贵贱, "seen from the Way, nothing is noble or base" — is the same move the site makes on every page: the [guan glossary](/glossary/guan/) records the verb, and the [source-verification tool](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/) is the site's own 大方之家 discipline. The river god is the site's model reader: the one who reached the gate in time.

**Uncertainty preserved:** an *outer* chapter passage (middle stratum), fully verified in both editions; the River Lord and Ruo are fable figures; the idiom's modern sense is flagged as a drift from the source's, per the recorded dictionary tradition.
