## The story

Four men — Zisi, Ziyu, Zili, and Zilai — made a pact: "Whoever can take nothing as the head, life as the spine, death as the rump — whoever knows death and life, survival and ruin, as one body — we will be his friends." They looked at each other, smiled, and were friends without words. Then Ziyu fell ill — his back bent double, his five viscera above, his chin buried in his navel, his shoulders above his head, his spine pointing at the sky — and still, his heart idle and unbothered, he crawled to the well, looked in, and said: "Ah! The maker of things has made me this cramped thing." Asked if he resented it, he answered with the chapter's arithmetic of transformation: if my left arm becomes a rooster, I'll use it to crow the dawn; if my right arm becomes a crossbow-pellet, I'll use it to hunt an owl; if my rump becomes wheels and my spirit a horse, I'll ride them — 安时而处顺，哀乐不能入也 (rest in the season, follow along, and grief and joy cannot enter): this is what the ancients called the untying of the suspended.

Then Zilai fell ill, gasping toward death, his wife and children weeping around him. Zili told them off — "Get back! Don't disturb the transformation!" — leaned in the doorway and asked: "How great is the maker of things — what will he make you into? Where will he take you? A rat's liver? An insect's arm?" Zilai answered with the smelter:

"Parents, to a child: east, west, south, north — I obey. Yin and yang, to a person, are more than parents. If they bring me near death and I refuse, I am the obstinate one — what fault is theirs? The Great Clod loads me with form, wearies me with life, rests me with age, quiets me with death — so what makes my life good makes my death good. Now the great smelter casts metal, and if the metal jumped up crying, 'I must become a Moye sword!' (今大冶铸金，金踊跃曰：'我且必为镆铘！'), the smelter would surely call it inauspicious metal. And to have once chanced into human form and then cry 'human, human!' — the maker of transformations would call that an inauspicious person. Now if I take heaven and earth as the great furnace (以天地为大炉) and the maker of transformations as the great smelter (以造化为大冶) — where could I go that would not be all right?" He slept calmly, woke easily.

## Framing: the metal's complaint

- **The pact is the chapter.** 死生存亡之一体 — the four friends' condition for friendship is the chapter's whole thesis, and the chapter is an *inner chapter* (declared per the [layering rule](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/)) — the book's most systematic treatment of death, in the [mourning family](/stories/qinshi-mourning/) this site has been building (drumming basin, the skeleton, the butterfly). Ziyu's bent body is the chapter's first exhibit: the maker's work, met with the well-mirror's calm — the [same untying](/glossary/xian-jie/) the chapter names 县解, the suspended released.
- **The smelter's judgment is the book's own method.** 金踊跃曰我且必为镆铘 — the metal that *demands its shape* — is the chapter's diagnosis of the human cry "human, human!" (人耳人耳): the form is a chance casting, and claiming it as one's own is the metal's leap. The image inverts the usual hierarchy — here the *maker* is the authority, and the material's preference is the fault. The site holds the passage against the book's own [craft passages](/stories/cook-ding/): Ding and the artisans cooperate with the grain; the leaping metal *argues with* it. The difference is the whole distance between 顺 (going along) and 悍 (obstinacy — the word the passage uses for the refusal).
- **The furnace sentence, and what it licenses.** 以天地为大炉，以造化为大冶 — then *any* casting is acceptable (恶乎往而不可哉 — "where could I go that would not do?"): the passage is the book's farthest statement of trust in the process, and the site keeps its license explicit — this is a *statement about dying*, made by a dying man to his friend, not a claim about medicine. The [illness question](/experiences/questions/can-meditation-cure-illness/) holds the modern boundary: the passage transforms the *attitude* toward the casting; it does not treat the body.
- **The drift, none needed.** 大冶铸金 survives in classical Chinese as the idiom for the maker's arbitrary power over the made; it did not enter the modern vocabulary the way [the drum-basin](/stories/qinshi-mourning/) did. The site keeps the passage's own vocabulary — 大块 (the Great Clod), 造化 (the maker of transformations) — as the book's cosmic names, untranslated-with-gloss, per the site's [term discipline](/tools/how-to-verify-sources/).
