## The story

The spring dries up. The fish lie together on the dry ground, keeping each other damp with their breath and wet with their spit — and the text says it in a breath:

"Rather than moistening each other with breath and spit, it would be better to **forget each other in the rivers and lakes**. And rather than praising Yao and condemning Jie, it would be better to forget both and merge into the Way."

## Framing: the famous line is the setup, not the lesson

This is the site's clearest case of a passage whose *idiomatic afterlife* inverts its function. The popular idiom 相濡以沫 — "helping each other with spit" — names heroic mutual devotion in hardship, and it is used today as a *praise* of sticking together. The passage itself uses the image as a **counterexample**:

- **The spit is not the good.** The fish moistening each other are not the passage's ideal — they are its image of the *fallback*, the desperate minimum when the water is gone. The devotion is real and the text does not mock it; it simply refuses to call it good, the way one refuses to call a tourniquet a healthy limb.
- **The water is the good — and it is invisible.** The actual target is 相忘于江湖: in the rivers and lakes, the fish *forget each other* — not coldly, but the way you forget your breathing when it works. The tradition's claim, stated in the [ziran](/concepts/ziran/) page's terms: the best condition is the one that needs no heroism because nothing is lacking.
- **The second parallel is the real lesson.** 与其誉尧而非桀也 — "rather than praising Yao and condemning Jie" — the passage doubles itself: moral praise and blame (Yao the sage, Jie the tyrant) are the dried-up spring of the mind, and 两忘而化其道 — forgetting *both* and merging into the Way — is the rivers and lakes. The passage is not about fish. It is about the mind's habit of surviving on judgment when it could be swimming.

## Why it belongs in this knowledge base

Because the site's sitting tradition lives exactly here: the [drowsiness pattern](/patterns/drowsiness-vs-stillness/), the [stillness question](/questions/what-is-stillness/), and the whole [zuowang](/practices/zuowang/) lineage are attempts to describe the *water*, not to polish the spit. And because the passage is the site's standing correction to its own readers: the practices documented here are instruments for getting back into the water — not a new and better way to moisten each other on dry ground.

**Uncertainty preserved:** an *inner* chapter passage (earliest stratum), fully verified; the fish image is fable, and the idiom's later life as a praise of devotion is a documented semantic drift the page flags rather than adopting.
